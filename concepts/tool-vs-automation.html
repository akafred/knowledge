Wielded Tool vs Automation

The distinction between a tool, which you wield with continuous control, feedback and intention and which builds your expertise, and automation, which acts on its own after a request. It argues that treating LLMs as "just tools" hides that using them means abdicating the work and the learning. Distinct from ai-employee-framing (tool versus colleague) because it classifies LLMs as neither.

Also known as: tool vs automation, wielded tool, LLMs are automation, abdicate the work, abdication

Documents (4)

  • 'AI' is Not a Tool Rich Hickey · 2026-10-22 source ↗ Reader 🖥️
    LLMs are not tools, because a tool is something you wield with continuous control, feedback and intention, whereas an LLM is automation that acts on its own after you make a request, so using one means abdicating the work and the learning that wielding a tool would give you.
  • TBM 428: Yes, Robot. Yes, Boss? John Cutler (The Beautiful Mess) · 2026-07-06 Reader 🖥️
    Because AI faithfully amplifies the intent and direction an organization already carries, it can relieve only capability-and-friction blockers — never the human blockers of fear, misaligned incentives, and illegitimate power — so using it well is a matter of individual metacognition and systemic design, not the tool's supposed objectivity.
  • What Survived Contact Sam Ruby · 2026-07-01 source ↗ Reader 🖥️
    When agents write the code, rigor does not vanish but migrates to the objective and its evaluation; directing an agent is therefore a management act (a hire, not a tool-use) whose one non-delegable duty is owning the objective and the oracle that checks it.
  • Presentation: The Ironies of A^2 I^2 (Ironies of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Incidents) J. Paul Reed · 2026-05-22 Reader 🖥️
    The ironies Bainbridge identified in 1983 — that automation makes the human operator more, not less, crucial while simultaneously eroding the skills needed to intervene — persist and intensify under AI, so engineering organizations must treat AI as an efficiency lever that demands deliberate cultivation of human thoroughness, mental models, and coordination practices rather than as a substitute for them.

Tensions

How far can day-2 operations be handed to self-healing / autonomous remediation?
from: What did the participants of the Thoughtworks 'Future of Software Engineering' Europe retreat (mid-2026) conclude about how agentic AI is reshaping software engineering?
  • Looking back at the Future of Software Engineering unconference (Bartosz Ocytko): A self-healing platform foundation should make whole classes of failure 'disappear', with increasingly autonomous background 'guardrail' agents refactoring, adding tests, and protecting invariants.
  • AI Is Changing Software Engineering. The Fundamentals Still Matter. (Abby Bangser): Detection, context, and suggestion are safe wins, but automated remediation must remain an intentional, context-specific decision, not a default end state.
  • What Survived Contact (Sam Ruby): Self-healing has, in practice, only moved halfway: diagnosis is automated while remediation is deliberately withheld from the machine.
Assessment: Ruby's report is descriptive (where practice actually stands) and Ocytko's is aspirational (where platforms could go); the gap between 'remediation is withheld' and 'failure modes disappear' is precisely the distance the optimists are projecting across. Bangser supplies the reason the gap may not close on efficiency grounds alone — the withheld human step is also the learning step.
Is the value question genuinely settled by evidence, or does uncertainty and sampling bias still dominate?
from: What did the participants of the Thoughtworks 'Future of Software Engineering' Europe retreat (mid-2026) conclude about how agentic AI is reshaping software engineering?
  • Notes from FOSE Europe (Giles Edwards-Alexander): The evidence is in; the value is clearly here and here to stay.
  • Finding Comfort in the Uncertainty (Annie Vella): Nobody has it figured out; there is more uncertainty than certainty, and the shared set of questions is the real outcome.
  • What Survived Contact (Sam Ruby): 'Rigor migrates' is about as settled as this young field gets, but the room is a non-random, incumbent-tilted sample, and the doubt is currently better evidenced than the hope.
Assessment: The single sharpest divergence, and it sits directly under the headline whether-to-how convergence: they narrate the same shift but disagree on what it establishes. Edwards-Alexander and Herlein read the room's confidence as evidence; Vella and Ruby read the same room as a self-selected in-group. Ruby has the better of it methodologically — he is the only one to interrogate the sample and to notice the evidence is asymmetric (upside anecdotal, downside studied), which is uncomfortable precisely because the cohort's confidence is built on the weaker side.
Where is the locus of safety for non-engineers / citizen developers building software?
from: What did the participants of the Thoughtworks 'Future of Software Engineering' Europe retreat (mid-2026) conclude about how agentic AI is reshaping software engineering?
  • Looking back at the Future of Software Engineering unconference (Bartosz Ocytko): In a tightly-constrained self-healing platform — not universal upskilling.
  • What Survived Contact (Sam Ruby): In the director's management skill — a non-engineer steering an agent has made a hire, and the requirement is that they can manage by objective.
  • AI Is Changing Software Engineering. The Fundamentals Still Matter. (Abby Bangser): In API-first, MCP-accessible tooling that supports rather than restricts the new builders.
  • Why did Martin Fowler invite 60 engineers to a ski resort? (Ivett Ördög): It is genuinely unsolved — the tension between democratisation and its risks is an open question.
Assessment: Four different answers to the same governance question: the platform (Ocytko), the director's discipline (Ruby), the tooling interface (Bangser), or nobody-knows-yet (Ördög). Ruby and Ocytko are the true opposites — Ruby puts the burden on a human management skill, Ocytko designs the human out by making failure modes disappear in the platform. That both are held by attendees of one retreat is a measure of how unsettled the citizen-developer problem is.
Is code a durable artifact to be maintained, or an ephemeral byproduct regenerated from a more durable source (spec/objective)?
from: What is the future of software development in the agentic/AI age?
  • We've Stopped Arguing About Whether AI Changes Software (Greg Herlein): Code is (or is becoming) ephemeral — a disposable byproduct the agent regenerates from the spec; the spec is 'the point.'
  • Fragments: February 4 (Martin Fowler (Feb 4)): When coding is free, the specification-plus-tests becomes the durable, primary engineering artifact.
  • Software Design in the Agentic Age: Place Your Bets (Mathias Verraes): Specifications could replace code as the source of truth, but throwaway regeneration is a bet he is unwilling to make; keep code durable and revertible.
  • Why did Martin Fowler invite 60 engineers to a ski resort? (Ivett Ördög): Code endures because the winning agents behave like engineers moulding a maintained codebase, not compilers regenerating from scratch.
  • What Survived Contact (Sam Ruby): The durability question is a category error — what endures is neither the code nor the prose spec but the objective and its oracle.
Assessment: The most-cited open question of the corpus, and genuinely unresolved (Herlein: 'no consensus here. None.'). The 'code is the better spec' counter is strong — code is unambiguous, executable, deterministic, whereas a prose spec is reinterpreted each run, which sits in tension with verifiability being the quality bar. Ruby's move is the most defensible: it dissolves the code-vs-spec fight by locating durability in the evaluation rather than any representation. Verraes and Ördög expose the load-bearing empirical unknown — nobody has data on regenerate-vs-patch cost or on long-term evolvability of agent-maintained code.
Where does the non-delegable human asset now live — the harness/platform, the oracle, the fundamentals, or the developer's understanding?
from: What is the future of software development in the agentic/AI age?
  • Notes from FOSE Europe (Giles Edwards-Alexander): In the harness — the continuously-evolving scaffolding of architecture guidance and rigour around the agents — though it must stay team-distributed, not centralized.
  • What Survived Contact (Sam Ruby): In the oracle — the objective's evaluation is the single thing the person directing the model cannot delegate back to it.
  • Jimmy Nilsson's Post — reflections after the Thoughtworks 'Future of Software Development' retreat (Jimmy Nilsson): In the fundamentals plus a renewed 'Extreme Humanism' — the durable disciplines and the conspicuously human element.
  • AI Is Changing Software Engineering. The Fundamentals Still Matter. (Abby Bangser): In right-sized boundaries and the operational loop — humans must stay in operations because their learning is the irreplaceable asset.
  • Fragments: February 9 (Martin Fowler (Feb 9)): In the developer's model-building and shared understanding — the craft of devising abstractions to reason about the domain.
Assessment: This is the deepest structural disagreement — every author agrees a human residue remains but nominates a different residue, and the choice determines what to invest in and what to teach. The harness-centric (Edwards-Alexander), oracle-centric (Ruby), fundamentals-centric (Nilsson/Bangser), and understanding-centric (Fowler) camps are not merely emphases: they imply different career ladders, different hiring, and different failure modes. Ruby's oracle and Bangser's operational-learning are the most operationally concrete; Nilsson's is the least argued. The absence in the corpus of anyone who holds 'nothing is non-delegable' is itself telling (see unexamined assumptions).
How far should software be automated — toward the fully-autonomous dark/software factory, or held back to bounded autonomy with humans in the loop?
from: What is the future of software development in the agentic/AI age?
  • Why did Martin Fowler invite 60 engineers to a ski resort? (Ivett Ördög): Full automation is genuinely viable in context and she is 'ironically' being pulled toward the dark-factory camp.
  • Looking back at the Future of Software Engineering unconference (Bartosz Ocytko): The aspirational endpoint is a largely self-evolving software factory; humans build and operate the backbone rather than the application.
  • AI Is Changing Software Engineering. The Fundamentals Still Matter. (Abby Bangser): Bounded autonomy — keep humans in the operational loop; automated remediation must stay an intentional decision.
  • What Survived Contact (Sam Ruby): Self-healing has stalled halfway on purpose: diagnosis is automated, remediation withheld from the machine.
  • Software Design in the Agentic Age: Place Your Bets (Mathias Verraes): Dark factories are safe only for orgs that already have high test coverage, skilled engineers, and a quality culture; most should cover fundamentals first and stay revertible.
Assessment: The spectrum runs from Ördög/Ocytko (full automation is arriving and desirable) to Bangser/Ruby/Verraes (deliberately withhold the last step). The decelerationists have the better of the safety argument today — Ruby's 'nobody will cede the blast radius' and Bangser's 'removing humans removes learning' identify concrete, unmet prerequisites — but Ördög's context-dependence (backends, COBOL) shows the boundary is domain-specific, not absolute. The real disagreement is less about capability than about who absorbs the blast radius when an autonomous fix goes wrong.
Does the current evidence favor optimism or doubt about agentic engineering's payoff?
from: What is the future of software development in the agentic/AI age?
  • Jimmy Nilsson's Post — reflections after the Thoughtworks 'Future of Software Development' retreat (Jimmy Nilsson): Optimistic: the mature, nuanced, human-centred discourse among leading practitioners is a good predictor of a healthy trajectory.
  • The Future Of Software Engineering (Thoughtworks): Pessimist-realist: there is an active regression underway that most optimism ignores.
  • What Survived Contact (Sam Ruby): Doubt is currently better-evidenced than hope, and that asymmetry is the uncomfortable thing to watch.
Assessment: The sharpest divergence in the corpus, because it is about the epistemic ground of every other claim. Nilsson's optimism is explicitly consensus-of-the-in-group and self-flagged as biased; Ruby and the Thoughtworks report both bring the only genuinely external evidence (DORA reversal, HBR intensification, outside surveys) — and it points the other way. On the current record, the skeptics have the stronger footing precisely because the optimistic case is methodologically weakest (a 'sample of one,' as Ruby says of his own best data). The whole cohort's cautious optimism may be an artifact of who was in the room.

Open gaps (11)

  • incompleteThe analogy between Bainbridge's control-room operators and software engineers is taken as given but never defended. Bainbridge studied operators of physical, bounded, regulated, slow-dynamics systems (nuclear plants, chemical processes, aviation cockpits) where (a) the human's role is fundamentally MONITORING-AND-OVERRIDE, (b) the system's correct behavior is specified ex ante, (c) regulators audit the human-machine boundary, and (d) the operator is licensed and trained on simulators. Software engineers are CONSTRUCTORS — they design the system itself, not just operate it; the system's correct behavior is partially undefined; no licensing or regulator polices the boundary; and the 'incident' role is only one slice of the work. Reed's mapping works best for the incident-response slice (which is what his job is) and is weakest for routine development. He never disambiguates these. The argument that 'AI ironies for engineers = Bainbridge ironies for operators' may overgeneralize from the incident-response edge case to the whole of engineering practice.
    Presentation: The Ironies of A^2 I^2 (Ironies of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Incidents) — read next: Read the original Bainbridge 1983 paper directly. Then Hollnagel & Woods on resilience engineering, and David Mindell's 'Our Robots, Ourselves' (history of human-machine collaboration). Pair with Naur's 'Programming as Theory Building' for the contrasting view that software engineering is theory construction, not operation.
  • incompleteReed's prescription assumes organizations will reinvest AI-driven efficiency gains in human thoroughness, but the same talk reports that the current pattern is the opposite: 'AI's main impact on my life is more incidents because we're generating more code.' The systems-thinking question of how to design feedback loops that prevent capture of efficiency-as-throughput is left untouched. Without that loop, the 'AI does efficiency, humans do more thoroughness' division is a moral exhortation, not a mechanism.
    Presentation: The Ironies of A^2 I^2 (Ironies of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Incidents) — read next: Donella Meadows on leverage points (especially feedback loops, rules of the system, and goals of the system). John Cutler on institutional overload and on how organizations metabolize efficiency gains. Possibly Christensen on capabilities-as-process — i.e., the org's existing process consumes new capability.
  • incompleteThe empirical evidence is thin and uneven. The nurses study (Woods et al., July 2025) is presented as decisive but is a single study, in a different domain (clinical prediction), with a likely small N; the developer study (METR-style) is cited from memory. Reed does not engage the contrary evidence (e.g., positive AI productivity findings from DORA, GitHub Copilot studies) or distinguish task types where AI does and does not pay off. The asymmetric-risk frame is compelling and probably right but is underdetermined by the cited data.
    Presentation: The Ironies of A^2 I^2 (Ironies of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Incidents) — read next: DORA 2024/2025 AI findings, the Princeton/MIT studies on developer AI productivity, METR's actual published methodology, Woods's published paper (rather than the slide). Also Cal Newport / Bainbridge-skeptics on whether the operator analogy fits knowledge work.
  • incompleteThe animacy paradox is introduced as a phenomenological observation but is not connected to design recommendations. If interpretation flips with situational stakes, what design pattern reliably keeps the deterministic-machine interpretation alive in high-tempo contexts? Reed gestures at attribution and explainability but doesn't develop this.
    Presentation: The Ironies of A^2 I^2 (Ironies of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Incidents) — read next: Lucy Suchman 'Plans and Situated Actions'; Dave Woods's papers on 'envisioned worlds' and on coordination breakdowns; possibly the air-traffic-control HCI literature on 'mode confusion'.
  • incompleteReed touches the systems-thinking question (juniors who never built the mental model, attrition of senior engineers carrying tacit knowledge) but doesn't push it to its leverage-point: the org's HIRING and TRAINING flows may be where the irony actually lives, not in the engineer's individual relationship to AI. The dynamic he describes is a stock-and-flow problem (the stock of tacit knowledge is depleting faster than it is being replenished) and warrants stock-and-flow analysis.
    Presentation: The Ironies of A^2 I^2 (Ironies of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Incidents) — read next: Meadows again on stocks-and-flows; the National Research Council's literature on knowledge attrition in nuclear and aerospace; and possibly the apprenticeship literature (Lave and Wenger, situated learning).
  • illogicalReed conflates 'AI is non-deterministic' with 'AI lacks interpredictability.' These are related but distinct. Humans are non-deterministic and have high interpredictability with their teammates. The reason AI lacks interpredictability is not non-determinism per se but lack of a SHARED MODEL of context, goals, and norms. This matters because it suggests the fix is not 'make AI more deterministic' but 'invest in shared context and norm-conformance' — a different research and product direction.
    Presentation: The Ironies of A^2 I^2 (Ironies of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Incidents) — read next: Herbert Clark on common ground in conversation; the multi-agent systems literature on shared mental models; recent work on AI alignment through context-engineering.
  • incompleteThe essay is almost entirely diagnostic on the systemic register: it insists AI cannot fix incentives, legitimacy or voice safety and that these must be addressed by humans/systems, but offers no method for doing so. The 'individual + systemic' synthesis names both levels without specifying how to act on the systemic one.
    TBM 428: Yes, Robot. Yes, Boss? — read next: Cutler's own 'AI Won't Save Your Kingdom' and legibility/legitimacy pieces; systems-change literature on incentive redesign; Amy Edmondson on building psychological safety as a deliberate practice.
  • incompleteThe capability/friction vs. motivational/political blocker taxonomy is presented as a clean binary, but many real blockers are mixed (e.g., 'missing scaffolding' can itself be a symptom of low legitimacy or fear). The essay doesn't address how to tell which kind of blocker you actually face — precisely the diagnosis on which its advice depends.
    TBM 428: Yes, Robot. Yes, Boss? — read next: Diagnostic frameworks that separate technical from adaptive problems (Heifetz, adaptive leadership); root-cause methods that trace surface friction to underlying incentives.
  • incompleteThe accountability question is raised and dropped: if AI makes bad-faith performance management cleaner and more defensible, the essay's own 'would they be successful?' implies existing legal/organizational recourse is inadequate, but it neither examines that recourse nor proposes countermeasures (audit trails, prompt provenance, HR governance).
    TBM 428: Yes, Robot. Yes, Boss? — read next: Agent-governance and decision-provenance literature; employment-law treatments of pretextual performance management; work on organizational undiscussables.
  • incompleteThe asymmetry claim — that AI amplifies dysfunction more readily than honesty — is argued qualitatively (bad-faith use is the low-effort default) but never sized. Whether the reflective-use gap is closable by design (defaults, prompts, scaffolds that nudge toward self-interrogation) is left unexplored.
    TBM 428: Yes, Robot. Yes, Boss? — read next: Choice-architecture / nudge work applied to AI defaults; 'scaffolded cognitive friction' as an antidote (cognitive-surrender literature).
  • incompleteThe whole cross-retreat comparison rests on an acknowledged seam: the author read only the public summary of the earlier retreat, so he cannot separate 'the conversation genuinely grew' from 'the earlier summary simply compressed this out.' The central 'what changed' claim is offered on face value with that limitation showing.
