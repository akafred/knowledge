Algorithmic Curation & Taste Narrowing

Recommendation systems tuned to maximize short-term engagement systematically under-explore — they converge on a user's existing favorites, overexpose them into boredom, and permanently suppress unfamiliar content before the user has had enough exposure to learn to like it, narrowing over years the range of genres and styles a person is ever exposed to. Countering the dynamic requires deliberate exploration layered on top of prediction accuracy, not more accurate prediction alone.

Also known as: recommendation algorithm, algorithmic curation, engagement-optimized recommendation, engagement-based curation, taste narrowing, consumption capital, straddling (recommender systems), exploration rate (recommenders), content discovery algorithm

Open gaps (4)

  • incompleteThe article never states the model's functional forms, parameter values, or sensitivity analysis (how the appreciation curve is shaped, what noise levels were tested, how many Monte Carlo trials produced the reported effect sizes). Without these, a reader can't judge how robust the 'moderate noise helps' conclusion is to the specific modeling choices — a purely theoretical model's conclusions can be highly sensitive to functional-form assumptions.
    Recommendation algorithms might be making your entertainment boring, new research suggests — read next: Read the underlying paper directly — Samsun Knight, "Engagement-based curation and the evolution of taste," Journal of Cultural Economics (2026), https://doi.org/10.1007/s10824-026-09591-3 — for the actual model specification and robustness checks.
  • incompleteThe article treats platforms as having essentially one dial (prediction accuracy vs. noise), but real recommendation systems already blend multiple discovery mechanisms — curated new-release rows, human-programmed playlists, explicit 'diversity' injections, cold-start exploration bonuses. It never asks whether real-world systems already implement something functionally like the paper's proposed fix, which would make the normative recommendation partially moot rather than novel.
    Recommendation algorithms might be making your entertainment boring, new research suggests — read next: Platform-side accounts of recommender-system design (e.g., published descriptions of Spotify's Discover Weekly / exploration mechanisms, or general recommender-systems literature on the exploration-exploitation trade-off) to check how much of this gap is already closed operationally.
  • uninformedThe study is a purely theoretical, simulated model with no human-subject or real-usage data — the article flags this directly, but doesn't examine whether the inverted-U 'consumption capital' curve (moderate exposure raises liking, excess exposure causes satiation) is itself empirically well-established for genre-level entertainment tastes specifically, as opposed to being carried over from general cultural-economics theory (Stigler & Becker) or adjacent phenomena like the mere-exposure effect and hedonic adaptation.
    Recommendation algorithms might be making your entertainment boring, new research suggests — read next: Empirical psychology/economics literature on hedonic adaptation and the mere-exposure effect (Zajonc) to check whether an inverted-U specifically holds at the genre/style level for music, film, and fiction — not just for individual songs or images in lab settings.
  • illogicalThe paper's proposed fix is framed as making the algorithm 'less accurate' or 'noisier,' but the actual causal lever the article describes is forced exploration regardless of predicted engagement — a distinct design choice from genuinely degrading prediction quality. Conflating 'noisy predictions' with 'committed exploration' muddies the practical implication: a platform could keep maximally accurate predictions everywhere and still reserve a fixed fraction of recommendation slots for exploration, capturing the same long-term benefit without the framing cost of 'deliberately recommend worse content.' This is exactly the systems-thinking move the article skips: it treats accuracy and exploration as one dial when the model's own results imply they're two.
    Recommendation algorithms might be making your entertainment boring, new research suggests — read next: Multi-armed bandit / exploration-exploitation literature (e.g., Sutton & Barto, *Reinforcement Learning*) which cleanly separates prediction accuracy from exploration policy — useful for reframing the paper's own proposed fix as a policy-layer change rather than an accuracy trade-off.
