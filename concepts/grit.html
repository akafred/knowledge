Grit (Passion & Perseverance)

Duckworth's trait construct — passion and perseverance sustained toward long-term goals over months to decades (consistency, not intensity) — necessary but not sufficient for excellence.

Also known as: grit, grit scale, passion and perseverance for long-term goals

Open gaps (3)

  • illogicalThe Phelps anecdote is offered as evidence that environment (not just personal trait) shapes outcomes, but Phelps's own choices — following coach Bob Bowman when he relocated, 'navigating to be around the best teammates' — are themselves described as gritty acts of agency. This blurs the very trait/situation distinction the argument is meant to establish: any environmental advantage can be re-described as a product of the individual's own grit in seeking it out, risking an unfalsifiable model.
    Leadership Summit 2026: Inspiring Grit and Growth Amid Unprecedented Change — read next: Look for research that operationalizes situational agency independently of grit — e.g., through random or exogenous assignment to environments (coach changes, team reassignments) rather than a single retrospective case study, which cannot disentangle the two.
  • incompleteAdler's four gap forms (uninformed/misinformed/illogical/incomplete) presuppose an argument to evaluate, but this excerpt supplies none — it is a toolkit table, a set of reflection prompts, a glossary, and acknowledgments. The evidence, worked examples, and inferential support for claims like 'quality of experience beats efficiency' or 'intentional imperfection produces better long-term outcomes' live in the book's main chapters, which were not captured in this Reader save. Nothing here can be assessed as backed by real mechanism or research versus inspirational framing, because no mechanism or research is presented at all — only vocabulary and prompts.
    Tiny Experiments — read next: Read the book's actual chapters (not this back-matter excerpt) to evaluate whether the experimental-mindset thesis is supported by cited research or worked case studies, or is asserted by authority and narrative alone.
  • uninformedNowhere in the toolkit, reflection prompts, or glossary is there engagement with when fixed goals or rigid plans genuinely outperform iterative experimentation — e.g. irreversible 'one-way-door' decisions where the cost of a failed 'tiny experiment' is not recoverable, regulated or high-stakes domains requiring upfront compliance, situations where credible pre-commitment itself is the valuable thing (a fixed goal as a costly signal or coordination device), or Duckworth's own evidence that sustained loyalty to one long-term goal (grit) predicts achievement better than nonlinear pivoting in many domains (see extract-leadership-summit-2026-grit-and-growth#p1). The 'Response 1 / Response 2' dichotomy risks pathologizing proportionate caution or risk-aversion as a mere automatic fear response rather than sound judgment about irreversibility or stakes.
    Tiny Experiments — read next: Read Annie Duke on distinguishing decisions that warrant maximal upfront information-gathering (one-way doors) from those that reward fast iteration (two-way doors); read Angela Duckworth's Grit for the counter-evidence on sustained single-goal commitment; read on precommitment devices as a rational response to time-inconsistency, not merely a limiting 'linear goal.'
