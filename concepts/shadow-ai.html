Shadow AI & AI Use Policy

Employee use of AI tools outside organizational policy, and policy maturity as the lever that limits it and the data-leakage and compliance risk it creates.

Also known as: shadow AI, unsanctioned AI use, personal AI tools at work, AI acceptable-use policy, AI use policy, shadow IT

Documents (6)

  • Presentation: AI is an Amplifier: Scale High Performance, Not Dysfunction Nathen Harvey · 2026-10-21 source ↗ Reader 🖥️
    AI is an amplifier of whatever organizational system it lands in, so its returns come not from adopting the tools but from fixing the surrounding system (seven DORA capabilities, small batches, user focus, platforms that serve agents and non-engineer builders) and even from radically rethinking bottlenecks like code review, while treating DORA's findings only as hypotheses to test locally.
  • The State of AI Security: Threats, Tools, and Skills Gaps Mike Loukides · 2026-10-21 source ↗ Reader 🖥️
    AI has permanently changed security for attackers and defenders alike, but O'Reilly's survey of security professionals shows defenders are worried about the wrong things (overreliance and data leakage rather than prompt injection, poisoning or agent permissions), are using only general-purpose AI rather than the specialized tools that actually help, and succeed mainly where organizations pair comprehensive AI policy, funding and skills-building as a whole-organization effort.
  • When Restrictive Cybersecurity Policies Backfire Aaron Kay, Russell Spitler, Peter Jin · 2026-10-21 Reader 🖥️
    Security policies that strip employees of autonomy, or that are later reversed without explanation, erode employees' belief that cyber threats are serious, so leaders must design and communicate policy (choice inside the guardrails, threat explained first, the exit planned, beliefs measured) to protect that belief and not only to reduce risk.
  • Is Your AI Training the Competition? William Marks · 2026-09-08 source ↗ Reader 🖥️
    Beyond protecting raw data, companies must actively defend 'logic sovereignty' — ownership of the specific expertise and reasoning their employees apply through AI tools — because AI providers absorb that expertise through feedback-clause carve-outs, synthetic-training pipelines, and the structural flywheel of heavy use, in ways standard 'no training on our prompts' contracts do not prevent.
  • Governing AI in the Cloud: A Practical Guide for Architects Dave Ward · 2026-06-15 source ↗ Reader 🖥️
    Architects should regain control of ungoverned ('shadow') AI not by trying to police the models themselves but by building a staged, automated control plane in the cloud infrastructure — discover every AI touchpoint, classify data at the moment of creation, and enforce access at the data path through IAM and policy-as-code — while making the compliant path the easiest one so teams adopt governance instead of routing around it.
  • Co-Intelligence, by Ethan Mollick: a Summary Jérémie Rostan · 2024-11-18 source ↗ Reader 🖥️
    Large language models are best understood not as traditional software but as an unpredictable, human-like 'alien intelligence' with jagged, unevenly distributed capabilities — which means working with them well requires deliberate, continuously renegotiated human practices (experimentation, oversight, personification, assuming today's AI is the worst you'll ever use) across creative work, the workplace, and education, in service of augmenting rather than replacing human intelligence, judgment, and meaning.

Open gaps (6)

  • incompleteFowler offers no mechanism for detecting when 'disposable' software is silently graduating into consequential software. The disposable/consequential boundary is treated as legible to the author, but in practice it is one of the most common failure modes in software organisations — internal scripts becoming critical infrastructure.
    Bliki: Vibe Coding — read next: Cutler on institutional overload and shadow systems; classic discussions of 'tactical' vs 'strategic' programming (Ousterhout, Philosophy of Software Design); literature on shadow IT and end-user computing.
  • incompleteThe article's proposed defenses — splitting a task apart and keeping only the 'stitching together' step in-house, or using a public model for rough ideas and fine-tuning a smaller in-house model on the real work — are never evaluated for practicality: how much of the value of frontier-model reasoning is lost when the integrating step is withheld, and whether a smaller in-house model can absorb frontier-quality reasoning without becoming a materially weaker substitute.
    Is Your AI Training the Competition? — read next: Research on task-decomposition costs in AI-assisted workflows; small-model fine-tuning/distillation-from-a-public-model literature and its quality gap versus frontier models.
  • incompleteThe book (per this summary) does not substantively address AI's environmental cost — resource extraction, energy consumption, CO2 emissions — despite being otherwise comprehensive across societal, educational and workplace impacts. The summary names this explicitly as a blind spot rather than the extraction inferring it.
    Co-Intelligence, by Ethan Mollick: a Summary — read next: Material on AI's compute/energy footprint and its organizational and policy implications.
  • incompleteMollick's 'Centaur' (a strategy for dividing labor with AI, paired with 'Cyborg') and Cory Doctorow's 'centaur' (a benefit-direction diagnostic, paired with 'reverse centaur') are two unrelated concepts that happen to share a word — a textbook term-translation hazard for syntopical reading across this corpus, since a naive keyword match would wrongly treat all 'centaur' references as the same idea.
    Co-Intelligence, by Ethan Mollick: a Summary — read next: extract-everyone-i-recommend-on-ai-is-optimistic-except-cory-doctorow (Doctorow's centaur/reverse-centaur) — read side by side with this document's Centaur/Cyborg to keep the two senses distinct in the registry and in any term-translation table.
  • incompleteThree of the five principles (make the secure path less aversive, explain the threat before the control, measure beliefs) are presented alongside the experiments but are not tested by them. The explain-first sequencing claim in particular has no described condition comparing orders.
    When Restrictive Cybersecurity Policies Backfire — read next: Experimental work on message sequencing and autonomy-supportive communication in compliance settings; security-awareness training effectiveness meta-analyses.
  • incompleteNo account of when the reactance cost outweighs the risk-reduction benefit. The authors insist some systems must be locked down, yet give no criterion for which controls should stay hard (e.g. pasting source code into public models, as in Samsung's actual leak) versus which should be offered with choice.
    When Restrictive Cybersecurity Policies Backfire — read next: Risk-tiering of controls (cf. risk-tiering and guardrail design); cost-benefit work on usable security.
