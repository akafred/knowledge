Tensions

Should the harness/platform and its safety be centralized or left distributed across teams?
from: What is the future of software development in the agentic/AI age?
  • Notes from FOSE Europe (Giles Edwards-Alexander): Distributed — dedicated harness-engineering teams are a mistake; per-team friction in building harnesses is creative and generative.
  • Looking back at the Future of Software Engineering unconference (Bartosz Ocytko): Centralized — safety for non-engineers should come from a tightly-constrained, self-healing platform that ships access and data guardrails.
  • AI Is Changing Software Engineering. The Fundamentals Still Matter. (Abby Bangser): Centralized enabling layer — platform, SRE, and ops teams should provide API-first, MCP-accessible tooling that others safely consume.
  • Fragments: February 18 (Martin Fowler (Feb 18)): Centralized golden path — platform teams should provide fast-but-safe 'bullet trains' with security built in by default.
Assessment: A real operating-model split. Edwards-Alexander's distributed stance rests almost entirely on the DevOps-team analogy (his own extraction flags it as asserted-on-intuition, not argued), whereas the platform camp has the stronger concrete case for the citizen-developer safety problem — you cannot distribute production-data guardrails to non-engineers. The tension is partly reconcilable: Ocytko himself concedes a platform tight enough for non-engineers may be too constraining for expert engineers' identity and innovation, which is exactly the freedom Edwards-Alexander is protecting. The unresolved question is whether one platform can be both.

  • incompleteThe boundary of 'essential human work' is asserted but under-drawn. Moreira says product people can safely leave AI to build software 'when safety and big concerns' are absent, while complex concurrent systems still need technical judgment — but she gives no criteria for classifying which systems warrant formal specification versus which can be left to unspecified AI generation. Without that decision procedure, 'defining correct behaviour is essential human work' cannot be operationalised: a team cannot tell where the line falls for their system.

  • illogicalThe CDR Calculus is explicitly marketed as a breakthrough over prior frameworks ('Most frameworks of this kind merely describe. This one forces leadership teams to stop debating AI ethics in the abstract and start making concrete choices about specific systems') but structurally it is a standard two-axis, four-quadrant positioning matrix — the same genre as a BCG growth-share matrix or Eisenhower matrix — with no stated methodology for scoring where a given deployment falls on either axis, no threshold distinguishing 'positive' from 'negative' impact, and every one of its eight worked examples was chosen because it fits its quadrant unambiguously in hindsight. Nothing in the mechanism itself (as opposed to the authors' own analytical skill in choosing examples) demonstrates that it 'forces concrete choices' rather than simply providing post-hoc vocabulary for choices already visible from outcomes. This is close to consulting-firm framework-packaging: a memorable four-box label set standing in for an actual measurement or decision procedure.
    Responsible AI Is Becoming a Growth Strategy — read next: Read against frameworks in the corpus that do carry real measurement machinery — extract-outsourced-ai-still-own-the-risk's mapping of regulatory regimes onto NIST AI RMF/ISO 42001, and extract-governing-ai-in-the-cloud's automated classification-and-enforcement pipeline (event-driven data classification, policy-as-code) — to see the difference between an operationalized governance framework and a rhetorical 2x2. Also worth testing the Calculus against a genuinely hard, real deployment (not one of the article's clean anecdotes) to see whether it actually helps classify ambiguity or only relabels the obvious.

Svenson never addresses whether the same consent-before-capture principle should bind non-lawyers in equally confidentiality-sensitive settings (an engineering leader in a vendor negotiation, an HR conversation, an M&A due-diligence call, a performance discussion), where no professional body issues an ethics opinion and the only floor is the (often much lower) state wiretap statute. His own framework — 'the risk isn't what gets stored, it's what gets captured' — has nothing legal-profession-specific about it and generalizes cleanly, but he doesn't make that move himself.
