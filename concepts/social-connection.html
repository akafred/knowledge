Social Connection & Relatedness

Belonging and non-instrumental colleague bonds as a first-order driver of team performance — the relatedness leg of self-determination theory — distinct from psychological safety (safe to speak up) and trust (the earned asset).

Also known as: relatedness, belonging, team cohesion, social connection, non-instrumental colleague bonds, SDT relatedness

    Drawing on Christopher Lasch's diagnosis of a self freed from social obligation but left fragile and image-dependent, Barker argues that meaning comes not from unlimited freedom and self-focus but from voluntarily binding yourself — through irreversible commitments, relationships spread beyond job and partner, self-compassion in place of comparison, internally chosen standards, and investment in the next generation.
  • TBM 438: Am I the Only Weirdo in This Company? John Cutler from The Beautiful Mess · 2026-09-05 Reader 🖥️
    Organizations look far more uniform in belief than they actually are because what you observe is a compound of three layered things — power-weighted norms, individual adaptation to those norms, and the actual diverse beliefs of the people underneath — and we systematically mistake the middle layer (visible adaptation) for the bottom layer (real belief) while underweighting the top layer (power) that is actually driving what gets displayed.
  • Amy Gibson's Post Amy Gibson · 2026-08-02 Reader 🖥️
    What determines whether a meeting works is not its agenda, room, technology, or timing, but what people walk away with — feeling heard, clear on priorities, energized — and Gibson proposes five distinct, differently-cadenced meeting types (weekly sync, monthly 1:1, monthly all-hands, quarterly strategy session, annual culture touchpoint) as the mechanism for reliably producing that outcome.
  • This Is How To Live A Good Life: 5 Secrets From Ancient Wisdom Eric Barker · 2026-07-19 Reader 🖥️
    Barker argues that living well is not a state to achieve or a fact to learn but a sustained practice — Aristotle's claim that character, self-control, and happiness (eudaimonia) all consist in habituated activity rather than insight, willpower, or declared intention — and treats this 2300-year-old framework as credible chiefly because contemporary psychological research keeps independently converging on the same conclusions.
  • What's in a Name: The Moth Podcast The Moth · 2026-04-10 Reader 🖥️

Open gaps (17)

  • incompleteRemote/hybrid applicability is under-evidenced relative to the headline promise. The article opens by promising the findings help 'even within a remote or hybrid work setting,' yet most differentiators are inherently in-person or proximity-based: 'time at the office discussing non-work matters,' meeting for coffee/tea/an alcoholic beverage. Only the phone-call behavior clearly transfers to distributed teams; the mechanism by which the rest survive full remoteness is not shown.
    5 Things High-Performing Teams Do Differently — read next: Investigate which specific connection behaviors actually transfer to fully-remote teams versus which depend on physical co-location, and whether deliberately engineered remote rituals reproduce the in-person effect.
  • incompleteNo context is given on team size, function (e.g. engineering vs. sales), growth stage, or remote/hybrid setup; the specific cadences and durations (30 min weekly, 45-60 min monthly 1:1s, quarterly 2-3hr sessions) are presented as universal defaults with no acknowledgment that they'd need adaptation for, say, a large distributed engineering org.
    Amy Gibson's Post — read next: Compare against context-sensitive team-design frameworks (e.g. your own pattern language on cross-disciplinary team spaces) that make cadence and structure conditional on team size, distribution, and work type rather than fixed.

  • illogicalThe article's central meta-argument — that modern research 'lands surprisingly close' to Aristotle, which validates his ethics — commits a selection bias: only convergent findings are cited (one 2011 self-control study, one happiness-pursuit study, the Harvard Study of Adult Development) while no divergent or disconfirming modern findings are considered, and no account is given of where Aristotle's ethics has been rejected, revised, or superseded by later moral philosophy or psychology.
    This Is How To Live A Good Life: 5 Secrets From Ancient Wisdom — read next: Look for modern findings or ethical positions that diverge from Aristotle (e.g., situationist critiques of virtue ethics — Gilbert Harman, John Doris — arguing character traits are far less predictive of behavior than situational factors) to test whether the 'convergence' really holds up once disconfirming evidence is included.
  • uninformedThe claim that 'psychology lands surprisingly close to Aristotle' rests on a single, unnamed 2011 study (linked only via a psycnet.apa.org DOI) with no reported researchers, sample, effect size, or replication status — treating one social-psychology finding from just before the replication crisis as settled confirmation of a philosophical claim, with no caveat about the state of that literature.
    This Is How To Live A Good Life: 5 Secrets From Ancient Wisdom — read next: Check the linked study's replication history and whether self-control-via-habit-avoidance findings (e.g., Roy Baumeister's ego-depletion-adjacent literature) have held up post-2015 replication efforts.
  • incompleteThe Harvard Study of Adult Development is invoked accurately as a real, long-running longitudinal study, but its purely correlational nature is not addressed — warm relationships correlating with health/happiness outcomes doesn't establish that Aristotle's specific mechanism ('eating salt together,' shared virtue-forming experience) is the operative pathway, as opposed to socioeconomic confounds, personality traits that independently cause both relationship quality and health, or reverse causation (healthier people sustain relationships more easily).
    This Is How To Live A Good Life: 5 Secrets From Ancient Wisdom — read next: Read the Harvard Study's own published analyses (Waldinger & Schulz, The Good Life) for how the researchers themselves discuss causal mechanism and confounds, rather than relying on the popular-summary framing used here.
  • uninformedBarker never engages passages in Aristotle's own texts, or secondary Aristotle scholarship, that complicate the article's implied 'flourishing is available to anyone who habituates well enough' message — notably Aristotle's own view that eudaimonia partly depends on external goods (health, wealth, good birth, good luck) not fully within an individual's control, which sits in real tension with a self-help framing that treats flourishing as purely a matter of individual habit choice.
    This Is How To Live A Good Life: 5 Secrets From Ancient Wisdom — read next: Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics Book I on the role of external goods and fortune in eudaimonia; Martha Nussbaum's 'The Fragility of Goodness' on luck's role in Greek ethical thought.
