TBM 437: Tokens, Hours, Points, and Other Curious Proxies

John Cutler · email · 2026-08-17 Reader 🖥️

AI Unit EconomicsSpeaking the Language of Capital AllocationDORA & Engineering MetricsMeasuring AI ImpactProxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)Token Throughput
Tokens are the latest in a lineage of seductive measurement proxies -- hours, story points, flow metrics -- that organizations mistake for a theory of value because they happen to be unusually easy to measure, and the fix is not a better proxy but making the underlying causal model of value explicit (treating the team as the cost unit, funding durable lanes, stating falsifiable hypotheses) so that ROI becomes the *output* of that model rather than a number computed directly from an easy-to-meter input.

practical · mixed — Argumentative in structure (a thesis defended through a sequence of case studies) but practical in aim: Cutler is arguing for a specific way of doing something -- fund teams and judge AI/engineering investment through an explicit causal model rather than through hours, story points, or tokens. The theoretical scaffolding (construct validity, manufacturing-vs-R&D economics) is in service of that prescription.

Key terms

  • return on tokens
    The vendor- and executive-favored framing that treats AI token consumption as a clean, ROI-ready denominator -- used ironically by Cutler as the latest instance of a recurring measurement mistake, not as a framework he endorses.
  • theory of the system Proxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)
    A set of explicit, falsifiable causal hypotheses ('we believe X will reduce Y without materially increasing Z') that a team or organization commits to about how an investment produces value -- the thing a serious business case actually consists of.
  • classes of service
    The manufacturing/Kanban practice of segmenting work by sufficiently different work processes or value profiles before comparing flow metrics across it -- without this segmentation, changes in throughput or cost may just reflect a changed mix of what was produced.
  • capacity (as distinguished from hours spent)
    An emergent, designed property of a system (a factory, a team) representing sustainable ability to meet demand -- not a fungible resource that gets 'spent' unit by unit at the moment of use.

Arguments

'Return on tokens' is not a solution to the ROI-measurement problem; it is the newest instance of an old, unsolved problem. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Hours failed as an investment proxy due to fungibility, quality-blindness, context-switching cost, and non-linearity.
  • Lean/flow metrics and story points failed as investment proxies because product work usually violates the manufacturing preconditions (classes of service, stable demand, homogeneous units) those metrics require.
  • The same three failure patterns (I-side focus, easy short-term attribution, gravitation toward what's easiest to measure) recur across hours, flow metrics, and tokens.
Implicit premises:
  • If a new metric shares the causal structure of prior failed metrics, it will fail in the same way regardless of how precisely it can be measured.
  • Measurement precision of an input is orthogonal to the validity of using that input as an ROI denominator.
Inferences:
  • Organizations and vendors currently embracing 'return on tokens' will eventually encounter the same attribution, non-linearity, and boundary-setting problems that undermined hours and story points, unless they change what they're measuring rather than just how precisely they measure it.
A serious AI investment case should be framed as an explicit, falsifiable 'theory of the system,' not as a point ROI estimate.
Explicit premises:
  • Point ROI estimates ('$10M spent -> $30M value') hide unstated assumptions about augmentation vs. replacement, commodity vs. differentiated capability, complementary assets, and downstream/lifetime costs.
  • Theory-of-the-system statements make those assumptions explicit and individually testable.
Implicit premises:
  • An explicit, falsifiable hypothesis is epistemically superior to an implicit, unfalsifiable one even when both produce the same headline number.
  • Organizations and teams are capable of tracking and updating belief in specific stated hypotheses over time, rather than only ever producing a single point estimate.
Inferences:
  • Teams given responsibility for AI spend need a stated theory of the system, not just a budget, in order to make intelligent tradeoffs between people, models, tools, and other forms of capacity -- this is effectively a prerequisite for decentralizing AI-spend decisions down to team level.

Narratives

  • The world shifted 'from a buffet model to buying each dumpling one at a time,' and now everyone is panicking about the ROI of each dumpling.
    → Supports the idea that the return-on-tokens anxiety was triggered by a change in the visibility/metering of AI cost, not by a change in the underlying difficulty of creating value with it -- the problem was always there, metering just made it legible.
  • The deep-onboarding specialist working in their 'magic hour' of uninterrupted morning productivity cannot be immediately swapped into a legacy backend refactor, and a highly skilled developer reactively pulled into unfamiliar code has less leverage than a more junior but contextually situated one.
    → Supports the proposition that hours are not fungible -- who spends the hour, and when, matters as much as how many hours are logged.
  • You might bang your head against a problem for eight hours and get nowhere, and the ninth hour unlocks all the value.
    → Supports the non-linear, threshold-based relationship between time invested and outcomes -- undermines any metric treating hours as a continuous input.
  • A team spends 5% longer on every enhancement to pay down debt and 'garden' the codebase; three years later, a version of that team (with some turnover) is 'absolutely flying,' but there's no clean place to 'book' that time or connect it to the eventual return.
    → Supports the case for treating the team as the cost unit connected to a causal model, rather than trying to decompose and attribute investment to individual time buckets -- some hours have compounding option value that no attribution scheme can capture.
  • Ten fill-in-the-blank 'We believe...' sentence templates, contrasted directly against the naive sentence 'We will spend $10M on AI and generate $30M of value.'
    → Functions as a worked example (rather than a narrative in the conventional sense) showing what a falsifiable, defensible AI business case actually looks like in practice, as opposed to a point estimate.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why does the return-on-tokens conversation feel so urgent right now?
    A change in metering/visibility (buffet -> per-dumpling billing) surfaced a problem that was already latent, compounded by companies wanting cover for headcount reductions they couldn't otherwise justify.
  • ✅ Are hours a legitimate way to understand investment?
    Yes for understanding where effort is going, but only with explicit caveats about non-fungibility, quality-blindness, context-switching cost, non-linearity, long-term option effects, and non-independence of allocation buckets.
  • ✅ Are lean/flow metrics (cycle time, throughput, story points) a legitimate proxy for investment or ROI?
    No -- legitimate only for continuous improvement (bottleneck-finding, system health), never as a proxy for investment or value; story points explicitly rejected beyond disposable, near-term use.
  • ✅ Is AI token spend comparable to an equivalent amount of human-capital spend?
    Not straightforwardly -- multiple structural disanalogies (externalities, fixed vs. variable cost, residual-capital capture, marginal-vs-average returns, complementary assets) make direct comparison unsound.
  • ✅ What should a defensible AI business case actually look like?
    A stated theory of the system -- explicit, falsifiable causal hypotheses -- rather than a point ROI estimate, with ROI properly understood as the observed output of that hypothesis, not the input pitch.
  • ⭕ Is a working, validated measurement scheme for AI ROI actually achievable right now?
    Cutler explicitly declines to claim this is solved: 'This is extremely important given that everyone is learning at the moment. No one has figured this out.' He reframes the question rather than answering it.

Gaps

  • incompleteCutler never specifies who is supposed to build, own, and adjudicate disputes about the 'theory of the system' / causal model he proposes -- finance, engineering leadership, product, or some new function. Without a named owner and a process for contesting a causal narrative's validity, 'make your theory of the system explicit' risks becoming just as rhetorically gameable as the proxies it replaces: a leader can write a confident-sounding, well-formatted causal story without it being falsifiable in practice, and nothing in the piece specifies who would catch that.
    Read next: Organizational-design and FinOps-style AI-cost-governance literature on who owns cross-functional investment decisions; DORA's Capabilities Model research on how measurement gets operationalized organizationally rather than just prescribed.
  • incompleteThe piece doesn't address Goodhart's Law risk in its own alternative. 'Movement in validated leading-indicator proxies' is still a metric, and once it's attached to a $1.5M-plus-token-spend team business case, it becomes exactly the kind of target Cutler spent the whole article warning is prone to gaming (as story points, deploy frequency, and PR volume already were). He treats 'validated proxies' as structurally immune to the same social/incentive dynamics he just diagnosed for every prior proxy.
    Read next: extract-8-engineering-metrics-ai-broke's argument that Goodhart's Law has become inescapable under AI (metric-gaming as a side effect of normal tool use rather than something requiring extra effort); general Goodhart's Law literature.
    closed byAre You Getting Your Money's Worth from AI? — Shore's 'measure the approach, then stop measuring' discipline — never tying a metric to individual/team performance, and retiring it once you've learned from a given approach — is a concrete, general mitigation for the Goodhart's-Law risk this gap flags in leading-indicator proxies, applicable regardless of which metric (tokens, PRs, or a validated leading indicator) is chosen.
  • incompleteNo discussion of scale or organizational-profile effects on the prescription. Does a $50M-token-spend enterprise need materially different governance from a five-person team's monthly token bill? 'Team as cost unit + explicit causal model' is presented as a universal recipe, but DORA's AI Capabilities Model research suggests AI-adoption outcomes cluster into distinct organizational-profile clusters -- implying that one governance recipe may not transfer cleanly across very different scales or team maturities.
