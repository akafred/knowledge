From the author's 48 hours among SF AI founders, as the author reports it: as models and software become cheap to swap and copy, durable AI value is moving into the surrounding layers (harness, proprietary or cleaned data, memory, evals and taste, distribution), so the test of a company is whether it gets better or worse when the next model drops, and revenue growth alone is a poor guide.

practical · mixed — A LinkedIn post: twelve numbered observations from 48 hours in San Francisco with AI founders and investors. It is anecdote and hearsay (mostly 'someone told me') mixed with the author's own speculation and a few reframing analogies. The implied action is for founders and builders: ask the 'new model' test question, build in harness/data/eval layers, and look for whitespace. It is not an evidence-based argument.

Key terms

  • Harness (as operating system) Agent Harness
    The software layer around a model that orchestrates and constrains it: model routing, industry data, expert-written evals, permissions and a review screen. It plays the role an OS plays for a CPU.
  • Skills and plugins (as apps)
    The third layer of the analogy: the applications that run on top of a harness.
  • GPT wrapper (reclaimed)
    A thin product layer on top of someone else's model. Isenberg uses it approvingly ('in a good way') for harness-centric teams that treat the model as swappable.
  • Better or worse when a new model drops
    A diagnostic for whether a company rides model improvements (benefits) or is made redundant by them (absorbed).
  • Worth more dead than alive
    A shut-down startup's archive of Slack, email and code can be licensed for more than the operating business is worth.
  • Taste (who can write evals) Taste & Judgment
    The ability to judge which of AI's endless ideas are good, operationalized as writing evals.
  • Deployment problems (physical AI)
    A robotics founder's claim, as relayed, that the hardest problems are now getting robots to work in the field. Human-fallback calls double as training data.

Arguments

Durable value in AI is moving away from the model and from copyable software toward the surrounding layers, especially data, evals and the harness. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Software is getting easy to copy (one person cloned about 10 apps worth $5B+ each).
  • A founder reports that the model is about 20% of the product and the rest is industry data, evals, permissions and review UX.
  • Data brokers and licensed archives of dead startups are reportedly earning money.
  • Enterprises find their internal data messy when they try to use it with agents.
Implicit premises:
  • A clone captures the original's value (or at least erodes it).
  • Data cannot be cloned as easily as code and so keeps its price.
  • The reported cases are representative rather than outliers.
  • AI labs will keep paying for data at current prices.
Inferences:
  • If data is the moat, then the data-broker market and dead-startup licensing are the most direct prices of that moat, and also the most bubble-prone.
  • Cleaning data is a services-heavy business more than a software one, which fits poorly with the claim that software is easy to copy.
Many AI companies will show huge revenue and terrible retention, so revenue growth is a poor guide to durability. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • The new bar is $1B in revenue in 12 months.
  • Nobody at the author's events talked about retention.
Implicit premises:
  • Fast AI revenue is substantially driven by experimentation and novelty rather than embedded use.
  • Silence in conversation indicates the metric is bad rather than merely unmentioned.
Inferences:
  • Taken together with 'worth more dead than alive' and the dotcom comparison, a retention shock would hit the data-licensing and valuation story hardest.
The test 'does the company get better or worse when a new model drops' separates durable AI companies from exposed ones. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Half the table went quiet when someone asked.
  • The best teams treat the model as swappable and route among models.
Implicit premises:
  • New model releases will keep expanding what the model can do natively, absorbing product features.
  • Companies that differentiate on the harness and data layers are strengthened rather than absorbed by better models.
  • A company can answer the question honestly in advance.
Inferences:
  • Model-swappability is itself a design requirement that can be assessed before a model drops.

Narratives

  • A dinner where someone asks 'if a new model drops next month, does your company get better or worse?' and half the table goes quiet.
    → Dramatizes that many AI founders have not thought through their dependence on model progress, which supports the model-drop test.
  • Someone shows the author about 10 apps worth $5B+ each that he has cloned.
    → Illustrates that software is now cheap to copy, which is the premise for 'value moves to data'.
  • A robotics founder describes robots that call a human when stuck, with each call logged as training data.
    → Shows deployment friction turning into a data flywheel, and supports the data-as-moat theme in the physical domain.
  • Stories of data brokers selling company data to AI labs for around $2M a week, and of shut-down companies licensing their Slack, email and code.
    → Supports the claim that data has become the valuable asset, at the possible cost of a bubble.
  • Founders sponsor buses, buy billboards and throw parties with famous DJs.
    → Evidence that distribution and attention are the bottleneck, and a dotcom-era echo.

Questions addressed

  • ⭕ What makes an AI startup durable as models keep improving?
    Offered as a test question (point 2) and a list of candidate layers (harness, data, evals, memory, distribution), with no worked-out answer.
  • ⭕ Where is the whitespace in AI right now?
    The stack analogy is offered as a tool for spotting whitespace, and data cleaning is the one concrete opportunity named. The author admits he is 'still figuring out which niches are left' in data brokering.
  • ⭕ Is the data-licensing and fast-revenue boom a beginning or a bubble?
    Explicitly left open ('I can't tell if it's the beginning or a bubble'), with only the retention worry and the dotcom comparison as hints.
  • ⭕ Is a harness a durable business or just a thin wrapper that platform providers will absorb?
    Asserted to be a good kind of wrapper because of the surrounding data and evals; absorption risk is not addressed.

Gaps

  • uninformedAlmost every load-bearing claim is unsourced founder-circuit anecdote: the $2M a week to data brokers, the roughly 10 cloned $5B apps, the 20% model share, the reported memory and robotics views. No company, number or method is given, the vantage (SF dinners, interested founders and investors) is selected, and the 'nobody talked about retention' claim is an argument from silence. The post cannot distinguish a real shift from circuit talk.
    Read next: Independent evidence on AI-lab data purchases and licensing prices, and on revenue quality and retention of fast-growing AI companies (cohort data rather than founder stories).
  • incompleteThe scarce-asset thesis is never reconciled. 'Value moves to data' (point 6), the model is 20% and the rest is data, evals and review UX (point 5), 'memory wins' (point 8), 'taste plus evals is the hire' (point 11) and 'distribution is the bottleneck' (point 12) each name a different scarce thing. The post does not say which dominates or how they interact, nor why a clone that is easy to build would not also be easy to feed with data.
    Read next: Moats and defensibility analyses for AI-era products (data loops versus distribution versus switching costs) that rank or order these candidates.
  • illogical'Harnesses are the new GPT wrappers, in a good way' reverses the usual meaning without argument. GPT wrappers were seen as fragile because platform providers absorb them; the post does not say why harnesses escape that, beyond one founder's unsourced 20% figure. The harness-as-OS analogy also sits uneasily with an OS being a platform layer one controls, whereas a harness depends on models it does not.
    Read next: Wrapper-versus-infrastructure and harness-as-product arguments that address platform absorption, and evidence for the model share of product value.
  • incompleteData licensing is presented as an opportunity (broker trade, selling a dead startup's Slack, email and code) with no mention of employee and customer consent, privacy law, trade secrets, copyright or contractual constraints. 'Worth more dead than alive' implicitly assumes the archive can legally and ethically be sold, and that labs can use it without exposing what people wrote in private channels.
    Read next: Legal and governance treatments of data licensing, workplace communications as training data, and the data-sovereignty implications of selling internal corpora.
  • incomplete'The AI that remembers the most about you wins' assumes memory produces lock-in and preference, without asking about portability, user control, privacy cost, or whether recall is more valuable than judgment about what to forget. The claim rests on a single unnamed consumer executive.
    Read next: Agent-memory design and its drift, poisoning and privacy risks; work on whether personal context creates switching costs.
