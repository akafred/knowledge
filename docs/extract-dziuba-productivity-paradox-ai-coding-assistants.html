The productivity paradox of AI coding assistants

Lisa Dziuba · article · 2025-09-12 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent SecurityContext Rot & Context LimitsMeasuring AI ImpactSoftware Supply-Chain SecurityTechnical Debt
AI coding assistants create a productivity placebo — they feel fast because of instant dopamine-reward feedback — but measured across independent studies they deliver marginal or negative net productivity for experienced developers on real codebases, degrade code quality and security, and the widely repeated '10x productivity' claim does not survive controlled measurement.

practical · argumentative — Builds a case against the prevailing '10x productivity' narrative by marshaling a sequence of external studies, each addressing one facet (perceived vs. measured speed, quality, security) of the same thesis.

Key terms

  • productivity paradox / productivity placebo
    The gap between how fast AI coding assistance *feels* (dopamine-driven instant feedback in the editor) and how fast it actually *is* when measured against working, production-ready code.
  • context rot Context Rot & Context Limits
    The degradation of AI output quality as more context accumulates within a session, because the model starts pulling in irrelevant details from earlier prompts.
  • the 70% problem / demo gap
    AI assistants reliably get a task about 70% of the way to a working solution, but the remaining 30% (edge cases, architecture fixes, tests, cleanup) is the hard, slow part; a demo only has to run once, production code has to run a million times without breaking, and that gap is why experienced developers in particular don't see net time savings.

Propositions

  • explicitAcross multiple independent studies (METR's RCT, the 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, Faros AI's telemetry), AI coding assistants' measured productivity effect is marginal or negative for experienced developers working in codebases they already know, even though those developers subjectively feel faster.conf: high
    supportsThe Acceleration Whiplash · p5 — Both independently make the same core claim: telemetry/measured data shows developers feel more productive with AI even as review/coordination costs rise, which self-report alone would miss.
  • explicitIn METR's July 2025 RCT, experienced open-source developers using AI tools (mainly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet) were on average 19% slower, despite predicting beforehand they would be 24% faster and still believing afterward they had been sped up by roughly 20%.conf: high
    supportsPresentation: The Ironies of A^2 I^2 (Ironies of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Incidents) · p14 — Both cite the identical METR 2025 RCT finding (developers felt faster, measured 19%/20% slower) as evidence that self-assessed AI productivity cannot be trusted.
  • explicitOutput quality degrades within a session as context accumulates ('context rot'), because the model pulls in irrelevant details from earlier prompts, producing bloated or off-target code that looks correct but doesn't solve the actual problem.conf: medium
  • explicitThe claim that AI makes developers '10x' more productive does not hold up against controlled research: GitHub/Microsoft's 2023 vendor-run benchmark found a 55.8% speedup concentrated among less-experienced developers on scaffolding work, and the MIT/Harvard/Microsoft 2024 field study of 4,867 professional developers found a 26.08% task-completion increase, again concentrated among junior/newer hires, with senior developers already familiar with the codebase seeing little or no measurable speed-up.conf: high
  • explicitAI-enabled parallelism (Faros AI's July 2025 telemetry across 10,000+ developers in 1,255 teams: +9% tasks, +47% pull requests per developer per day) trades typing time for more context-switching, orchestration, and validation overhead across more parallel workstreams, canceling out much of the apparent speed-up.conf: medium
  • explicitAI-generated code introduces measurably more security risk than human-written code: Apiiro's 2024 research found 322% more privilege-escalation paths, 153% more design flaws, a 40% increase in secrets exposure, a 2.5x higher rate of critical (CVSS 7.0+) vulnerabilities, and 60% more reviewer security comments — while AI-assisted commits were merged into production 4x faster, bypassing normal review depth.conf: medium
    supportsThe Acceleration Whiplash · p3 — Both document rising downstream quality/incident costs (bugs, vulnerabilities, review burden) accompanying AI-adoption-driven throughput, from different vendor telemetry sources (Apiiro's security metrics here, Faros's delivery metrics there).
  • explicitAI coding assistants expand the software supply-chain attack surface itself (new runtimes, plugins, MCP connections) as shown concretely by the 2025 Google Gemini CLI remote-code-execution bug and the 2024 poisoned Amazon Q VS Code extension update that instructed the assistant to delete local files and shut down AWS EC2 instances.conf: high
  • explicitAI closes the 'demo gap' quickly (getting to a working-looking first version, roughly '70% of the way') but the remaining ~30% — edge cases, architecture fixes, tests, production cleanup — is where experienced developers who already know the right solution lose the most time, because the assistant adds friction rather than removing it for them.conf: medium
    supportsHow AI will change software engineering · p3 — Both describe the same underlying mechanism — fast AI-generated output that looks done but isn't — as the hidden cost that offsets apparent speed gains, framed by McMahon's piece as 'flab' that increases the rest of the team's downstream cost.
  • implicitThe '10x engineer'/10x-AI-productivity narrative persists disproportionately in business leadership relative to developers because it offers a politically convenient justification ('do more with less') that doesn't require engaging with where the actual delivery bottlenecks (design review, PR queues, test failures, context switching, deployment waits) actually lie.conf: medium

Arguments

The common claim that AI coding assistants deliver dramatic (even '10x') productivity gains for professional software development does not hold up to controlled measurement — especially for experienced developers working on real, already-familiar codebases. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • METR's RCT found experienced OSS developers 19% slower with AI despite believing they were faster.
  • The 2025 Stack Overflow survey found only 16.3% of developers report AI improved productivity 'to a great extent,' with 41.4% reporting little or no effect.
  • The 2023 GitHub/Microsoft and 2024 MIT/Harvard/Microsoft studies found real but modest gains (55.8% and 26.08%) concentrated among less-experienced developers doing scaffolding-type work, not seniors.
  • Faros AI's 2025 telemetry shows AI-enabled parallelism is partly consumed by increased context-switching and review/orchestration overhead.
Implicit premises:
  • The specific study populations (OSS maintainers for METR; Stack Overflow's self-selected respondents; enterprise developers at Microsoft/Accenture/a Fortune 100 firm for the MIT study) generalize to professional software development broadly despite differing tasks, tools, and time windows.
  • Telemetry and RCT data are more trustworthy than self-reported developer sentiment or vendor-run benchmarks, and the specific studies foregrounded are representative of the evidence base rather than selected because they support the thesis.
Inferences:
  • Headcount or roadmap decisions premised on an assumed 10x AI productivity multiplier are likely miscalibrated, and the business pressure to believe the narrative may itself be sustaining it independent of the evidence.

Narratives

  • The author's own Cerbos dev team is split between an AI-enthusiast camp (Cursor/Claude Code for shipping speed) and a skeptical camp; a quoted teammate calls agent code output untrustworthy and 'an instant tech-debt factory' while conceding it's useful as a codebase-grokking tool, and separately flags that patterns learned while using these tools break with every model update.
    → Grounds the abstract productivity-paradox argument in the author's own team's lived, ongoing disagreement, and supplies two observations (demo-vs-production distrust; model-update instability) not covered by the cited external studies.
  • Marcus Hutchins's quote describing LLMs as hijacking 'the human brain's reward system,' giving the feeling of achievement without the heavy lifting.
    → Supplies the psychological mechanism behind the METR illusion (why developers felt faster while measurably being slower).

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Do AI coding assistants actually make professional developers more productive?
    Modestly and unevenly — real gains concentrated in MVP/scaffolding/junior-developer contexts; marginal-to-negative for experienced developers working in codebases they already know well.
  • ✅ Where does the oft-repeated '10x productivity' claim come from, and does it hold up?
    It does not hold up against controlled studies; the author attributes its persistence partly to a business incentive to believe a convenient 'do more with less' narrative.
  • ⭕ Are the studies juxtaposed here (different years, populations, tools, and methodologies) actually measuring a comparable quantity, such that their specific percentages can be meaningfully compared or summed into one 'productivity paradox' picture?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article juxtaposes studies with materially different methodologies, populations, task types, and time windows (2023 vendor-run benchmark on a toy HTTP-server task; 2024 enterprise field study; 2025 METR RCT on real OSS repos; 2025 Faros telemetry) as though they measure the same underlying quantity ('AI coding productivity'), without discussing whether the specific percentages are actually comparable across such different conditions.

  • incompleteThe security findings (Apiiro's 322% / 153% / 40% / 2.5x / 60% figures) come from a single 2024 vendor-published study, cited without independent replication, methodology detail, or any discussion of Apiiro's own commercial interest (a code-security vendor) in publicizing high AI-code-risk numbers — a pattern this corpus elsewhere explicitly flags when a vendor's own research underwrites an adoption or risk claim.
