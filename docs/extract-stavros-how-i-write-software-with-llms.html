How I write software with LLMs

stavros.io · article · 2026-03-09 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent HarnessAgent Planning ArtifactsHuman-in-the-LoopMulti-Agent OrchestrationSupervisory Programming
A solo practitioner can now sustain multi-week, tens-of-thousands-of-line AI-written software projects at a lower defect rate than hand-writing, but only by replacing 'ask one model to do everything' with a fixed division of labor across models (a strong model as architect doing interactive up-front design, a cheaper model as developer doing strict implementation, and one-to-three structurally independent models as reviewers) plus explicit human approval gates -- and only on technology the human already understands well enough to catch bad architectural decisions before the agent compounds them.

practical · mixed — Primarily practical (argues for a specific workflow to adopt), expository in its description of the multi-model architect/developer/reviewer pattern, and narrative in its long annotated real-session transcript that carries most of the concrete evidence.

Key terms

  • Architect
    The single, strongest-available model the author personally interacts with via chat; its job is to negotiate goals/constraints/tradeoffs with the human until explicitly told 'approved', then split the agreed design into a low-level, file-and-function-level task plan for the developer.
  • Developer (agent role)
    A deliberately weaker, more token-efficient model (Sonnet 4.6 in the author's setup) whose job is strictly to implement the architect's plan with minimal latitude for its own high-level choices, then hand off to reviewers.
  • Reviewers (cross-family review)
    One to three models, deliberately drawn from different vendors/families than the model that wrote the code, that independently critique the plan and diff; disagreement among reviewers is escalated to the architect rather than resolved by the developer.

Arguments

A durable, trustworthy coding-agent workflow for a solo practitioner requires a fixed, named division of labor across structurally independent models (architect, developer, reviewers) plus enforced human approval gates, rather than one model doing everything end to end in a single session.
Explicit premises:
  • A model reviewing its own just-written code is close to useless, since it tends to agree with its own prior decisions even from a clean context.
  • Different models have genuinely complementary, somewhat stable strengths and weaknesses (Codex: pedantic/good reviewer; Opus: decisions that track the author's own judgment; Gemini Flash: creative problem-finding).
  • Models left unsupervised at a decision boundary tend to act overeagerly, starting implementation once they believe (not once the human confirms) that the plan is understood.
Implicit premises:
  • A human with strong pre-existing domain knowledge of the target technology is a precondition for the whole workflow's safety -- the system does not actually catch its own architectural blind spots; the human's domain knowledge is the thing that does, which the workflow's framing as a general 'how to write software with LLMs' method understates.
  • The token/latency overhead of relaying work between an expensive architect model, a cheap developer model, and up to three reviewer models is worth paying, even though the author himself notes this ferrying is 'annoying' without native agent-to-agent harness support.
Inferences:
  • If review quality comes from structural independence between the drafting and reviewing model rather than from any one model's raw capability, the practice generalizes beyond a solo practitioner's personal taste into an organizational policy question -- i.e. an argument for mandating cross-vendor model diversity in team tooling, which the article never frames as anything beyond individual preference.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What specific workflow lets multi-week, tens-of-thousands-of-line AI-written projects stay maintainable instead of degrading into unmaintainability after a few days, as the author says used to happen to him?
    A fixed architect/developer/reviewer role split across different models, hand-written agent instructions, and an explicit human approval gate before implementation begins.
  • ⭕ Does this workflow's safety mechanism (a human who understands the architecture well enough to catch bad decisions) survive when the 'human' is not one person but a team that must share that understanding?
    Not addressed; the article is written entirely from the frame of a single practitioner who personally holds all architectural context across every change.
  • ⭕ Why does review granularity (line -> function -> architecture) keep moving upward as models improve, and does that trend eventually eliminate the need for the human-domain-knowledge safety net the rest of the article depends on?
    The author notes the trend and speculates review may not even need to happen at the architecture level 'next year', but does not reconcile this with his own claim, two sections later, that unfamiliarity with the underlying technology is specifically what causes the workflow's main failure mode.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe workflow's entire safety mechanism rests on one human continuously holding architectural context across every change ('I still know every choice that was made above the function level'). The article never considers the team-scale version of this: what happens to the approach when more than one human must jointly own that architect role, how plan files or chat transcripts become durable shared artifacts rather than one person's private conversation history, and how disagreement among human architects (not just among reviewer models) would be resolved.

  • incompleteThe central claim -- a defect rate 'probably significantly lower' than hand-written code -- is entirely self-reported impression with no measurement methodology: no defect count, no time-to-resolution figure, no comparison baseline. This is the same claim that extract-tornhill-borg-code-red-business-impact-code-quality and extract-the-economic-benefit-of-refactoring attempt to quantify rigorously (defect counts via Jira correlation; token cost via a controlled before/after experiment), which this article could be measured against but isn't.
    Read next: See extract-tornhill-borg-code-red-business-impact-code-quality and extract-the-economic-benefit-of-refactoring for quantified methodologies this article's central claim could be tested against.
  • illogicalThe article asserts a trend toward needing less human review per model generation (line -> function -> architecture, possibly disappearing next year) while simultaneously asserting that the workflow's core safety property depends on a human who deeply understands the architecture catching bad decisions before they compound. If review granularity keeps moving upward to the point of disappearing, the same logic implies the human safety net the rest of the article depends on would disappear with it -- a tension the piece never surfaces or resolves.
  • incompleteThe costs of the prescribed safety apparatus -- up to three reviewer models per change, an architect conversation that can run thirty minutes, and an explicit human approval gate -- are never weighed against the speed/defect-rate benefits the article narrates in detail. There is no accounting of total token spend, wall-clock time, or how the cost/benefit shifts for a less important change where the author says he uses fewer reviewers.
