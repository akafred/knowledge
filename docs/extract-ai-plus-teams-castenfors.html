AI + Teams = ?

Marcus Castenfors · article · 2026-09-26 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI Employee vs Tool FramingCoordination CostDurable, Domain-Owning Teams (vs. Fluid Teams)Multiplayer vs Single-Player AITaste & Judgment
The default way people use generative AI on a team — everyone working alone in their own chat window — quietly damages team alignment and accrues a 'collective debt'; teams do better when they deliberately decide where to prompt together (especially to define the problem, which is a human act AI cannot do well) versus where to delegate to AI individually or collectively, and when they widen the room to outside domain experts now that AI has removed the technical barrier to those experts contributing directly.

practical · mixed — A short Substack essay that reports three findings from an NYU 'buildathon' workshop study and converts each into concrete team practices to adopt — expository summary of someone else's research plus the author's own argued recommendations.

Key terms

  • Collective debt
    The accruing misalignment cost of working individually with AI in parallel — progress feels fast and productive to the individual, but the group's shared sense of direction erodes and must eventually be repaid by reconciling divergent outputs.
  • Single-player vs. multi-player AI usage
    Single-player: one person privately prompting AI alone. Multi-player: a team prompting together in the same place/session so the AI's output is immediately shared and debated rather than reconciled after the fact.
  • Lean forward vs. lean back AI
    Lean forward: a person actively drives and AI supports (appropriate when defining the problem). Lean back: a person lets AI do more of the work (appropriate once solutioning, after the problem is clear).
  • Idea vs. execution
    An 'idea' is the insight/product-discovery moment of correctly framing which problem to solve, drawn from synthesizing many (often tacit) data sources — a fundamentally human act. 'Execution' is building/prototyping/coding once the problem is clear — where AI excels.

Arguments

Teams should prompt together at key inflection points (especially problem-shaping) but can fan out individually once a shared direction is set — a converge/diverge/converge rhythm, not uniform individual or uniform collective AI use. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Parallel individual AI-chat use caused information overload and 'trouble' in the study.
  • One participant observed the team's collaborative capacity with AI felt greater than when everyone used it individually.
  • AI makes solo progress feel fast and sufficient, which crowds out the pre-AI habit of aligning together before splitting up.
Implicit premises:
  • Alignment skipped early resurfaces later as costlier rework or an unresolved 'debt to the collective' that compounds the longer it's deferred.
  • Not all AI use needs to be collective — only the decisions with the most downstream divergence risk (shaping, direction-setting) need the team in the same place.
Inferences:
  • The right default is a deliberate rhythm — align collectively at inflection points, diverge for individual exploration, reconverge to decide — rather than a fixed rule of always-together or always-alone.
  • Teams need an explicit mechanism (a written norm, a facilitated session) to identify which moments are inflection points, since the article does not otherwise specify how to recognize one in the moment.
As AI absorbs execution work, teams should rebalance membership toward including outside domain experts rather than remaining an insular PM/design/engineering unit. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI executes well but frames problems poorly.
  • The study's winning teams had diverse, non-technical perspectives (policy, lived experience, business model).
  • Removing the technical barrier to prototyping lets non-engineers build and show their own ideas directly, rather than needing an engineer to translate for them.
Implicit premises:
  • The main remaining bottleneck on team performance, once AI handles execution, is the breadth and quality of problem framing rather than implementation speed.
  • Adding outside experts does not introduce coordination costs large enough to offset the framing benefit they bring.
Inferences:
  • Teams in domains with concentrated external expertise (e.g., compliance-heavy or strongly marketing-driven domains) should consider staffing permanent experts into the team rather than only consulting them episodically.
  • This recommendation is in some tension with the article's own Theme 1: widening the group to more, more-heterogeneous contributors could reproduce the same parallel-work misalignment risk it just diagnosed for individual AI chats — a tension the article does not address.

Narratives

  • The opening vignette personifies a new AI teammate as 'chatty, diligent... always cheerful and eager to help. Works around the clock. Doesn't eat lunch... after "thought for 42s", sometimes hallucinates and goes off on a tangent.'
    → Frames AI as a social/team-dynamics question rather than a tooling question from the first line, and foreshadows the piece's conclusion that AI's quirks (unreliable judgment, no shared context with the rest of the team) are precisely why deliberate team practices — not just better prompting — are what determines the outcome.
  • The NYU 'buildathon': a 6-hour workshop where student teams tackled real New York City civic problems (grocery affordability, bike lane safety, childcare access) using AI tools at every stage, judged on solution quality and how effectively they used AI, with researchers observing, interviewing, and assessing the teams throughout.
    → Supplies the empirical ground for all three themes; functions evidentially rather than as mere illustration — every subsequent claim in the piece traces back to an observation or quote from this workshop.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What happens to team dynamics when AI teammates are introduced?
    Three themes: individual parallel AI use damages alignment; AI is strong at execution but weak at problem-framing; diversity of thought (not technical skill) predicts innovation quality.
  • ⭕ Is there a general rule for when AI use should be single-player versus multi-player, or lean-forward versus lean-back?
    No fixed rule is given — only a prompt for each team to 'facilitate a session' and write down its own principles — so how a team identifies the relevant inflection points in the moment is left unanswered.
  • ⭕ Does widening a team to include more, more-heterogeneous outside experts risk reproducing the same parallel-work misalignment problem the article diagnosed for individual AI use?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe evidentiary base is a single 6-hour student 'buildathon' on invented civic problems with ad hoc teams formed for the exercise — not a real, ongoing product team with history, roles, incentives, and accountability. The piece draws durable team-structure recommendations ('revisit norms of team structures,' 'consider staffing more permanent experts') from a one-day exercise without addressing whether the findings generalize to long-lived teams with sunk-cost dynamics and hierarchy that a one-day hackathon team doesn't have.
    Read next: Look for longitudinal studies or case studies of real, ongoing product teams adopting shared/multiplayer AI practices and outside-expert staffing, to see whether the buildathon findings hold up over time and under real organizational constraints.
  • incompleteThe piece recommends bringing more outside experts into the room to add diversity of thought, but never addresses the coordination/alignment cost of doing so — the same 'parallel work creates problems' dynamic it diagnosed for individual AI chats could plausibly recur with a wider, more heterogeneous group of contributors who have different working styles, tools, and incomplete shared context.
    Read next: Literature on cross-functional team size, coordination cost, and the trade-off between diversity-of-perspective gains and communication-overhead losses as group heterogeneity increases.
  • incompleteNo method is given for how a team recognizes, in the moment, which decisions are 'important inflection points' requiring multi-player prompting versus the many smaller decisions safe to delegate individually — the piece asserts teams should 'set aside time' to define this but offers no concrete heuristic or worked example.
    Read next: Pair/mob programming literature and decision-point frameworks (e.g., when to pair vs. work solo) that already address a structurally similar problem for human-only collaboration.
    closed bySoftware Teaming: A Mob Programming, Whole-Team Approach — The 'Pause for Clarity' chapter gives exactly the concrete, named heuristic that gap asked for — a short enumerable list of in-the-moment triggers (solution not working, can't articulate the idea, code getting messy, anyone requests clarity) for recognizing when a decision point needs full-team consultation rather than solo handling, for human-only collaboration.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • Genie Lessons: Nobody Wants Agents · g2 — Gives a concrete, workshop-tested sketch of multiplayer AI collaboration that the cited gap said Beck never provided: prompting together at defined inflection points (especially problem-shaping), explicit single-player-vs-multi-player team norms, and a lean-forward/lean-back distinction for how actively a human drives versus delegates. It remains partial — the sketch comes from a one-day student exercise, not real-time collaborative software or a sustained team, so it narrows rather than fully closes the gap.
