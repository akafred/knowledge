A year after Claude Code's launch, coding itself is — in Boris Cherny's own, carefully qualified words — 'largely solved' for the kind of programming he does, which has pushed both the bottleneck and the interesting work for Anthropic's Claude Code team upstream: from writing code to reviewing/prioritizing it, from engineering as a distinct discipline to a convergence of engineering/product/design, and from building for today's model to deliberately betting on, and designing for, the model that will exist six months from now.

Key terms

  • latent demand
    (Boris) The product-design principle of watching what users are already improvising a product into doing despite it not being designed for that, and building directly for it — extended by Boris beyond human users to models themselves: watching what a model tries to do on its own ('being on distribution') and designing minimal scaffolding around that.
  • underfunding
    (Boris) Deliberately assigning fewer people to a project than the work seems to need, as a management tactic — the resulting urgency and intrinsic drive to ship pushes engineers to delegate maximally to Claude rather than to ask for more headcount.
  • build for the model six months out
    (Boris) A deliberate product-strategy bet: design for a more capable future model rather than optimizing for the current model's limitations, accepting weak product-market fit in the interim, because scaffolding built around today's limitations is typically wiped out by the next model generation anyway.
  • plan mode Claude Code
    (Boris) Deliberately described as mechanically trivial: a single injected sentence telling the model not to write code yet, used for roughly 80% of Boris's own tasks as a lightweight checkpoint where the model proposes a plan before the human approves execution.
  • race to the top
    (Boris, internal Anthropic framing) Open-sourcing safety-relevant infrastructure (e.g. Claude Code's agent sandbox, made to work with any agent) so that competing labs and vendors adopt the same safety practice, framed as a deliberate competitive dynamic Anthropic wants to win by raising the industry's safety floor rather than by keeping a proprietary safety advantage.

Arguments

Teams building AI products should deliberately avoid over-scaffolding/constraining the model and should build for a future, more capable model rather than optimizing for the model that exists today. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Scaffolding typically yields only a 10-20% performance improvement.
  • That gain is usually wiped out by the next model generation.
  • The Claude Code team's own history (building for the model six months out, accepting weak early product-market fit) preceded large growth once the model caught up.
Implicit premises:
  • The rate of model capability improvement is fast and predictable enough to bet product strategy on it.
  • A product built around today's model limitations can become a liability once those limitations lift, not just a wasted but harmless investment.
Inferences:
  • Well-capitalized teams can afford to tolerate a long period of poor product-market fit while betting on a future model; smaller or cash-constrained teams, for whom near-term traction is existential, face an asymmetric version of this bet that the interview never addresses.
Review, not code generation, is now the central engineering bottleneck at Anthropic, and the practice that addresses it is extending AI-assisted review while still preserving a layer of human review, rather than removing human review entirely. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Claude reviews 100% of pull requests at Anthropic.
  • A human review layer still exists after that, for anything beyond pure throwaway prototype code.
  • Boris explicitly names 'who's going to review them all' as the open question once building became cheap.
Implicit premises:
  • Automating review at the Claude layer reduces, but does not eliminate, the need for a final human check.
  • The specific threshold for when human review can be skipped is left to individual engineer judgment, not a formalized policy described in the interview.
Inferences:
  • As more review work is pushed onto AI, the open design question becomes precisely which changes still require a human reviewer and which can be fully automated — a boundary the interview gestures at ('unless it's pure prototype code') but never formalizes.

Narratives

  • Boris, accustomed to debugging memory leaks himself via heap snapshots and a specialized debugger, watched a newer team member simply ask Claude Code to investigate a memory leak; Claude performed the same investigative steps Boris would have (took a heap snapshot, wrote a disposable just-in-time analysis tool, found the issue) and shipped a fix faster than Boris's own manual process.
    → Illustrates the 'mindset shift' cost Boris names explicitly — that even a deeply experienced practitioner defaults to old workflows and has to consciously 'transport himself to the current moment' — making this a concrete, personal instance of the skill/practice-obsolescence question running through the whole interview.
  • A reported historical interview with a 15th-century scribe, asked how they felt about the printing press, who said they were relieved to be freed from the tedious work of copying text between books so they could focus on the parts they actually enjoyed (illustrating/drawing art, bookbinding).
    → Boris uses this found anecdote as a direct analogy for his own stated relief at no longer doing 'the tedious part' of coding (git plumbing, dependency wrangling) in favor of 'what to build' — explicitly mapping his own emotional experience onto a five-centuries-old precedent to normalize and legitimate his own practice change.
  • A chain of 'latent demand' case studies: Facebook Marketplace emerging from the observation that ~40% of Facebook Groups posts were informal buying/selling; Facebook Dating emerging from the observation that 60% of profile views were between non-friends of the opposite gender; and Cowork emerging from watching non-engineers (a data scientist growing tomato plants, someone analyzing their genome, someone recovering wedding photos from a corrupted hard drive) improvise Claude Code via a bare terminal for non-coding tasks.
    → A sequence of analogous stories used to justify generalizing 'latent demand' from an established product-management heuristic into Boris's broader claim that the same logic applies to designing for models, not just for human users — the repetition across three unrelated products is the implicit argument for why the principle should be trusted.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Is coding, for an experienced practitioner like Boris, already a fully solved problem?
    Yes, for 'the kind of programming that I do' — but with an explicit caveat that full hands-off operation isn't yet safe at scale, since a human still needs to check correctness and safety, especially when many people are running the agent.
  • ✅ What is the next frontier once coding itself is solved?
    Branching into general, non-coding computer-use tasks (Cowork) and models that proactively generate their own ideas and prioritization from feedback/telemetry, rather than only executing assigned coding tasks.
  • ⭕ Why do designers, per Lenny's informal poll, report enjoying their jobs less since AI adoption at a notably higher rate (20%) than engineers or PMs (~10%)?
    Boris can only offer that Anthropic's own designers are an atypical, already-coding, technically-screened sample, and says he'd like to hear more from designers outside that context.
  • ⭕ If junior engineers no longer accumulate the 'reps' of manually reviewing and being reviewed by human colleagues (because Claude now reviews 100% of PRs first), how do they develop the judgment that manual code review used to teach, and does Boris's own team — self-selected, mission-driven, pre-screened for coding aptitude even in non-technical roles — generalize to a team built from a normal hiring pool?
