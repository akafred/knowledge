Forsvaret med egen data-grunnlov

Silje Rognsvåg · article · 2026-08-18 Reader 🖥️

Cloud Vendor Portability & Lock-in AvoidanceData Mesh & Data ProductsPublic-Sector Digitalization
The Norwegian Armed Forces are building a 'data constitution' (FDSA) that makes data — not the technology or vendor processing it — the stable core of digital infrastructure, so that suppliers can be swapped without disruption; Norway's Digitalization Minister argues this same data-centric logic should extend to the rest of the public sector.

Key terms

  • data-grunnlov (data constitution)
    An informal, constitution-like framing for FDSA — a foundational, durable set of data rules meant to outlast changes in technology and vendors, in contrast to an ordinary IT policy or architecture standard that gets revised alongside the tech stack.
  • Forsvarets datasentriske arkitektur (FDSA) / data-centric architecture
    An architecture where data is the stable, primary organizing unit — decoupled from the systems that produce or consume it, exposed via standardized interfaces and products, carrying its own security/access/sharing rules — while the technology and vendors sitting on top of the data remain freely replaceable.
  • utbyttbar (exchangeable)
    The property Markussen wants for technology specifically — swappable without disrupting the underlying data — paired against data, which he explicitly says should NOT be exchangeable/mutable in the same way (it should remain stable, standardized, and portable across whichever technology processes it).

Arguments

Forsvaret's (and by extension, other public-sector) digital architecture should be organized around durable, standardized, vendor-neutral data rather than around current technology or vendor choices. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Future decisive technology is unpredictable over a 5-10 year horizon.
  • Data can in principle be kept stable and standardized independent of whichever technology or vendor processes it.
  • Today's vendor lock-in happens because data — not just technology — gets bound to specific systems.
Implicit premises:
  • Decoupling data from application/vendor technology is technically achievable at the scale of a national defense organization working with NATO, NSM, and FFI — the article treats this as a design choice rather than an open engineering problem.
  • Vendor switchability is valuable enough (implicitly for operational resilience/beredskap) to justify the cost of building and enforcing a formal 'data constitution.'
  • An institution can self-impose a regulatory-style mandate (as the state once imposed number portability on telecom operators) and have it hold with comparable force, even without external legal compulsion.
Inferences:
  • If FDSA succeeds, today's dominant technology and cloud/AI vendors become substitutable within defense-relevant infrastructure — a geopolitically loaded implication (reduced dependence on incumbent, often foreign, vendors) that the article gestures at (NATO, NSM involvement; 'KI-modeller' as an example of unpredictable future tech) but never states directly.

Narratives

  • The number-portability analogy — being able to keep your phone number when switching telecom operator, which wasn't possible before the state mandated it of the industry.
    → Carries the article's central persuasive move: it recasts an abstract, hard-to-visualize architecture principle (data-centric, vendor-neutral infrastructure) into a concrete, universally-experienced precedent, making FDSA's vendor-independence goal feel both intuitive and achievable via regulation-style precedent rather than purely technical argument.
