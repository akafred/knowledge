The Orchestrator's Tax

Rahul Garg (martinfowler.com; edited/guided by Martin Fowler) · rss · 2026-07-28 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent Context Files (CLAUDE.md / AGENTS.md)Cognitive LocalityContext EngineeringContext Rot & Context LimitsHuman as Orchestrator of AgentsMulti-Agent OrchestrationRight-Sizing Boundaries & Primitives
The real value of a subagent is not the wall-clock time it saves through parallel execution but what it keeps out of the orchestrator's context, so multi-agent workflows should be designed and governed around protecting the orchestrator's limited working memory — via task splits that respect 'cognitive locality' and via minimal, fact-justified standing rules — rather than around maximizing delegation, agent count, or governance.

practical · mixed — Structured as a first-person incident narrative (a specific multi-agent coding session) that is progressively generalized into a theoretical claim (context as a competed-for, not just capacity-limited, resource) and then converted into practical prescriptions (four CLAUDE.md rules plus a meta-heuristic for writing future rules).

Key terms

  • Orchestrator Multi-Agent Orchestration
    The main AI session/thread that spawns and coordinates subagents — the only part of the system that accumulates understanding across a session (design rationale, constraints, prior trade-offs) and therefore the locus that must be protected.
  • Cognitive locality Cognitive Locality
    Tasks that require reconstructing the same mental model of a codebase (architecture, conventions, surrounding code) should be assigned to the same agent/context; splitting them by task rather than by the knowledge they require forces independent, duplicated reconstruction.
  • The orchestrator's tax (title term)
    The cost paid not by running subagents, but by the orchestrator itself when it lets low-value information (e.g. a raw background-agent transcript) into its own context — a cost that, unlike a one-time token bill, is carried forward and 're-charged' on every subsequent turn.
  • Standing instruction file (CLAUDE.md)
    Treated as itself a scarce, cumulative resource: 'every extra line in a standing instruction file is a cost paid again on every future session,' so a rule must justify its own recurring cost, not just its one-time correctness.
  • The 'one missing fact' heuristic
    A test for whether a discovered gap deserves a new standing rule: ask whether a reasonably competent orchestrator would make the right decision once it knew the single missing fact. If yes, state the fact. If the fix instead specifies a decision procedure (approvals, checkpoints, mandatory steps), that's a sign of encoding process where a small clarification would do.

Arguments

The primary benefit of subagents is protecting the orchestrator's context/working memory, not saving wall-clock time. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The duplicated-orientation cost of running four subagents was real but was not identified as the biggest cost.
  • The transcript-polling incident introduced tens of thousands of tokens of raw, noisy content that persisted across every subsequent turn.
  • A token bill is one-time, but context pollution taxes every future turn.
Implicit premises:
  • The orchestrator's downstream judgment quality depends on what currently occupies its context, not merely on whether the context window has remaining capacity.
  • Attention is a competitive resource, not just an additive one — more content in context degrades the model's ability to weigh what matters even when space remains.
Inferences:
  • If protecting orchestrator context is the real function of subagents, agent count and task splits should be sized by orientation/consolidation logic (cognitive locality) rather than by throughput or wall-clock goals — an inference the author draws explicitly in the following section.
A newly discovered gap should usually be encoded as a stated missing fact, not as a decision procedure (e.g. a confirmation gate).
Explicit premises:
  • A confirm-before-spawn gate was proposed after discovering that subagents don't inherit active skills.
  • The author found no evidence that bad spawn plans were actually slipping through for lack of a confirmation step.
  • A universal gate would add a round-trip cost to every similar future session and risks being rubber-stamped over time ('approving on autopilot').
Implicit premises:
  • Decision procedures (approvals, checkpoints) impose a recurring attention cost every time they fire, whereas a stated fact costs attention only when it's actually relevant.
  • The human can reliably distinguish, at the time of the incident, a 'missing fact' gap from a genuine 'recurring bad judgment' gap that would warrant a procedural safeguard.
Inferences:
  • Any candidate standing rule can be pre-tested by imagining a competent orchestrator that already knows the fact in question — if that alone would produce the right decision, a checkpoint is unnecessary.

Narratives

  • The opening incident: four subagents running against a .NET response-pipeline refactor start to feel harder to reason about than the code itself, prompting the author to stop and interrogate the workflow rather than the code.
    → Grounds the entire piece's generalizations in one concrete, present-tense session rather than abstract multi-agent theory; supplies the specific artifact (the transcript dump) that makes the 'pollution vs. parallelism' distinction legible rather than asserted in the abstract.
  • A status check ('check on the agents') triggers a tool call that imports a full raw background-agent transcript — tens of thousands of tokens of JSONL, reasoning, and tool output — into the main thread, and this happens twice.
    → Supplies the concrete mechanism behind the piece's central claim that context pollution, not duplication or parallelism, was the standout cost — and behind the broader claim that pollution is carried forward on every subsequent turn.
  • A later session reveals that subagents don't inherit skills active in the parent session; the author's first instinct is to add a universal confirm-before-spawn gate, but he abandons it after realizing there was no evidence of the underlying failure mode it would guard against.
    → Illustrates the 'one missing fact' heuristic in action (rather than stating it abstractly) and demonstrates the author correcting his own over-governance instinct.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What was the actual biggest cost of running four subagents in this session?
    Attributed to the orchestrator importing full raw agent transcripts via status polling — but the author explicitly flags this ranking as the orchestrator's own unmeasured self-report, not an instrumented finding.
  • ✅ Were four subagents simply too many?
    Explicitly abandoned as the wrong question rather than answered — the author reframes the inquiry around what belongs in the orchestrator's context instead of how many agents ran.
  • ✅ What should subagents actually be used for, if not primarily to save time?
    To isolate noisy intermediate reasoning from the orchestrator's context and to respect cognitive locality when splitting work.
  • ⭕ How much governance (confirmation gates, mandatory checkpoints) should be layered onto an agent workflow?
    Explicitly left open: "I don't have a settled view of how much governance is enough, and I don't think this piece earns one."
  • ⭕ How should one measure whether the orchestrator's own self-critique of a session is trustworthy?
    Named as an open question in the closing list rather than answered.
  • ⭕ What decision procedure lets an orchestrator recognize 'cognitive locality' — i.e., detect before spawning that two tasks will require reconstructing the same mental model — so it can act on rules 1 and 4?
    Not addressed; the piece supplies the diagnosis after the fact (in the incident review) but not a method for making it prospectively.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe four standing rules use specific numeric thresholds ('two to four agents,' 'five as a consolidation signal') that the author explicitly says were calibrated to his own workflow and to one model (Claude Sonnet 5) on one codebase, and disclaims as universal constants — yet the piece gives no method for a different reader to derive their own thresholds, only the instruction to 'modify and optimize.'
    Read next: Look for calibration methods rather than fixed numbers — e.g. Osmani's alternative anchor ('the correct number of parallel agents equals the number you can code-review properly') ties the threshold to a measurable capacity rather than to incident review alone.
  • incompleteThe piece never specifies the actual decision procedure an orchestrator should run to detect 'cognitive locality' before spawning agents — i.e. how it recognizes, in advance, that two tasks will require reconstructing the same mental model of a codebase. Rules 1 and 4 (prefer 2-4 agents; treat overlapping file ownership as a consolidation signal) assume this diagnosis is already available; they don't produce it, and file overlap is only a weak proxy for shared mental-model requirements (two tasks can need the same understanding without touching the same files, or touch the same files with genuinely independent context needs).
    Read next: This is the exact gap the task brief flagged and the piece's most productive incompleteness: look for task-decomposition or dependency-analysis frameworks that could operationalize 'shared mental model' as a measurable property rather than a post-hoc diagnosis (e.g. right-sizing-boundaries / chunking-context literature, or explicit action-complexity/context-complexity rubrics).
  • illogicalThe piece states once, explicitly, that its central ranking claim (transcript-polling as the largest cost) is 'the orchestrator grading its own mistake' and 'not a measured fact' — but the same self-report is then treated as reliable enough to justify permanent CLAUDE.md rules and to found the article's closing thesis about a 'third thing worth watching' in agent workflows. The epistemic caveat is stated once and not carried through the argument's confidence level for the rest of the piece.
    Read next: Compare against pieces that build instrumented (not self-reported) cost accounting for agent sessions, to see whether the transcript-polling cost actually dominates duplication cost once measured rather than inferred.
    closed byMy AI Kept Pushing Me to Ship, So I Asked It Why — Fowler's piece flags that it treats an orchestrator's self-graded, unmeasured claim as reliable enough to justify permanent CLAUDE.md rules without carrying its own stated epistemic caveat through the argument. This article builds and demonstrates exactly the missing discipline: it structurally separates a model's checkable self-report of behavior (trustworthy) from its unreliable self-report of reasons (a confabulated story per Turpin et al.), and validates the resulting standing rule via an independent, fresh-context second opinion rather than trusting the original session's self-grading.
  • incompleteThe 'one missing fact' heuristic (state a fact, don't prescribe a procedure) is itself applied inconsistently within the essay: rule 3 ('do not allow repository-wide git operations inside concurrent agent prompts') is a decision procedure/hard constraint, not a stated fact, yet it is presented alongside three fact-like rules without acknowledging that some gaps (systemic/safety risks) may genuinely warrant a rule-as-procedure rather than a rule-as-fact, undermining the heuristic's claimed generality.
    Read next: Look for a taxonomy distinguishing 'missing fact' gaps from 'systemic risk' gaps that would justify when a hard constraint (vs. a stated fact) is the right form for a standing rule.
  • uninformedThe piece arrives at its fleet-sizing and context-budget numbers from a single incident without engaging existing multi-agent/harness literature that already proposes related numeric or structural heuristics — e.g. Osmani's 'correct number of parallel agents equals the number you can review' (tied to human review capacity rather than orchestrator context) or Zuill/Herr's practice of keeping an agent's context window around half-full by moving detail into on-demand skill files.
    Read next: extract-osmani-orchestration-tax-is-you and extract-software-teaming-age-of-ai-zuill-herr, both already in the corpus, offer adjacent-but-distinct anchors for the same class of threshold.
