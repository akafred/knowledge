Podcast: How Will We Train Developers If AI Does the Routine Work: A Conversation with Scott Hanselman

Michael Stiefel (host) with Scott Hanselman (guest, VP, Microsoft/GitHub) · podcast (InfoQ Architects Podcast; Reader category: rss) · 2026-09-14 Reader 🖥️

The software industry must institutionalize a formal, metriced 'preceptor' role -- modeled on nursing's dedicated teaching-nurse system -- whose explicit job is to grow junior engineers into seniors, because AI has absorbed the routine, low-stakes toil junior developers used to learn from, and without deliberate, funded intervention the industry's short-term hyper-optimization will hollow out the senior-engineer pipeline within a generation.

practical · mixed — Primarily practical/argumentative: the conversation argues the industry ought to adopt a specific organizational mechanism (the preceptor role). It is carried substantially by narrative (the god-object anecdote, the nursing analogy, the wax-on-wax-off metaphor, the seed-corn analogy) rather than abstract argument, and closes with an expository personal-reflection segment (the Architect's Questionnaire) that is mostly color rather than thesis-bearing.

Key terms

  • preceptor / preceptorship model
    An explicit, formally designated senior-engineer role -- borrowed directly from nursing's teaching-nurse system -- whose job performance is evaluated on how well they train and produce new senior engineers, not on how much code they personally ship or how much they resemble a manager or tech lead.
  • wax on, wax off
    The Karate Kid metaphor for the tedious, repetitive, low-stakes toil junior engineers were traditionally assigned; Hanselman's point is that this toil was not incidental busywork but the actual mechanism through which craft skill was absorbed.
  • engineering thrive / time to first PR
    A deliberately non-productivity metric tracking how a new hire feels about landing their first pull request, used as a signal of personal growth and enablement rather than output.
  • the third place
    The informal social space distinct from work and home; Hanselman's claim is that remote work, social media, and AI collapse work, home, and the third place into one undifferentiated place, eliminating a developmental resource.
  • architecture (vs. features)
    The big-picture, systemic design judgment distinct from generating working feature code; Hanselman's specific claim is that AI is 'myopic' and characteristically bad at this, evidenced by AI accumulating an undisciplined 'god object' in his own current project.
  • too agile
    Hanselman's claim that AI has not killed agile but has taken its fast inner-loop/outer-loop philosophy to an unstable extreme, where speed without discipline causes the work to 'wobble' and risk flying off the road.

Arguments

The industry needs a formally named, specifically evaluated preceptor role rather than relying on informal mentorship.
Explicit premises:
  • Junior engineers used to learn through routine toil that AI now performs.
  • Nursing solved an analogous training problem by creating a dedicated, metriced preceptor role, separate from general nursing duties, whose reviews are based on whether they can mint new senior nurses.
Implicit premises:
  • A teaching role only reliably functions when it is formally named, owned, and measured -- the informal mentor/tech-lead model failed for lack of this accountability structure, not for lack of senior willingness to teach.
  • What worked for nursing (a regulated, licensure-driven profession) will transfer to software engineering, which has neither licensure nor an equivalent regulatory forcing function.
Inferences:
  • If preceptor reviews genuinely reward 'minting new seniors' over shipped code, this creates a structural conflict with the incentives (velocity, ship dates, headcount efficiency) that typically govern engineering-manager and tech-lead performance -- a tension the conversation never surfaces or resolves.
Removing junior 'toil' work via AI threatens to collapse the pipeline that produces senior engineers. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Senior engineers currently emerge from junior engineers who performed the toil.
  • AI is now performing that toil.
Implicit premises:
  • There is no alternative mechanism (deliberate practice, synthetic exercises, structured pairing) that reproduces the same tacit learning as doing real, low-stakes work under real stakes.
  • The causal link between 'did the toil' and 'became senior' is necessary, not merely one contingent historical path among several (e.g. project-based learning, pairing, or classroom instruction might substitute).
Inferences:
  • If the mechanism really is toil-dependent, deliberately reintroducing engineered toil or adversarial practice scenarios (what elsewhere in the corpus is called 'synthetic apprenticeship') would be a candidate substitute -- Hanselman never proposes or engages this possibility, instead reaching directly for the preceptor/pairing model.
AI-heavy development workflows still require dedicated human architectural review. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Hanselman's own current project accumulated a 'god object' over several weeks of AI-assisted development.
Implicit premises:
  • A single practitioner's own anecdote, on one project, over a few weeks, generalizes to AI's architectural capability broadly.
Inferences:
  • This implies architecture-level review needs a deliberate, dedicated checkpoint in any AI-heavy workflow -- consistent with, but logically distinct from, the preceptor argument (a checkpoint for code quality is not the same claim as a role for training people).

Narratives

  • The 'god object' anecdote: over several weeks, AI assistance on Hanselman's current project accreted an undisciplined super-struct he now has to remove before it 'collapses under its own weight.'
    → Makes the abstract claim 'AI performs poorly at architecture' concrete and personally credible -- he is not speaking hypothetically, he is dealing with it as he records the podcast.
  • The nursing-preceptor analogy: Hanselman's wife is a nurse preceptor, and nursing has a formal, metriced teaching-nurse role distinct from general nursing.
    → Supplies the entire structural template for the central policy proposal, letting Hanselman carry the argument by borrowed institutional design rather than abstract organizational theory.
  • 'Wax on, wax off' (Karate Kid).
    → Reframes tedious junior work as pedagogically load-bearing rather than wasteful, making the loss of that work feel like a real deprivation rather than a pure productivity gain.
  • The seed-corn farmer analogy, offered by Stiefel and enthusiastically endorsed by Hanselman ('the analogies are legion').
    → Reinforces the intergenerational-capital framing: consuming today's training material forecloses tomorrow's harvest of senior engineers.
  • Hanselman drives to Seattle every four to six weeks 'to make sure that my badge still works and that the young people are still present,' despite being fully remote his entire Microsoft career.
    → Demonstrates he personally practices the anti-isolation prescription he argues for, giving the claim about in-person value personal credibility rather than being purely prescriptive.
  • Personal maker anecdotes: the 3D-printed garage-door button cover, the DIY Peloton alternative ('PedalScape'), and the 30-year-old Visual Basic app still running at Pioneer Courthouse Square.
    → Establishes Hanselman's long-term maker/teacher identity and his valuing of software that 'holds up under pressure' for decades -- retroactively explains why he cares so much about training successors rather than just shipping features.
  • The Y2K joke: 'they're going to go and pull me out of retirement in 2035 to go and fix the next big date bug.'
    → Dark-humor illustration of the concrete cost of pipeline collapse -- an industry forced to summon retirees because it failed to grow successors, foreshadowing a repeat of a known historical failure mode.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How will the next generation of software engineers be trained if AI absorbs the work juniors used to learn from?
    Adopt a formal preceptor role, evaluated on mentee growth rather than shipped code, modeled on nursing.
  • ✅ What should an early-career engineer's on-ramp look like when 'hitting the ground running' is not realistic?
    Track 'engineering thrive' (time-to-first-PR) as a morale/growth metric, and insert deliberate, conscious growth moments at every step rather than assuming AI-mediated speed substitutes for development.
  • ✅ Does remote work help or hurt mentorship?
    Hurts, in Hanselman's view -- though he says it can be 'pushed through' with deliberate metacognitive effort and periodic in-person contact.
  • ✅ Has AI killed agile?
    No -- but it has driven agile's fast-loop philosophy to an unstable extreme ('too agile') that requires deliberate discipline (Kanban, user stories, a real SDLC) to keep from destabilizing.
  • ✅ Is AI the cause of the documented decline in entry-level hiring since 2017?
    No -- Hanselman attributes it mainly to pre-existing corporate short-termism and 'hyper optimization,' with AI cast as an accelerant or scapegoat rather than the root cause.
  • ⭕ What are the psychological/sociological costs of AI-mediated work?
    Named (loneliness, loss of flow state, anthropomorphization risk, the collapsed 'third place') but not systematically addressed -- the only concrete remedies offered are the preceptor program and Hanselman's personal practice of periodic in-person travel.

Gaps

  • illogicalHanselman argues both that AI is centrally responsible for eliminating the junior-training on-ramp (the entire premise motivating the preceptor proposal) and that 'AI is not going to take your job -- the hyperoptimization of some C-suite executive is going to take your job,' attributing the pipeline collapse mainly to short-termism rather than AI. These two framings are never reconciled: is AI the proximate mechanism, or merely the current instrument of a short-termism that predates it? The conversation never specifies what a hyperoptimizing company without AI would have done to the junior pipeline, so the counterfactual attribution is left unexamined.
    Read next: Look for empirical work separating pre-2017 entry-level hiring decline trends from the post-ChatGPT AI-coding-adoption period, to test whether AI is additive to or merely relabeling a pre-existing trend.
  • incompleteThe preceptor proposal is evaluated only on the promise that preceptors will be 'metriced' on 'how well they train people,' but no metric is actually specified beyond the anecdotal claim that Microsoft's 'first cohort of preceptors... has been very successful' and the qualitative 'engineering thrive'/time-to-first-PR framing, which is itself explicitly described as a morale signal, not a competency signal. There is no description of what a preceptor's performance review actually measures when it comes time to say a trainer 'shouldn't be a trainer.'
    Read next: extract-hbr-mentoring-matters-ai-era's more specified model (structured cadence, explicit competency goals, a written one-page agreement) offers a concrete alternative worth comparing against Hanselman's still-informal 'it's been very successful.'
  • incompleteHanselman never addresses whether the preceptorship model as described (pairing, fellowship, code review) actually transmits architectural judgment -- the very skill he says AI cannot do -- or only reproduces the narrower skills the old toil-based system produced. If AI is already doing entry-level toil, and preceptors are pairing/reviewing rather than assigning toil, it is unclear preceptors reproduce the 'wax on, wax off' mechanism he says was essential; this is a gap between his own diagnosis (what created seniors before) and his own prescription (what he proposes now), which may be a different learning mechanism entirely.
    Read next: Look for accounts of what specifically preceptor/pairing sessions consist of at Microsoft's pilot -- do trainees do hands-on toil under supervision, or primarily observe and discuss AI-generated diffs?
  • incompleteNo engagement with organizations that lack Microsoft's scale, cash, or executive sponsorship -- a resource-constrained startup, a non-tech company's small engineering team, or a highly outsourced/offshore engineering org has no obvious path to implementing preceptorship as described (400-500 person 'shards,' formal cohorts, explicit executive buy-in).
  • incompleteThe 'third place' collapse and anthropomorphization-of-AI risks are raised as real sociological costs, but Hanselman offers no organizational remedy beyond his own personal practice (periodic travel to Seattle); there is no discussion of what fully distributed, no-office companies could do instead, nor any engagement with whether his prescription (in-person contact) is feasible for organizations that chose remote-first specifically to access geographically dispersed talent.
