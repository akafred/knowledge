Key terms

  • Operational context
    The combination of process data, business knowledge, and decision intelligence that gives AI a real-time, proprietary understanding of how a specific enterprise actually functions (as distinct from generic, public-internet-derived context).
  • RoAI (Return on AI)
    The as-yet-unrealized business value/ROI enterprises expect from AI investment, framed as the open question the whole industry is now asking after the pilot-hype phase.
  • Generalist vs. specialist AI
    A framing device: out-of-the-box AI models are 'generalists' (fluent but generic); AI grounded in a business's operational context becomes a 'specialist' capable of enterprise-specific judgment calls.

Arguments

Enterprises should adopt an operational-context layer (i.e., a platform like Celonis's) before or alongside AI deployment in order to realize AI ROI at scale.
Explicit premises:
  • Generic AI models produce generic, low-value answers to enterprise-specific questions.
  • Enterprise data needed for specificity is proprietary, private, and fragmented.
  • Operational context (process data + business knowledge + decision intelligence) closes that gap and produces specific, actionable, trustworthy answers (illustrated by the supply-chain reorder example).
  • Companies that have adopted operational-context tooling (Fujitsu, Novo Nordisk) report strong results.
Implicit premises:
  • A vendor's own selected customer case studies, without disclosed methodology or independent verification, constitute sufficient evidence that a general causal claim ('operational context → ROI') holds broadly, not just for these two accounts.
  • The specific, richly-detailed 'good' AI answer in the demonstration dialogue is achievable in practice at the fidelity shown, rather than being an idealized illustration constructed for persuasive effect.
  • The article's own vendor (Celonis) is representative of, or equivalent to, 'operational context' in general — i.e., the argument for the general concept doubles as an argument for the specific product.
Inferences:
  • If operational context is genuinely the binding constraint, then AI vendors and enterprises should be able to produce independently verified, apples-to-apples RoAI comparisons (with vs. without operational-context tooling) rather than relying on anecdote — the article does not attempt this.

Narratives

  • A hypothetical exchange where a manager asks an out-of-the-box AI model about a demand spike and reordering; without operational context the AI gives a vague hedge, but with operational context it names a specific region, SKU, stockout risk percentage, lead times, and a recommended action (authorize air freight).
    → Makes the abstract 'generalist vs. specialist' proposition vivid and concrete — shows rather than argues for the value of operational context.
  • Fujitsu case: using operational context for inventory management, achieving a reported 20% reduction in excess inventory and multimillion-dollar savings across 10 processes.
    → Supplies a quantified, named-company proof point for the article's central claim.
  • Novo Nordisk case: using operational context and 100+ AI agents to speed clinical development, with an executive quote about needing a 'digital twin' of the operation, expecting a 12-month reduction in time to market.
    → Supplies a second, higher-stakes (pharma) proof point and a customer voice endorsing the underlying premise.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why are enterprises not seeing the AI returns (RoAI) that early hype promised, despite near-universal AI adoption?
    Because AI models are generalists lacking the enterprise's proprietary operational context, so they can't make reliable, specific, trustworthy decisions at scale.
  • ⭕ How would one independently verify that 'operational context' (as opposed to other factors — organizational readiness, change management, data quality generally, model capability improvements over time) is the binding constraint on AI ROI, rather than just a plausible-sounding, commercially convenient explanation?
    Not addressed — the article treats its causal claim as established via anecdote and framing rather than testing it against alternative explanations.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article presents exactly two customer stories (Fujitsu, Novo Nordisk), both sourced from Celonis's own case-study pages with UTM referral tracking, as evidence for a general causal claim about operational context driving AI ROI. No independent benchmark, third-party audit, control group, or disclosed methodology accompanies either figure (20% inventory reduction, 12-month timeline reduction, 100+ agents). This is a textbook vendor self-reported metrics pattern, and per the reader's stated preference for benchmark data and practitioner experience over vendor claims, this piece should be read as marketing framing with a real (if unoriginal) causal intuition, not as rigorous analysis.
    Read next: Look for independent, third-party research on enterprise AI ROI drivers (e.g. academic studies, analyst firm reports without vendor sponsorship, or practitioner postmortems) that isolate 'context/data quality' from other commonly cited blockers like organizational readiness, change management, and governance.
  • incompleteThe article never engages with competing or complementary explanations for why '90% use AI but only 10% scale it successfully' — e.g. organizational readiness, unclear ownership, change-management failure, poor evals/feedback loops, or unrealistic expectations set by leadership — all of which appear elsewhere in this corpus as major explanatory factors for enterprise AI adoption stalling. By attributing the gap almost entirely to a missing operational-context layer (conveniently the sponsor's product category), the piece is incomplete about the multi-causal nature of the 90%-vs-10% gap.
