Building an Autonomous Enterprise for Real-World Services with Netic Founder Melisa Tokmak

No Priors (hosts Sarah Guo and Elad Gil, guest Melisa Tokmak) · podcast · 2026-08-01 Reader 🖥️

AI-Native CompanyEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessGrit (Passion & Perseverance)Hiring Signal DesignAgencyMoats & DefensibilityVendor Self-Reported Metrics
Real-world essential-service businesses (HVAC, plumbing, roofing, hospitality, pet care) should be run as an 'autonomous enterprise' — AI agents handling the full customer-facing and operational decision loop while humans remain only for hands-on service delivery — built as one compounding, multi-tenant product rather than assembled company-by-company through an AI roll-up, because durable advantage in these industries comes from operational depth across the model, orchestration/harness, and product layers together, which neither near-term robotics nor frontier AI labs are positioned to replicate.

practical · mixed — A VC-podcast founder interview: argumentative (defends a specific strategic choice — product platform vs. AI roll-up — and dismisses two competitive threats) interleaved with expository description of the business and narrative anecdotes (the -20°F outage, her origin story, a new hire's interview answer) that carry the argument implicitly.

Key terms

  • Netic-first / N1
    A customer whose very first interaction with the essential-service business is with a Netic AI agent rather than a human — her metric for AI-native adoption depth.
  • Autonomous enterprise
    An essential-service company where every function except the actual hands-on service labor itself — intake, diagnosis, routing, revenue generation, customer engagement — runs autonomously on AI.
  • Essential services
    A deliberately broad category: billion-dollar home-services companies (HVAC, plumbing, electric), consumer wellness/membership businesses, hospitality, automotive, and pet services — large, high-volume, real-world businesses distinguished from tech-serving-tech startups.
  • AI roll-up
    Elad's term, used as the explicit foil throughout: a firm (e.g. Long Lake) buying operating businesses in a vertical and optimizing them with AI, as opposed to Netic's single scalable product sold across many independently owned operators.
  • The three layers
    Her framework for where competitive defensibility lives in an AI-for-real-world-services product: the underlying model, the harness/orchestration built on top of it, and the product/software layer — all three, not any one alone, and specifically not the model.
  • Permanent underclass mentality / 'AGI pill' view
    A critical label for a mindset (which she attributes especially to Gen Z candidates) that treats the next 18–36 months as existentially decisive — believing that if you don't make money or learn everything now, AI will permanently erase your economic value.

Arguments

Building one scalable product platform for the essential-services vertical is a better strategy than acquiring and running individual operating businesses (an AI roll-up). strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Her skill set and mission fit building a product, not doing M&A.
  • A product built to optimize one acquired company doesn't generalize to other companies in the same industry.
  • She wants to serve every real-world business in the category, not a fixed portfolio of owned assets.
Implicit premises:
  • Product-market depth compounds faster across many customers than deal-by-deal operational improvement inside a fixed set of owned businesses.
  • The addressable market is large enough that platformizing the whole vertical is a strictly bigger opportunity than owning a portfolio within it.
Inferences:
  • If compounding product depth is the real lever, a roll-up's returns are capped by the number and quality of deals it can close and operate, while a platform's returns scale with the size of the whole addressable vertical — an inference she gestures at ('scale of the product') but never states explicitly.
Frontier AI labs are not a competitive threat to Netic. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Labs iterate and kill products fast; enterprises in these industries want durability instead.
  • The enterprise vertical is fragmented (~20 competing point solutions), unlike the labs' consolidated consumer coding-agent race.
  • Labs' research incentive is to solve problems in the most generalizable way possible ('wait for AGI'), which is operationally and intellectually lazy for a problem like essential services.
  • Defensibility requires 'last mile' harness/orchestration/product work the labs aren't positioned or incentivized to do.
Implicit premises:
  • Labs will keep prioritizing general-purpose model and product research over building deep, narrow, operationally embedded vertical products.
  • Enterprises' current preference for durability over rapid iteration will persist even as lab products mature.
  • The vertical's current fragmentation is a stable structural feature, not simply an unclaimed opportunity a lab or well-funded incumbent could still seize.

  • A large roofing company that previously sent door-knockers neighborhood-to-neighborhood now has Netic connect satellite hurricane/roof-material data into its agents, so the same platform both handles inbound/outbound conversations and proactively identifies which neighborhoods to target.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How should an AI company serve fragmented, operationally complex real-world service industries — as a product platform, or by buying and optimizing existing operators (a roll-up)?
    Build a single compounding, multi-tenant product platform: it fits her skill set, generalizes across customers, and isn't capped by deal flow the way a roll-up is.
  • ✅ Will physical robotics displace this human-plus-AI labor model?
    Not in the current 'chapter' — dexterity limits, building/home variation, and the emotional stakes of the interactions push robotics into a future, separate chapter.
  • ✅ Are frontier AI labs a competitive threat to vertical AI-for-real-world-services companies?
    No, in her view — labs are focused on generalizable model/product progress and fast iteration, not the durable, operationally embedded 'last mile' work these industries need.
  • ⭕ Is private equity's posture toward AI still purely cost-cutting, or has it genuinely shifted toward value creation?
    She asserts the industry playbook and rhetoric have shifted toward wanting real ROI and net-new revenue, but also states PE conversations 'always' start focused on cost-cutting and that she has to actively redirect them — leaving the actual extent of the shift unresolved.
  • ✅ What screens reliably for high-performing hires at an AI-native company?
    Demonstrated, sustained agency/follow-through across a candidate's whole life, probed through open-ended 'hardest thing you've done' interviewing rather than a fixed rubric or credential check.
