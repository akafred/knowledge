Optimizing teams: A dual perspective on Team Topologies

Elias Lieberich · article · 2024-09-26 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Team Topologies' four-type model is a useful starting vocabulary, but both authors argue that applying it well requires practical, product-aware judgment calls it doesn't itself supply — Lieberich's engineering-facing checklist (well-defined scope, disambiguated boundaries, minimal dependencies) and Wille's product-facing one (small teams, end-to-end ownership of a user journey, splitting by product/user-group/value-moment/funnel-stage rather than by technology) — because the book's own categories never fully resonated with a product-management, rather than engineering, view of where team boundaries should fall.

practical · mixed — Two linked essays (product coaches Elias Lieberich and Petra Wille, published together by Mind the Product) each arguing for how to apply/adapt the Team Topologies framework in practice — expository summary of the model followed by prescriptive, checklist-style advice. Treated as one document per the assigned slug/metadata; the second author's distinct voice is flagged throughout.

Key terms

  • well-defined scope
    Lieberich: a team's problem space should be narrow and intriguing enough that engineers can stay engaged with it for long periods, which he claims is the actual mechanism behind 10x-value teams — not org-chart position.
  • moments of value creation
    Wille: end-to-end slices of a user journey (e.g., job-searching, bookmarking, applying) used as a splitting dimension once product- and user-group-based splits are exhausted — distinct from a technical fracture plane.
  • don't ship your org chart
    Wille's anti-pattern label: designing product teams to mirror the existing organizational reporting structure rather than product/user needs, which she claims causes inefficiency and silos regardless of intent.
  • Team Topologies' four types (as critiqued, not merely summarized)
    Both authors treat stream-aligned/enabling/complicated-subsystem/platform as a given vocabulary, but Wille explicitly states it 'never fully resonated' with her because it is framed from an engineering rather than product-management perspective.

Propositions

  • explicitTeams with a well-designed topology deliver roughly an order of magnitude ('10x') more value than otherwise-comparable teams, regardless of their formal reporting lines.conf: low
  • explicitReorganizing how teams work together is costly, so a team topology should only be changed when there is clear evidence the current structure isn't working (e.g. huge steering-group decisions, engineering as a detached black box, product managers needing to request engineering resources, low motivation/high churn).conf: high
  • explicitA team's sustained engagement and resulting productivity depend more on having a narrow, intriguing, well-bounded problem space than on org-chart position or reporting structure.conf: medium
  • explicitSplitting teams by product line (e.g. 'Team Pants') or by abstract business goal (e.g. 'Retention team') without a genuinely bounded, solvable problem produces teams that must touch the whole system and fight each other for shared resources (e.g. UI real estate).conf: medium
  • explicitTeam Topologies' four-type taxonomy, because it is framed from an engineering perspective, does not fully resonate as a tool for product-management-led team design.conf: high

  • explicitTeams should be kept small — ideally no more than eight to ten people — and claims of needing more (e.g. regulatory compliance) should be challenged rather than accepted as a structural excuse.conf: high
    supportsTeam Topologies: Organizing Business and Technology Teams for Fast Flow · p13 — Wille's hard '8-10 person' team-size ceiling independently converges with Team Topologies' Dunbar's-number-grounded argument that team size/architecture must respect limits on stable human-group interaction.
  • explicitA product team should have end-to-end responsibility for a complete piece of the user journey (e.g. acquisition through onboarding) rather than a horizontal technical slice, to promote accountability and avoid silos.conf: high
    extendsTeam Topologies: Organizing Business and Technology Teams for Fast Flow · p12 — Wille's 'end-to-end responsibility for a part of the user journey' is a product-first version of Team Topologies' fracture-plane principle (boundaries chosen to match what a team can hold at acceptable cognitive load) — reframed around user-journey slices rather than technical/cognitive-load boundaries.
  • explicitTeams should minimize dependence on other teams for discovery, delivery, and release, because frequent mutual blocking is itself a signal the structure needs reassessment.conf: high

Arguments

Team topology should be judged and designed primarily by whether it gives each team a genuinely solvable, bounded problem — not by which named category (stream-aligned, platform, etc.) it formally belongs to. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Engineers' motivation and productivity track directly with having an intriguing, well-bounded problem space.
  • Teams split by product line or abstract business goal without a bounded problem end up needing to touch the whole system, producing friction and resource conflict rather than focus.
Implicit premises:
  • A team can satisfy the formal definition of a topology type (e.g. 'stream-aligned') while still lacking a genuinely bounded, solvable problem — the taxonomy's labels don't guarantee the underlying property that actually matters.
  • Engineer motivation is treated as the primary causal driver of the claimed 10x value gap, rather than, e.g., technical architecture quality, leadership quality, or market conditions, any of which could confound the anecdote.
Inferences:
  • A naive application of the four-type taxonomy (labeling teams correctly) could still fail to produce good outcomes if scope-boundedness isn't separately checked.
Team topology design should start from product/user-journey dimensions (product, user group, value-moment, funnel stage) rather than from Team Topologies' engineering-oriented four-type taxonomy, when the person doing the designing is in a product-management role. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The book's four-type framing is engineering-oriented and 'never fully resonated' from a product-management perspective.
  • A small, better-fitting set of dimensions (product, user group, user-journey moment, funnel stage) maps directly onto what a product leader needs to reason about: who the team serves and what slice of value they own.
Implicit premises:
  • The underlying goals Team Topologies pursues (fast flow, minimal cross-team dependency, clear ownership) are compatible with — and perhaps better served by — a product/user-journey-first decomposition than by the book's engineering-first categories, i.e. these are two routes to similar ends rather than competing goals.
  • A product leader's native vocabulary (user group, funnel, value moment) is treated as self-evidently more actionable for team design than an engineering vocabulary (subsystem, platform), without engaging the case where technical cohesion genuinely requires a complicated-subsystem or platform team regardless of product framing.
Inferences:
  • If product- and engineering-oriented decompositions of the same system disagree (e.g. a 'pricing and packaging engine' Wille or Lieberich would carve as one subsystem-type team might cut across the user-journey splits Wille recommends), the two authors' frameworks could produce conflicting team boundaries for the same organization — a tension neither essay resolves explicitly.

Narratives

  • A worked example of a booking engine (airline/travel/fashion retailer) carved into a stream-aligned 'User buying flows' team, a subsystem/enabling 'Pricing and packing engine' team, and a platform 'Base data' team, with a cross-cutting leadership team.
    → Supports Lieberich's well-defined-scope and disambiguated-boundaries propositions by showing a concrete, non-product-line, non-business-goal decomposition — and (unremarked by either author) itself contains a complicated-subsystem-style team that cuts across the user journey, in tension with Wille's purely product-journey-based splitting dimensions.
  • A job-board example illustrating Wille's four splitting dimensions (product, user group — employers vs. job seekers, value moments — searching/bookmarking/applying, funnel stages).
    → Supports the proposition that product/user-journey dimensions, applied in sequence as a company scales, give a workable alternative to a purely engineering-first topology.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How should a product/engineering organization decide when and how to restructure its teams?
    Only when clear dysfunction signals appear (per Lieberich); then slice by bounded, intriguing problem scope with disambiguated cross-team boundaries (Lieberich), or by product → user group → value moment → funnel stage (Wille), while avoiding org-chart-mirroring and pure-technology splits.
  • ✅ Does the Team Topologies four-type taxonomy serve product managers as well as it serves engineering leaders?
    No, according to Wille — it 'never fully resonated' from a product-management perspective, and she substitutes her own product-journey-based rules and dimensions instead.
  • ⭕ What happens when product-journey-based team boundaries and genuinely technical (subsystem/platform) boundaries disagree about where to cut the system?
    Not addressed — both authors' own worked examples include a subsystem/platform-style team, but neither discusses how to reconcile that with a pure user-journey or user-group decomposition when the two disagree.

Gaps

  • illogicalWille's claim that Team Topologies' four-type model doesn't resonate from a product perspective, paired with her own worked example containing a complicated-subsystem/platform-style team (the job board's shared 'base data' or pricing logic, by analogy to Lieberich's booking-engine example), suggests her rejection of the taxonomy may be more about vocabulary/framing than about the underlying need the taxonomy is tracking (that some real boundaries are technical, not product-journey-shaped) — a tension the piece doesn't surface or resolve.

  • uninformedThe headline '10x value' claim for well-topologized teams is presented as personal observation ('I have been on these teams a few times') with no comparison group, metric definition, or controlled attribution — exactly the kind of practitioner-anecdote-as-data this reader's persona is skeptical of (wants benchmark data over vendor/anecdotal claims), and strikingly similar in rhetorical shape to AI-adoption 10x productivity claims made elsewhere in this corpus (see extract-latent-space-yegge-vibe-coding-manifesto#p2) without either source examining whether the two '10x' claims share, confound, or are independent of each other.
    Read next: Look for controlled or larger-sample studies isolating team-topology quality as a productivity variable, independent of the AI-adoption productivity claims appearing elsewhere in the corpus.
  • incompleteNeither author considers how their prescriptions would need to change if AI coding agents substantially reduce the human execution work a team does — e.g. whether 'ideally 8-10 people' and 'end-to-end responsibility for a part of the user journey' still hold when a 2-3-person team plus agents can cover what used to require a larger team (a question other, more recent corpus documents engage directly).
