Presentation: From Retrieval to Reasoning: Building Production-Ready Agentic AI Systems with Knowledge Graphs

Cassie Shum · rss · 2026-09-12 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent ObservabilityArchitecture Decision RecordsContext EngineeringKnowledge BaseOrganizational LegibilityRetrieval & RAGSpec-Driven DevelopmentSoftware Supply-Chain Security
A knowledge graph earns its place in agentic software engineering less as a retrieval-accuracy mechanism (an edge that has largely eroded as models got better at parsing raw context) than as an organizational substrate for four practices: assembling task-specific context bundles, tracing decision provenance from idea to production, treating code rather than spec as the ground truth of system behavior, and making agent activity visible enough to audit and manage.

practical · conference talk transcript with live demo and audience Q&A — QCon AI presentation; personal-lessons-learned framing, explicitly disclaimed as neither dogma, a GraphRAG tutorial, nor a framework prediction. Two parts: why a knowledge graph is the foundation of agentic systems, then four practical patterns demonstrated via an internal engineering harness.

Key terms

  • context bundle / context as a bundle
    A task-specific assembly of graph-linked sources — domain knowledge, the current spec, prior related work, engineering standards, and the relevant code repo(s) — selected by proximity in the knowledge graph (e.g., which policy documents are in 'my team's neighborhood') rather than by dumping every available document into the prompt.
  • provenance (for decision making) Software Supply-Chain Security
    A queryable, branching audit trail in the graph linking an idea, to spec, to every human-agent back-and-forth intervention, to the merged PR, to production — captured continuously as a 'flywheel' where each action is written to the graph and then summarized.
  • code as truth, spec as kernel
    The implementation, not the specification, is what actually is real and driven to production; the spec's lasting value is capturing intent, and the two must be reconciled and related to each other in the graph rather than the spec being treated as disposable and re-derived from scratch each pass.
  • visibility (agent/team visibility) Organizational Legibility
    Retrospectively queryable records of agent activity — tokens used, turns taken, cost per task, context bundle contents and size, tasks shipped over time — surfaced from the same graph that already holds context and provenance data, used for audits, reporting, and team-effectiveness review, not merely a live progress display for one user.
  • knowledge graph as substrate (vs. retrieval system)
    The graph functioning as the foundational infrastructure layer of the engineering practice itself — feedback-loop tracking, audit, visualization — rather than chiefly a mechanism for producing a fuller answer to a question.
  • fast, honest mirror
    Agents don't independently improve or degrade outcomes; they accelerate whatever engineering practices (good or bad) already exist in an organization, exposing weak practices as fast, unmaintainable output and strong practices as fast, reliable delivery.

Arguments

The knowledge graph's justification for agentic engineering has moved from retrieval accuracy to serving as the substrate for context assembly, decision provenance, spec/code reconciliation, and visibility. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Models/agents have become good enough to answer correctly from raw, undifferentiated documents, eroding GraphRAG's original retrieval-accuracy differentiator.
  • Her own controlled on-call-policy experiment found identical correctness with vs. without a knowledge-graph-curated context bundle.
  • The graph nonetheless produced a measurable efficiency gain (fewer tokens, fewer turns) over feeding an agent everything.
  • Provenance tracking, spec/code reconciliation, and cross-domain visibility all depend on durable, queryable relationships that ad hoc RAG chunking does not provide.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizational engineering practice (auditability, traceability, efficient resource use) is a legitimate and valuable target for infrastructure investment, independent of raw answer-correctness.
  • The efficiency and governance benefits observed on one small internal experiment generalize to production scale, which she has not yet tested.
Inferences:
  • From (retrieval accuracy no longer differentiates) + (the graph still delivers efficiency, provenance, and traceability value), infers that the graph's justification has shifted rather than disappeared.
Whether agentic acceleration produces good or bad outcomes for an organization depends on the underlying engineering culture and practices already in place, not on the agent's raw capability. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Feedback loops that used to take weeks or months now surface a bad spec/story in about 40 minutes, so poor practice is exposed (and good practice is rewarded) far faster than before.
  • Teams with mature engineering practices (testing, CI/CD, well-formed handoffs) ship fast and correctly with agents; teams without them produce fast but unmaintainable "AI slop."
  • Her partner's startup case: a fully vibe-coded, demoed, customer-validated app collapsed once it needed to scale, for lack of testing/CI-CD discipline.
Implicit premises:
  • Agent capability is currently high and uniform enough across the compared teams/situations that observed variance in outcomes should be attributed to surrounding practice rather than to differences in the underlying model or agent.
  • One vivid secondhand anecdote (the startup) is representative of a general pattern rather than an outlier.
Inferences:
  • From (fast feedback loops) + (culture-dependent variance in outcomes across her own team and one anecdotal case) to (agents are a mirror of, not a cause of, engineering quality).
Code, not the specification, is the true record of system behavior and intent, and both must be captured and related to each other in the graph rather than the spec alone being trusted or discarded. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Specs are rarely implemented exactly as written; the real business criteria are revealed through implementation, not dictated by the spec.
  • Regenerating a spec after each pass erases what was learned during implementation ("the agent erased what the builder discovered").
  • Treating an agent as fire-and-forget on a spec produces results that diverge from what was actually wanted.
Implicit premises:
  • What matters organizationally is not just what happened (the code) but why (the intent), so both need to be captured and related, not code alone.
  • A single graph relationship between "intent" (spec) and "reality" (code), refreshed via periodic summarization, is a workable and sufficient representation of this lineage, as opposed to richer historical version-diffing or other mechanisms.
Inferences:
  • From repeated observed failure of specs-as-truth infers a corrective practice: graph-relational reconciliation of intent and reality.
Agent/team visibility — queryable after-the-fact traces of cost, turns, context bundles, and task history — is necessary for managing agentic engineering teams. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Without captured visibility, nobody — human or agent — can answer "what are our agents doing?" retrospectively.
  • Retrospective queries (cost per task, audit trail, context bundle size) require the underlying data to have already been captured somewhere queryable.
  • Because context, provenance, and spec/code data were already stored in the graph from Patterns 1–3, visibility queries came largely "for free."
Implicit premises:
  • A record the agent itself writes back into the graph is sufficient evidence for trustworthy audit and reporting purposes — an assumption never tested against independent, tamper-resistant instrumentation.
Inferences:
  • From (the data was already there) infers that visibility is a natural, low-cost consequence of Patterns 1–3 rather than a separately engineered capability.

Narratives

  • Cassie's own career arc — from coder, to a "hand-wavy" architecture consultant at Thoughtworks and elsewhere, back to the codebases via agentic engineering, pivoting her graph practice based on lessons from the last six months.
    → Establishes personal authority for the practical, lessons-learned framing and licenses the talk's explicit epistemic humility ("this is not dogma... just Cassie's experience").
  • The Auth0/OAuth demo — an idea ("implement Auth Auth") bound in the harness to an internal architecture Google Doc and a GitHub repo, so the agent works from the organization's own prior implementation and spec instead of researching Auth0/Stripe/Facebook patterns generically.
    → Makes the abstract "context bundle" pattern concrete; illustrates and supports the claim that org-specific context beats generic model/internet knowledge.
  • The on-call-policy A/B experiment across teams Meridian, Squad Cypress, and Atlas — the same task run twice, once with a graph-curated 'nearest neighbor' set of policy documents, once with all ~30 documents dumped in unfiltered.
    → Serves as the talk's sole quantitative evidence; grounds the pivot away from "GraphRAG for accuracy" toward "knowledge graph for token/turn efficiency."
  • Her partner's startup co-founder vibe-coded an entire application in three months, demoed it successfully to an excited customer, then everything fell apart once it needed to go to production and scale, for lack of testing and CI/CD discipline.
    → Illustrates and supports the "fast, honest mirror" thesis — agents accelerate whatever engineering practice (or its absence) already exists.
  • Her own prior claim, made on a podcast six months earlier, that "code is cheap, spec is the real truth" — which she now calls naive.
    → A self-corrective narrative that legitimizes Pattern 3 (code as truth) as a hard-won, revised lesson rather than an armchair claim.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Is GraphRAG dead — does a knowledge graph still add value now that models are so good at parsing raw context?
    Not dead, but its value proposition has moved from retrieval accuracy to efficiency (tokens/turns) plus non-retrieval functions (provenance, code-truth reconciliation, visibility).
  • ✅ How do you keep specs from going stale or wrong as agents implement quickly?
    Treat code as the authoritative truth, keep the spec brief and intent-focused, and continuously reconcile spec-intent vs. code-reality back into the shared graph rather than regenerating the spec from scratch each pass.
  • ⭕ How do organizational/tribal knowledge and shifting institutional rules stay usable by agents over time?
    She names the problem clearly (stale wiki pages, dead rules, decisions superseded over time) and asserts the graph should capture decision lineage, but describes no working mechanism for automatically detecting when a captured rule or decision has been superseded.
  • ✅ When is a knowledge graph worth its overhead versus simpler localized-agent-with-links approaches?
    Use it only when a demonstrated benefit exists (organization-specific context needs, or efficiency at scale) — it is not a default choice given its real build/maintenance cost.
  • ⭕ Is the harness's own audit trail (the agent writing its actions and summaries back into the graph) trustworthy enough for governance/audit purposes, given the agent both performs and reports on its own work?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe harness's provenance/audit trail is written back into the graph by the same agent (or an agent-driven summarization step) that performed the work, with no described independent, agent-can't-edit instrumentation layer verifying the record matches what actually happened — a self-reported chain of custody.
    Read next: Read against extract-the-meter-was-always-running (especially its claim that an agent cannot be the system of record for its own authority, cost, or refusals, and that evidence must be captured at instrumentation points the agent cannot bypass) for a corpus-level argument that self-reported agent provenance specifically fails an auditor.
  • incompleteThe core empirical claim — that a knowledge-graph-curated context bundle reduces tokens/turns versus dumping all documents — rests on a single internal experiment across roughly 30 documents in one organization for one task type (on-call policy), explicitly not yet validated for accuracy or savings at customer scale.
    Read next: Look for a follow-up talk or post from Cassie Shum / RelationalAI reporting the promised customer-scale validation, or independent benchmarks comparing graph-curated context assembly against raw-document retrieval on token/turn efficiency.
  • uninformedThe talk never engages with non-knowledge-graph approaches to the same four problems — e.g. a plain versioned document store with metadata tagging for context bundling, git/PR history for provenance, or a dedicated telemetry platform for visibility — so it is unclear whether a knowledge graph specifically is necessary or simply one implementation choice among several.
    Read next: Compare against extract-context-store-evolutionary-architecture's 'context store' proposal (a deterministic, versioned, repo-native record of intent, behavior, and architectural conformance) that claims similar benefits without a graph database — do the two proposals differ in capability, or mainly in storage technology?
  • incompletePatterns 2 and 4 (provenance and visibility) both assume that once everything is 'in the graph,' useful queries 'come for free,' but the talk gives no account of who designs and maintains these queries, how results stay honest as the graph schema evolves, or what happens when the harness itself — by her own admission in Q&A 'a bunch of devs clamoring something together' rather than a maintained product — breaks or drifts.
    Read next: Look for material on internal-tooling sustainability and platform-as-product discipline to test whether an informal internal harness like this one survives team turnover or organizational scaling.
  • incompleteThe claim that 'domain and context changes over time' and that decision lineage needs tracking is asserted as important, but no mechanism is described for how the graph detects that a previously captured rule or decision has been superseded, versus simply accumulating an ever-growing, potentially contradictory set of historical facts that an agent must disambiguate among — recreating, inside the graph, the very 'which policy applies' problem a plain agent already has.
    Read next: She flags this herself in the Open Questions section ('agentic memory architecture... how do we do this at scale'); worth tracking whether a later Shum/RelationalAI publication addresses temporal invalidation of graph facts.
  • illogicalThe claim that 'the moat isn't the models... it's the domain knowledge and the graph you put together' sits in tension with her own Q&A answer that the graph's original differentiator (retrieval accuracy) has been erased by model improvements, and that a graph should only be built when a specific benefit justifies its overhead. If the moat is the domain knowledge itself — which her own Pattern 1 experiment shows a sufficiently capable model can already use well from plain documents — it is unclear whether graph structure per se is the moat, or merely one delivery mechanism for domain knowledge that models will keep needing less structural help to use.
    Read next: Watch for this tension against extract-context-store-evolutionary-architecture and other retrieval-augmented-generation corpus material on whether structuring context in a graph remains a durable moat as base-model context handling keeps improving.
