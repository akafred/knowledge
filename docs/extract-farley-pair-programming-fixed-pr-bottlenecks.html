Pair Programming Fixed Our Pull Request Bottlenecks

Modern Software Engineering (Dave Farley) — clip from The Engineering Room podcast · video · 2025-07-11 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Lean & Toyota WayPair & Mob ProgrammingPsychological SafetyPull Requests & Async Code ReviewTrunk-Based & Continuous Delivery
Pair programming did not just make pull-request review faster for this team — it made the pull-request gate itself unnecessary, because the review, the four-eyes compliance requirement, and the knowledge-sharing that PRs exist to provide were already satisfied continuously by working together, which let the team drop branches entirely and move to minutes-long commit-to-production cycles.

practical · narrative — A first-person case-study interview: a team lead recounts, conversationally with Dave Farley, how adopting pairing dissolved their pull-request bottleneck and what changed afterward. The argument is carried by anecdote and self-report, not formal evidence.

Key terms

  • waste clock
    A self-devised ritual/mindset for continuously surfacing small, individually-tolerable inefficiencies (a two-minute build, a duplicate CI run) that compound into real cost — framed as something everyone, not just developers, is subject to.
  • four eyes
    The compliance requirement that two people be involved in a change — satisfied, in this team, by the pair itself, with the pull-request approval reduced to a rubber-stamp artifact that merely records the requirement was met.
  • feedback junkie
    A half-joking label for the state of having adapted to fast feedback loops so thoroughly that any slower loop becomes intolerable — presented as the 'only downside' of continuous improvement via tightened loops.

Arguments

Adopting pairing as a team's working default removes the pull-request bottleneck rather than merely making pull requests faster or smaller.
Explicit premises:
  • Review already happens live during pairing, so the post-hoc PR gate adds no new review value.
  • The compliance 'four eyes' requirement is satisfied by the pair, so PR approval becomes a formality rather than a control.
  • Shared context from pairing nearly eliminates merge conflicts, removing the practical need for long-lived feature branches.
  • Once branches became unnecessary, previously invisible waste (duplicate CI runs on branch and main) became visible and removable too.
Implicit premises:
  • PR review's actual value comes from 'two people genuinely engaged with the change,' not from the artifact or the merge gate itself — so any practice that produces that engagement without the gate is at least equivalent.
  • A team's collective authority (vs. one developer alone) is required to remove organizationally-accepted waste, because individual complaints about small, normalized costs get dismissed.
Inferences:
  • Removing the PR gate doesn't just save review time — it exposes a backlog of adjacent waste (slow local builds, duplicate pipeline runs) that the gate had been obscuring, which a team can then also eliminate.
  • If four-eyes compliance can be satisfied by the pair itself, the PR's remaining function in a pairing team is purely an audit-trail artifact, not a review mechanism — raising the same question Morris's and Laforgia's pieces ask: how do you produce that audit trail without reviving the PR gate?

Narratives

  • Discovering that a two-minute Maven build (accepted for years as 'how the world is') became intolerable once two people were sitting together waiting for it, which triggered a chain of further waste-elimination.
    → Supports the proposition that pairing makes previously invisible costs visible, driving them to be fixed — illustrates the 'waste clock' mechanism.
  • Contrast between 2011-12, when one person flew down from Tromsø to personally decide every release, and today's five-minutes commit-to-production.
    → Dramatizes the magnitude of change from gated, centralized, big-batch releases to pairing-enabled continuous delivery.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What triggered this team's move away from pull requests?
    Large pull requests and PR daisy-chains were disrupting flow; the tech lead instituted collaborative review instead of asynchronous back-and-forth.
  • ✅ Does pairing conflict with being an introvert?
    No — the guest reports that introversion does not mean antisocial, and the social/fun dimension of pairing became a genuine source of satisfaction, not just a productivity tool.
  • ⭕ Does this approach generalize beyond a single four-person team, or hold up under a stricter external audit of the 'four eyes' compliance claim?
