How soon AI will start firing programmers?

Yegor Bugayenko · article · 2023-09-04 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI Labor DisruptionCoding AgentsCognitive LoadPull Requests & Async Code Review
Writing in September 2023, weeks into the ChatGPT era and before any coding agent existed, Bugayenko predicts that AI 'robots' will progressively absorb a widening list of software-engineering tasks — starting with low-stakes, assistive work (bug reporting, pull-request review, refactoring, documentation, backlog grooming) and ending, as the furthest-off and most consequential item, with AI monitoring individual programmers' activity to appraise their productivity and determine 'which human contributors are most valuable to their projects' — so that the title's question about firing programmers is answered not by a date but by a position in the list: last.

theoretical · expository — A short, enumerated forecast (16 ordered predictions) written weeks after ChatGPT 3.5's public debut, not an argument for action. The piece asserts what will happen and in roughly what order, deliberately avoiding timelines ('items later on the list represent further-off predictions') despite the title's 'how soon' framing.

Key terms

  • Robots
    Bugayenko's blanket term for AI systems (specifically, generative-AI-driven automation building on ChatGPT) that will act on a codebase and its surrounding project artifacts — filing reports, opening pull requests, commenting on code — largely indistinguishably from a human contributor ('the originators of the pull requests may remain unaware whether their reviewer is human or robotic').

Arguments

AI taking over individual performance appraisal and determining programmers' relative value is a real, eventual capability, but one of the last capabilities to arrive — later than full code generation, review, and fixing. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • The list is explicitly ordered from nearer-term to further-off predictions.
  • Performance Appraisal is the final item on the list.
Implicit premises:
  • Tasks requiring judgment about human behavior and worth are intrinsically harder for AI to perform reliably than tasks requiring judgment about code, and therefore arrive later.
  • The ordering reflects genuine technical difficulty rather than social/organizational reluctance to deploy AI for appraisal — i.e., the delay is a capability gap, not a deployment-choice gap.
Inferences:
  • If the real binding constraint on AI performance appraisal were organizational appetite rather than technical capability, the list's ordering-by-remoteness would systematically understate how soon such appraisal could actually arrive once the technical building blocks (which the list's earlier items describe) are in place.
  • Three years later, several of the article's 'nearer-term' predictions (PR review, bug fixing, refactoring) are documented in this corpus as operating at scale, which invites re-examining whether the 'further-off' items are still further off for the same reason, or have simply not been tried yet.

Questions addressed

  • ⭕ How soon will AI start firing programmers?
    Not answered directly or with a timeline; the article substitutes a different, answerable question (what sequence of tasks will AI take over, ordered by remoteness) and lets Performance Appraisal's position at the end of that sequence stand in for an answer to the title's question.
  • ✅ What tasks currently performed by human programmers will generative AI take over, and in roughly what order?
    A 16-item ordered list, from bug reporting and PR review through documentation, requirements formalization, and technical debt analysis, to behavioral prediction and performance appraisal.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article never specifies what criterion separates a 'nearer-term' prediction from a 'further-off' one — is it required context window, required access to organizational/behavioral data, required trust from humans, or something else? Without a stated criterion, the ordering reads as intuition rather than a model, and it is impossible to tell whether Performance Appraisal is placed last because it is technically harder or because it is more consequential and uncomfortable to automate.
    Read next: This corpus's material on AI-as-amplifier and AI-labor-disruption, for frameworks that do distinguish technical-capability constraints from organizational/social-adoption constraints on which AI capabilities actually get deployed.
  • incompletePerformance Appraisal is described purely as a measurement-and-ranking capability ('determine which human contributors are most valuable') with no engagement with whether the underlying metrics (activity volume, comment sentiment, CI/CD failure correlation) would be a reliable basis for that determination — the article treats measurability as equivalent to validity.
    Read next: extract-lenny-forsgren-measure-ai-developer-productivity, which argues at length that individual productivity metrics are easily gamed and often misleading in the AI era — directly undercutting the premise that an AI monitoring 'each programmer's activity' would produce a trustworthy appraisal.
  • uninformedWritten before any agentic coding tool existed (ChatGPT 3.5 only, no tool use, no autonomous execution loop), the article has no way to anticipate that several of its 'assistive' predictions (PR review, bug fixing) would later be delivered not by discrete bot contributions to a repo but by persistent, scheduled, multi-run agent loops — a structural form this corpus's loop-engineering material shows is now central to how such capabilities are actually deployed.
