Should You Still Become a Software Engineer in 2026? GitHub VP

Jean Lee (interviewer); Scott Hanselman (guest, VP & Member of Technical Staff, Microsoft/GitHub) · video · 2026-06-25 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI as Normal TechnologyAI SlopHuman-in-the-LoopIronies of AutomationMalleable SoftwareMeasuring AI ImpactSkill–Ability GapSystemic Apprenticeship & Skill TransmissionTrust
AI coding agents are the latest in a recurring cycle of developer 'power tools' that change the tools but not the craft or its human accountability, so the real risk of the AI era is not that engineers become obsolete but that the profession fails to deliberately re-engineer its junior-to-senior training pipeline (via something like nursing's preceptor model) before AI hollows out the tasks juniors traditionally learned on.

practical · mixed — An interview that mixes narrative (Hanselman's personal history and live tool demos) with argument (accountability, apprenticeship-collapse, career advice), all in service of practical conclusions: what an aspiring engineer should do, and what organizations should do about junior hiring.

Key terms

  • slop cannon vs. AI-augmented software engineering
    The two poles of a spectrum for how AI-generated code enters a codebase: 'slop cannon' is uncritical, sycophancy-driven AI output flooding in unreviewed ('vibes'); 'AI-augmented' is AI-assisted authorship that still passes through the same rigorous SDLC (signing, testing, CI, review) as any other code source.
  • same ingress point
    The principle that code should be verified identically regardless of where it came from — his own hands, an anonymous open-source PR, or an AI — because the SDLC gate, not the source's identity, is what makes code trustworthy.
  • preceptor (nurse preceptor model)
    A named, formally credentialed senior role ('nurse trainer, nurse preceptor') that a newcomer can safely approach with basic questions without status penalty — proposed as a replacement for the 'intern/apprentice' framing in software engineering, where the senior's job is explicitly to make more seniors.
  • T-shaped
    Specializing deeply in one area while maintaining broad competence ('Swiss Army knife') across the rest of the stack — his answer to 'generalist or specialist?'
  • Tiny Tool Town
    His personal catalog site (500+ tools) for small, single-audience 'dumb' tools, where the entire application datastore is literally GitHub issues and the site is fully static (Astro-generated, no database, no realtime layer) — his working example that most engineers reach for infrastructure they don't need.

Propositions

  • explicitRoughly 70% of the code Hanselman personally writes today is AI-augmented, but it is safe only because it flows through the same software development lifecycle (code signing, testing, GitHub Actions, Dependabot, agentic review) as code from any other source.conf: high

  • explicitBecause AI cannot be held accountable, the human who reviews and merges bears full responsibility for what ships — exactly as if an anonymous human contributor's pull request had caused the same harm.conf: high

Arguments

It is always the human reviewer/merger's fault, not the AI's, when AI-assisted code causes harm in production. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • An anonymous human contributor's PR that breaks something is the merger's fault for approving it without looking.
  • AI-generated contributions should be treated identically to an anonymous human contributor's contributions.
Implicit premises:
  • The provenance of code (human hands, open-source PR, or AI) is irrelevant to who bears responsibility — only the review/verification gate determines trust.
  • Trust in any code source must be earned through verification infrastructure (tests, CI, agentic review), never extended by default.
Inferences:
  • An organization's real AI-safety posture is mostly a proxy for the rigor of its pre-existing SDLC — teams with weak ingress controls before AI will see amplified risk, not novel risk.
Software engineering as a profession will collapse without deliberate reinvestment in early-career hiring and training.
Explicit premises:
  • Senior engineers do not appear from nowhere; they are produced by a junior-to-senior pipeline.
  • If a company stops hiring/training juniors, its only source of seniors is hiring them away from competitors.
Implicit premises:
  • AI cannot yet substitute for the tacit, judgment-forming experience that junior-to-senior progression provides.
  • The industry-wide pullback on junior hiring is short-term-cost-optimizing at the expense of a systemic, longer-horizon supply problem.
Inferences:
  • A company that trains its juniors well risks becoming a net exporter of talent to competitors that free-ride and don't train — a collective-action problem that a single firm's preceptorship program (like Microsoft's) does not by itself solve.

Narratives

  • Hanselman's personal 42-year history of being told his craft was ending — not a 'real programmer' without assembler (1989), 'brain rot' from syntax highlighting (late '90s), 'death of coding' from Stack Overflow copy-paste (mid-2000s).
    → Shows, rather than argues, that the current AI panic is structurally identical to prior tool-adoption panics — grounds proposition p1.
  • The 'emoji bake' PR: a 2,000-line-diff contribution that first looked alarmingly large, but on inspection was a legitimate encoding-bug fix with 100 lines of new tests, which Hanselman reads carefully before running 'adversarial reviews with three or more models.'
    → Demonstrates what disciplined trust-but-verify review actually looks like in his own GitHub Copilot app workflow — supports p3 and p10.
  • The nurse-preceptor analogy, drawn from his wife (a nurse) and son (training to be one): a preceptor's name tag signals 'safe person, ask me anything,' contrasted with the fear a junior engineer feels asking a senior engineer what DNS stands for.
    → Grounds the abstract preceptorship proposal in a concrete, working institutional model from another profession — supports p5.
  • A young person told Hanselman he 'one-shotted' a Minecraft clone; when challenged to redo it without using the word 'Minecraft,' he couldn't, and the required prompt exploded in length.
    → Illustrates how much implicit specification a familiar proper noun smuggles into a prompt, and why a generic AI-generated clone demonstrates little — supports p8 and p9.
  • Tiny Tool Town (500+ community-submitted tools, backed entirely by GitHub issues, statically generated with Astro) and his 22-year blog (300MB of in-memory XML, invalidated only on new posts) — both managed day-to-day by his own agents doing triage and maintenance checks.
    → Concrete demonstration that simple, static, disposable architectures usually suffice, undercutting reflexive reach for databases/real-time infrastructure — supports p11.
  • 'Hansel Projects Today' — his personal AI-built dashboard that triages 125 to-dos and 111 PRs by emotional/energy state, informed by his own Type 1 diabetes and blood-sugar variability, distinguishing human PRs from bot PRs.
    → Shows AI used to make its author 'a better human' rather than just a faster one — supports p13.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Should you still become a software engineer, given AI coding agents?
    Yes — if you build strong fundamentals, communicate intent clearly, and build something personally meaningful rather than a generic AI-generated clone.
  • ✅ How does the current AI-driven shift in software engineering compare to previous major tool shifts (web, mobile, cloud)?
    It's the latest instance of a recurring pattern of tool-driven panic; the underlying craft has not changed across any of the prior shifts and Hanselman expects the same here.
  • ✅ What share of a senior practitioner's real day-to-day workflow is AI-assisted, and how is it kept safe?
    About 70% of his own code, kept safe by routing all code — human, open-source, or AI — through the same unchanged SDLC (signing, testing, CI, agentic review).
  • ⭕ How does the industry preserve or rebuild the junior-to-senior training pipeline once AI absorbs junior-level tasks?
    Hanselman names the collapse risk and describes an emerging, unvalidated Microsoft preceptorship pilot (with Microsoft Research exploring 'teaching models' for juniors) as a partial response, but does not resolve the cross-company free-rider dynamic he himself raises.
  • ⭕ Is Hanselman's own extremely high-agency, tool-building practice (building his own agentic triage system, running 100+ PRs across a personal portfolio, VP-level access to GitHub's own AI tooling) representative of what an average practicing engineer can achieve, or contingent on his unique seniority and resourcing?

Gaps

  • incompleteHanselman names the junior-training collapse as an industry-existential risk but the only concrete remedy offered — Microsoft's internal preceptorship pilot — is single-company, unvalidated, and does not address the free-rider dynamic he himself names: a firm that trains juniors well risks losing them to competitors that don't train at all. No mechanism is given for why individual-firm preceptorship programs don't just subsidize poachers.
    Read next: Look for industry-level (not single-firm) proposals for the apprenticeship collective-action problem, or follow-up reporting on whether Microsoft's preceptorship pilot produces measurable retention/promotion outcomes.
    closed byPodcast: How Will We Train Developers If AI Does the Routine Work: A Conversation with Scott Hanselman — That gap notes no mechanism was given for why individual-firm preceptorship doesn't just subsidize poachers. Here Hanselman partially addresses it: poached seniors 'use their competitor's techniques and their competitor's culture,' giving firms a private-benefit reason to grow their own people rather than rely on poaching -- though this argues training is still worthwhile despite leakage, not that the free-rider/collective-action problem itself is solved.
  • uninformedThe '~70% AI-augmented' and 'three sessions is optimal' figures are self-reported, uninstrumented estimates from a single high-context practitioner (a VP with deep, privileged access to GitHub's own Copilot tooling and the authority to insist on rigorous SDLC infrastructure) rather than measured or benchmarked data, and may not generalize to engineers with less resourcing or organizational authority.
    Read next: Compare against this corpus's instrumented AI-impact measurement sources (e.g. DX/Pragmatic Engineer throughput studies) rather than taking a single practitioner's self-report as representative.
  • illogicalThe 'know the basics because the automation will break' argument (Waymo/stick-shift analogy) is asserted as self-evidently true, but is never reconciled with his own opening anecdote mocking the 1989 claim that 'you're not a real programmer without assembler' as mere panic — he doesn't say which fundamentals are actually load-bearing today versus which are the modern equivalent of the assembler gatekeeping he dismisses.
    Read next: extract-det-skal-vaere-vanskelig or other ironies-of-automation sources for a more rigorous account of which specific skills survive automation versus which atrophy safely.
