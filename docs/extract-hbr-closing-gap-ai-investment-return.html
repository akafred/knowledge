Closing the Gap Between AI Investment and Financial Return

David L. Rogers, Krishnan Sankaranarayanan · article · 2026-09-23 Reader 🖥️

AI Value-Driver AlignmentEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessMeasuring AI ImpactProxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)Vendor Self-Reported Metrics
Because enterprise AI value is the product of three multiplicative factors—technology, adoption, and strategic alignment—rather than their sum, companies fail to convert heavy AI investment into measurable financial return primarily because they neglect the most overlooked and highest-leverage factor, strategic alignment, and must instead anchor every AI investment to their own value drivers, industry-value-chain position, and income-statement economics rather than pursuing generic, vendor-shared use cases.

practical · mixed — Argues for a specific course of action ('put your money where your value is') using a mix of expository framework-building (the value equation, the four-step method) and narrative case evidence (Nike, Netflix, Nestle, Merck, Aramco). The propositions are ultimately in service of what executives should do, not just what is true.

Key terms

  • AI Value Gap
    The gap between AI activity/investment (pilots, training, infrastructure, model adoption) and measurable financial return — described as 'the defining problem for executives leading AI efforts in large enterprises.'
  • Value driver AI Value-Driver Alignment
    'A lever that, when moved, changes the profitability of the business in a way that matters' — a firm- and industry-specific economic lever (e.g. procurement spend, asset utilization, customer retention), not a generic business metric.
  • Strategic alignment
    How closely the problems solved with AI align with a company's strategic priorities, balance sheet, and income statement — the third, most overlooked factor in the value equation.
  • Position in the industry value chain
    Where a firm sits within its industry's chain of production/value capture (e.g. commodity producer vs. specialty producer within chemical manufacturing), which determines which value drivers matter most for that specific firm, even holding industry constant.

Arguments

Strategic alignment deserves disproportionate executive attention relative to further investment in technology and adoption.
Explicit premises:
  • AI value equals Technology x Adoption x Strategic Alignment (multiplicative).
  • If any factor is near zero, total value collapses regardless of the other two factors' strength.
  • Most companies have already learned to invest in technology (right model, right data) and adoption (change management) but neglect strategic alignment.
Implicit premises:
  • Returns to further investment in a factor that is already well-resourced are lower than returns to fixing a comparatively neglected factor — a weakest-link/diminishing-returns assumption the bare multiplicative form does not itself establish (a product function alone doesn't tell you where the next marginal dollar returns most; that requires knowing each factor's current level).
  • Technology, adoption, and strategic alignment are an exhaustive, non-overlapping decomposition of what determines enterprise AI value — no other factor (e.g. data governance, regulatory risk, or organizational trust) meaningfully modulates it.
Inferences:
  • For a company that already has strong strategic alignment but weak technology or adoption, the equation's own logic implies further strategy work would be low-leverage for that company — a firm-specific caveat the article's blanket 'strategy is most overlooked' claim elides.
Executives should run the four-step process (define value drivers, locate value-chain position, mine the income statement, predefine financial metrics) before committing AI investment. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Value drivers differ sharply by industry and by a firm's position within its industry's value chain (Westlake vs. Givaudan).
  • Reading the income statement line by line generates hypotheses for where AI could move revenue or cost.
  • Companies that visibly followed this pattern (Aramco, Netflix, Merck, Nike, Nestle) report large measured returns tied to a specific, named value driver and predefined metric.
Implicit premises:
  • The correlation between value-driver alignment and measured return in these five case studies reflects a causal, replicable relationship rather than confounding by the fact that these are large, resource-rich incumbents likely already strong on the technology and adoption factors too.
  • Managers can accurately identify their true value drivers and income-statement leverage points in advance, and this identification exercise is not itself subject to the same organizational blind spots (IT/innovation ownership, disconnection from P&L) the article names as the root cause of the original problem.
Inferences:
  • If value-driver identification is itself politically difficult (per the article's own diagnosis — IT ownership, leaders avoiding hard choices), the four-step method's success may depend on an organizational precondition (P&L-connected AI ownership) the article never explains how to establish.
AI anchored to a firm's specific economics builds sustainable competitive advantage, while generic vendor-use-case AI does not. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Competitors have access to the same models and generic use cases as any given firm.
Implicit premises:
  • Deep knowledge of one's own business economics is itself durably hard for competitors to replicate — asserted ('your strategy is your moat') without addressing whether competitors could run the same four-step analytical method on their own income statements.
Inferences:
  • If the moat depends on the analytical method itself (now published to a mass HBR audience), rather than on proprietary data or execution speed, the article's own act of publishing the method works against the durability of the advantage it describes.

Narratives

  • The 'one minute saved' thought experiment: identical time-saving improvements are worthless in one business (reabsorbed into slack, off the critical path) and transformative in another (bottleneck task, done tens of thousands of times, or hazardous exposure).
    → Makes concrete the claim that value from an AI improvement is determined by strategic position (where the task sits relative to the business's economics and risk), not by the technical quality of the improvement itself.
  • Westlake (commodity chemical producer, capital-intensive, cost-driven) vs. Givaudan (specialty flavors/fragrances producer, R&D- and IP-driven) as two firms in the same industry with dramatically different value drivers.
    → Supports the claim that value drivers depend on a firm's position in its industry's value chain, not just its industry, so the same AI use case would be misapplied if transplanted between them.
  • Five named case studies — Nike, Netflix, Nestle, Merck (introduced as qualitative value examples, then given quantified returns), plus Aramco (introduced only in the final section) — each tied to a specific value driver and a specific financial or operational metric.
    → Serves as the article's main empirical support for the entire prescriptive method: that anchoring AI to value drivers causally produces large, measurable returns.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do large companies fail to see measurable financial return from heavy AI investment?
    Because they pursue low-value opportunities and neglect strategic alignment — not because of technology or people failures.
  • ✅ How should executives decide which AI opportunities to pursue?
    Anchor investment to the company's own value drivers, its position in the industry value chain, and specific income-statement lines.
  • ⭕ How should you measure whether an AI investment produced financial return?
    The article says metrics must be defined in advance and tied to the chosen value driver (e.g. false-reject rate for Merck), but gives no general method for isolating AI's causal, incremental contribution from other concurrent initiatives or macro conditions.
  • ✅ Why is strategic alignment specifically neglected relative to technology and adoption?
    IT/innovation ownership disconnected from P&L; belief that a powerful model with enough data avoids hard choices; framing AI as a skill to acquire; technology-first starting points driven by competitive fear.
  • ⭕ Does anchoring AI to value drivers durably protect returns once competitors adopt the same discipline?
    The article asserts a durable 'moat' without addressing that it has just published the general method to a mass audience, nor distinguishing alignment-as-analysis (replicable) from alignment-as-proprietary-data (genuinely rare).

Gaps

  • incompleteNo methodology is given for isolating AI's causal, incremental contribution to the reported outcomes (Aramco's $2.6B, Netflix's ~$1B, Merck's >50% reduction, Nike's 20%/15%/10% figures) from other concurrent initiatives, macro conditions, or reporting incentives. All figures appear self-reported by the companies, their vendors, or press coverage, with no control-group or counterfactual comparison — the same 'vendor self-reported metrics' pattern flagged elsewhere in the corpus.
    Read next: Read James Shore's AI-ROI series (extract-shore-ai-money-worth-part1) for a methods-focused critique of anecdotal and self-reported AI impact claims, and how to demand rigor before trusting a number.
  • incompleteThe article diagnoses IT/innovation teams disconnected from P&L responsibility as a root cause of misalignment, but never says who should own the value-driver-mapping exercise instead, how cross-functional buy-in is secured, or how disagreement between P&L owners about which value driver an AI investment should target gets resolved.
    Read next: Read extract-hbr-stop-automating-design-new-processes for its four-mode (Assist/Approve/Audit/Automate) authority framework, which at least specifies where human decision authority sits once a workflow is chosen.
  • incompleteThe article never explains how 'adoption' and 'strategic alignment' interact: if a technically excellent, strategically well-aligned AI solution fails to be adopted, is that an independent adoption failure, or does poor strategic targeting itself suppress adoption because employees can tell the problem doesn't matter? The equation treats the three factors as independent multiplicands, but the 'one minute saved' example suggests strategic relevance may itself drive whether people bother to adopt a tool.
    Read next: Read James Shore's AI-ROI series on the change-management cost of adoption (extract-shore-ai-money-worth-part1) to test whether adoption and strategic alignment are genuinely separable factors or entangled.
  • illogicalThe equation Value = Technology x Adoption x Strategic Alignment is presented as a genuine multiplicative model, with the associated 'any near-zero factor collapses value' inference, but is never operationalized with measurable units for any factor; it functions as a metaphor for 'all three matter and weak links dominate,' not a formula the authors have used to compute or predict value. Downstream inferences drawn from its multiplicative structure (e.g. which factor deserves the next marginal dollar) are not licensed by anything demonstrated in the piece.
    Read next: Compare against a piece that attempts actual quantitative ROI modeling of AI investment, to see the gap between a metaphorical and an operational 'AI value equation.'
  • incompleteThe 'moat' claim does not address that the article itself publishes the general four-step method to a mass HBR audience, nor does it engage with why competitors following the same publicly available method wouldn't erode the described advantage. It also never distinguishes alignment-as-analysis (replicable by any competent competitor reading this article) from alignment-as-proprietary-data (a genuine, durable moat).
    Read next: Read strategy literature on sustainable competitive advantage / the resource-based view to test whether 'knowing your own economics' is actually rare or inimitable once the method for finding it is public.
