Andrew Kelley Interview: Why He Built Zig, Banned AI Contributions, and Moved Zig off GitHub

Bruno Couriol · rss · 2026-09 Reader 🖥️

Because AI-generated open-source contributions are reliably low-quality and net-negative once maintainer review time is counted, and because code review is really an investment in cultivating future human core contributors rather than a pure quality filter, healthy open-source governance requires categorically banning AI-assisted contributions and preferring infrastructure (Codeberg over GitHub) whose institutional incentives favor long-term stability over corporate growth pressure.

practical · mixed — An InfoQ news article relaying and quoting a JetBrains interview with Zig creator Andrew Kelley. Primarily practical/argumentative — it justifies two governance decisions (banning AI contributions, migrating off GitHub) — with an expository, narrative section explaining Zig's technical origin story. The 'author' of the thesis is really Kelley, relayed by journalist Bruno Couriol through selected quotations.

Key terms

  • contributor poker Trust
    The bet a maintainer makes when allocating scarce review time: investing it in a contributor likely to grow into a long-term, skilled core team member, as opposed to spending it on a one-off 'drive-by' contributor who will never return. Used by Kelley to argue that AI-assisted contributors are structurally always in the 'drive-by' category, so investing review time in them is a bad bet by definition.
  • slop / garbage contributions
    AI-generated pull requests and patches that are not merely low quality but actively negative-value: they consume maintainer review time, and after a few review rounds reveal the submitter has no real understanding of the change.
  • laundering (the chat)
    The practice of a contributor pasting a maintainer's review feedback into an AI chatbot, then pasting the chatbot's response back to the maintainer as if it were the contributor's own reasoning, in an attempt to disguise AI use — which Kelley says is nonetheless detectable.
  • diverging value systems
    Kelley's explanation for why Bun rewrote itself from Zig to Rust with heavy AI assistance: not because Rust's language features are technically superior for Bun's purposes, but because Bun and Zig now hold different views on how AI should be used in producing and reviewing code.

Arguments

Zig should categorically ban AI-assisted code contributions rather than evaluate them case-by-case.
Explicit premises:
  • AI-generated contributions are typically low-quality and, once maintainer review time is counted, net-negative value.
  • Maintainer/reviewer time is a fixed resource that does not scale with contribution volume.
  • Code review functions as an investment in mentoring future core contributors; AI-assisted contributors are reliably 'drive-by' and do not repay that investment.
  • Disguised ('laundered') AI use is detectable by experienced maintainers, so the ban is enforceable in practice.
Implicit premises:
  • The project's goal is to grow a sustainable human contributor community and its social capital, not merely to accumulate correct code — code volume and community health are not the same currency.
  • Distinguishing acceptable from unacceptable AI-assisted contributions case-by-case would itself cost more reviewer time than a blanket rule, so a categorical policy is more efficient than a graduated one.
  • A contributor's demonstrated learning trajectory, not just the correctness of a given patch, is what code review is meant to select for.
Inferences:
  • The ban is fundamentally a social-trust and talent-pipeline governance decision, not primarily a code-quality-detection mechanism — implying that even an AI-assisted contribution indistinguishable in quality from a human one could still be rejected in principle if it doesn't represent a real human learning to become a contributor.
  • Projects that evaluate AI contributions purely on output correctness, rather than on contributor trajectory, will rationally end up with a different (more permissive, disclosure-based) policy than Zig's.
Zig migrated its canonical repository from GitHub to Codeberg.
Explicit premises:
  • GitHub's continuous integration became persistently unreliable for Zig ('simply stopped working').
  • Codeberg's CI worked reliably once adopted.
  • Nonprofit-run infrastructure is a more stable long-term partner than for-profit corporate or startup infrastructure, because nonprofit incentives favor continuity over growth/profit maximization.
Implicit premises:
  • Infrastructure reliability and stability matter enough to justify the cost and friction of migrating an established, widely-used open-source project's canonical repository.
  • GitHub's for-profit, Microsoft-owned structure and the reliability decline Kelley observed are causally or at least dispositionally linked — an inference Kelley draws largely from timing/correlation with rising AI-tool load, not from a demonstrated mechanism.
  • A nonprofit-hosted forge is expected to remain comparatively insulated from the growth pressures (including AI-driven infrastructure strain) that are affecting GitHub.
Inferences:
  • Kelley's stated preference for nonprofit infrastructure extends his skepticism of AI-driven, growth-oriented incentive structures beyond code contributions to the choice of developer platform itself — a coherent, broader governance stance rather than two unrelated decisions.
  • If the causal link to AI workload specifically turns out to be wrong (see gaps), the CI-reliability premise for migrating would still independently justify the move, even without the AI framing.

Narratives

  • Kelley recounts trying to build a native digital audio workstation and hitting a dead end with each existing option in turn: JavaScript lacked hardware control, Go's garbage-collection pauses broke real-time audio, pre-1.0 Rust's borrow checker stalled UI font-rendering work for weeks, and C++ produced persistent memory-corruption bugs that ate up debugging time.
    → Shows, rather than abstractly argues, why Zig's specific design choices (manual memory management, low-level control, no GC pauses) were necessary — grounding the language's design philosophy in a concrete, personally-motivating engineering failure rather than PL theory, and establishing Kelley's credibility as a serious systems-language creator whose later AI-skepticism should be read as engineering judgment, not reflexive technophobia.
  • The anecdote of a contributor pasting a maintainer's review feedback into an AI chatbot and pasting the chatbot's reply back as if it were their own understanding, which maintainers can nonetheless detect.
    → Illustrates and makes vivid the otherwise-abstract 'garbage / negative-value contributions' proposition, and implicitly argues that trust and authenticity in the contributor relationship — not just code correctness — are what the ban protects.
  • Bun's rewrite of its entire codebase from Zig to Rust in a heavily AI-agent-assisted effort, and Kelley's public reaction to it in his own post, 'My Thoughts on the Bun Rust Rewrite.'
    → Serves as a real-world foil that lets Kelley state his central reframing thesis — that the Bun/Zig split is about diverging values around AI use, not about Rust being technically better than Zig — against a concrete, well-publicized counter-example that could otherwise be read as a referendum on Zig's adequacy.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why did Zig ban AI-assisted contributions entirely rather than adopt a graduated review or disclosure policy?
    Fixed reviewer time plus the mentorship-investment framing of code review ('contributor poker') make AI-assisted contributors a categorically bad bet, regardless of individual output quality.
  • ✅ Why did Zig migrate its repository off GitHub to Codeberg?
    Persistent GitHub CI failures plus a preference for nonprofit-run infrastructure over corporate/startup infrastructure, on stability-of-incentives grounds.
  • ✅ Why was Zig created in the first place?
    A concrete, personal project (a native digital audio workstation) exposed hard limitations in JavaScript, Go, Rust (pre-1.0), and C++ that no existing language addressed together.
  • ✅ Does Bun's successful AI-assisted rewrite from Zig to Rust imply Zig lost on technical merits?
    Kelley explicitly denies this framing, attributing the split to diverging project value systems rather than language features.
  • ⭕ Does a blanket AI-contribution ban actually protect code quality and community trust better than a disclosure-and-accountability policy that still admits AI-assisted contributions from contributors who demonstrably understand their changes?

The interview doesn't engage with or rule out that competing explanation for its own case.

  • incompleteThe blanket-ban policy treats all AI-assisted contributions identically regardless of whether the human contributor understands and can defend the change, yet Kelley's own 'laundering' complaint implies maintainers can already detect disguised AI use in practice — raising the question of why a graduated policy (require and verify genuine understanding, rather than excluding by origin) wouldn't achieve the same quality/trust goals without a categorical exclusion. The interview doesn't address this alternative.
    Read next: Compare with other open-source projects' disclosure-and-review policies for AI-assisted contributions, to see whether detection-plus-accountability achieves comparable outcomes to a blanket ban.
  • uninformedNo discussion of whether Zig's ban risks excluding contributors who use AI only as a minor productivity aid but still deeply understand and own their changes — the 'AI-assisted' vs. 'AI-generated' distinction that other maintainers wrestle with via disclosure policies rather than blanket exclusion.

  • incompleteThe article never quantifies what fraction of Zig's actual pull-request volume was AI-related before the ban, so the scale of the problem the policy was designed to solve is asserted, not measured.
