Key terms

  • blue ocean Blue Ocean Strategy
    A previously unknown or uncontested market space where demand is created rather than fought over, as opposed to competing within an existing, overcrowded industry ('red ocean', implied but not named here). Identified by examining industry-imposed pain points that exclude noncustomers, not by benchmarking existing competitors' offerings.
  • noncustomers Blue Ocean Strategy
    People who don't currently buy in an industry because something about the industry's existing offerings excludes them (e.g., high installation cost, noise) — the population blue ocean strategy targets first, before existing customers.

Arguments

Readers should try the eight blue-ocean diagnostic questions at their next offsite or strategy planning meeting. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Blue ocean strategy (finding uncontested demand) sustains higher performance than competing in an overcrowded industry.
  • The framework has just been demonstrated to work again, in a fresh, non-tech case (Midea/air conditioning) as well as the familiar tech one (NVIDIA/AI).
Implicit premises:
  • Answering the diagnostic questions honestly is itself enough to surface an actionable opportunity — no further methodology (pricing, channel, execution) is required to get value from the exercise.
  • A strategy that worked for a large, resourced Chinese home-appliance conglomerate generalizes to whatever size or type of organization the reader leads.
  • The two cases cited (NVIDIA, Midea) are representative successes rather than survivorship-biased examples selected because they worked, out of a much larger population of attempted blue-ocean bets that didn't.
Inferences:
  • If blue oceans reliably attract fast, profitable growth once found, competitors should be expected to follow quickly — the piece never asks how long a newly identified blue ocean stays uncontested, i.e. how fast it turns red.

Narratives

  • Midea, the Chinese home-appliance giant, redesigned its air-conditioning offering around two buyer pain points — high installation cost and loud portable units — rather than competing on the existing feature set, as Europe's summers grow hotter.
    → Concretizes the abstract blue-ocean claim and extends it beyond its most familiar (tech/AI) illustration, functioning as the piece's sole evidence that the framework still works in 2026.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How can a firm find new, high-growth market opportunity instead of fighting over share in an existing, crowded market?
    Via blue ocean strategy: diagnose which noncustomers your industry excludes and why, using the eight framing questions (two are quoted) — resolved at the level of 'ask these questions,' not through a worked methodology showing what Midea did after identifying the pain points, or what results followed.
  • ✅ What should HBR.org cover in the coming month to be useful to its readers?
    By ranking readers' self-reported top leadership challenges from a 100+ respondent survey and committing to run articles against each.
  • ⭕ How representative or precise is the leadership-challenges ranking, given the five listed percentages sum to only 66% of respondents?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece quotes only 2 of the 8 diagnostic questions Kim & Mauborgne propose, and never shows what Midea actually changed (price point, installation model, product design) or what business results followed (share, revenue, adoption) — the case study asserts an insight but not its execution or outcome.
    Read next: The new/enhanced edition of Kim & Mauborgne's blue ocean strategy book itself, for the full eight-question framework and the Midea case in detail.
  • incompleteThe piece doesn't engage the standard critique of blue ocean strategy: that a genuinely uncontested market space rarely stays uncontested once demand and profitability are demonstrated (competitors imitate, the 'blue ocean' turns 'red'), and that framework case studies are drawn from known successes (NVIDIA, Midea) rather than from a representative sample of firms that tried the same diagnostic and failed — a survivorship-bias problem common to consulting-style strategy frameworks.
    Read next: Critical strategy literature on first-mover advantage erosion and imitation dynamics, or empirical follow-up studies on blue ocean strategy's track record since its 2005 introduction.
  • incompleteThe survey report gives five ranked leadership challenges with percentages that sum to 66% of respondents, with no statement of what the remaining 34% said (an 'other' category? unlisted lower-ranked challenges? was the question multi-select?), no total respondent count beyond 'more than 100', and no description of how respondents were recruited (self-selected newsletter subscribers who chose to answer a survey) — making it impossible to assess how representative the ranking is of leaders generally, versus of engaged HBR.org newsletter readers specifically.
    Read next: HBR's own methodology notes for the Insider Insights survey series, if published, or a larger-sample leadership-challenges survey with disclosed methodology for comparison.
