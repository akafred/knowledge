Are You Getting Your Money's Worth from AI?

James Shore · rss · 2026-09-08 Reader 🖥️

AI SlopAI Unit EconomicsBurnout & RecoveryEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessGoodhart's LawMeasuring AI ImpactProxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)Technical Debt
Because AI's costs and benefits in software development are real, substantial, and easily misjudged by anecdote, self-assessment, or gut feel, organizations facing consequential tradeoffs between speed, cost, and people's jobs must rigorously measure both sides — using durable, outcome-anchored, approach-level metrics rather than gameable activity proxies like lines of code, pull-request count, or individual performance targets.

practical · mixed — Practical: the essay argues for an action (measure AI's ROI rigorously; don't measure X, Y, Z) rather than for a theoretical understanding of AI. Form is mixed — expository framing of the problem, argumentative critique of bad metrics (LOC, PRs, self-assessment), and short practitioner anecdotes used as evidence throughout. This is part 1 of a 5-part series (parts 2-4 plus a CFO epilogue, publishing 15/22/29 Sep 2026); it is extracted here on its own terms — as the 'why, and what not to do' essay — but its argumentative shape (setup + negative case) is incomplete by design and should be read as scaffolding for the constructive methodology promised in later parts.

Key terms

  • Spending Efficiency / Cost per Value-Add Day
    Shore's own proposed metric (shown in a graph, not yet defined in prose): dollars spent per day of genuinely value-adding work, as opposed to overhead or maintenance time. Used to argue that AI can look cost-efficient in the near term and much less efficient over a longer horizon.
  • value-add work / delivering value
    Work whose output actually matters to a business outcome, as distinct from any completed ticket, PR, or line of code. Used to argue that speed metrics and value are not interchangeable.
  • approach (as the unit to measure)
    A specific way of using AI — a tool, workflow, or policy choice — as opposed to a person or team. Shore's proposed antidote to Goodhart's Law: measure and compare approaches, not the people using them.
  • slop AI Slop
    AI-generated code that is voluminous but not valuable — Shore's shorthand for what lines-of-code actually measures once AI is doing the writing.

Arguments

Organizations must rigorously measure AI's actual costs and benefits rather than relying on anecdote, consensus opinion, or gut feel. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • AI adoption carries substantial real costs (thousands to tens of thousands of dollars per engineer per month).
  • Those costs likely come out of personnel budgets, with real consequences for people's jobs.
  • There are also real business costs to not adopting AI.
  • Anecdotal reports about AI's benefits are unreliable and sometimes fictional.
  • Engineers' self-assessments of AI's speed benefit are demonstrably wrong (the METR study).
Implicit premises:
  • Decisions with major consequences for people's livelihoods carry a higher-than-usual obligation to be evidence-based rather than anecdote-driven.
  • Rigorous measurement of AI's ROI is achievable in practice — the argument assumes measurement is tractable, not merely that anecdote is bad.
  • The intended reader (an engineering leader) has the standing and resources to run a measurement program — the argument doesn't obviously extend to a solo developer or very small team.
Inferences:
  • If a leader cannot or will not measure rigorously, neither 'AI is a clear win' nor 'AI isn't worth it' is a supportable claim — unmeasured confidence in either direction is equally unwarranted.
Lines of code and pull-request count should not be used to measure AI-era productivity. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Lines of code has been known for decades not to correlate with productivity, and AI's verbosity worsens this by inflating code volume without adding value.
  • Pull-request count previously had only a loose correlation to development pace, resting on engineers producing PRs at a 'consistent and logical' human rate; AI breaks this by producing code faster than engineers can review.
  • Once people believe a metric is being used to measure productivity, they are tempted to game it.
Implicit premises:
  • A metric's validity is contingent on the underlying human behavior that generates it staying stable — once the generative process changes (AI writes the code instead of a human), a metric's definition can stay the same while its validity silently breaks.
  • PR count's status as even a rough productivity proxy was itself never independently validated — it worked only because engineers behaved consistently by habit, not because a causal link to value was ever established; AI didn't invalidate a proven metric so much as remove the convenient regularity that had been propping up an already-unvalidated one.
Inferences:
  • The same logic — metric validity depends on an unstated behavioral regularity that AI can silently break — likely applies to other activity metrics Shore doesn't name here, not just LOC and PRs; the essay doesn't generalize this into an explicit principle or enumerate which other metrics are at risk.
AI-impact metrics should be used to evaluate an approach, then retired — never used as an ongoing performance measure for teams or individuals. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Goodhart's Law (quoted from Robert Austin): once a measure becomes a target, workers take shortcuts, measured performance rises while true performance declines, and the measurement system becomes dysfunctional.
Implicit premises:
  • Measuring an 'approach' rather than 'people' is assumed to sidestep Goodhart's Law, because an approach (unlike a person) has no agency or incentive to game the metric — though people still choose and advocate for approaches, so this insulation is only partial and is not defended explicitly.
  • Learning from a metric is treated as a one-time or intermittent event ('learn from it, and stop measuring'), implicitly assuming approaches don't need re-evaluation as conditions change.
Inferences:
  • This assumption sits in tension with the essay's own earlier point that AI costs and capabilities are actively shifting (subsidies ending, prices dropping, models improving) — if the ground truth an approach was evaluated against keeps moving, 'stop measuring' risks leaving an organization blind to exactly the kind of change the essay itself says to expect.

Questions addressed

  • ⭕ How do you know whether you're getting a good return on AI investment in software development?
    Not answered in part 1 — the essay argues only that the question matters and that gut feel/anecdote can't answer it; the measurement methodology is deferred to parts 2-4.
  • ✅ What should you NOT measure when trying to quantify AI's impact?
    Lines of code, pull-request count, and any metric used to measure individual or team performance rather than an approach.
  • ✅ Should you assume today's AI token costs will hold when projecting ROI?
    No — today's costs are subsidized and unstable; model both optimistic and pessimistic pricing scenarios with explicit assumptions.
  • ✅ Is a bigger AI-driven productivity multiplier automatically better?
    No — not if its cost forces offsetting cuts, or if it isn't sustainable once maintenance costs and burnout are factored in over the long term.
  • ⭕ If metrics should be tied to evaluating an approach and then retired ('learn and stop'), how do you know when a shifting cost/capability landscape (which the essay itself says to expect) warrants re-measuring an approach you'd already stopped measuring?
