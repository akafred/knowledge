Presentation: Platform Engineering for Everyone - Success Can’t Be Coded

Max Körbächer · rss · 2026-07-20 Reader 🖥️

Cognitive LoadDeveloper Experience & FlowDORA & Engineering MetricsPlatform EngineeringSociotechnical ArchitectureTechnical DebtTrust
Platform engineering succeeds or fails on product-mindset discipline — an explicit, negotiated purpose; principles that substitute for ad hoc decisions; continuous multi-layered measurement (SPACE/DevEx over DORA); disciplined technical-debt retirement; and a self-sustaining community — rather than on technology choices, because infrastructure-first thinking (a disposition most platform engineers carry from their DevOps/infrastructure backgrounds) causes teams to build for the tech instead of the people who must voluntarily choose to adopt it.

practical · mixed — A conference-talk transcript. Mostly argumentative (building the case that infrastructure-first thinking is the root cause of platform failure and product mindset is the fix), interleaved with expository framework definitions (iron triangle, principles, SPACE/DevEx) and narrative anecdotes (Backstage-portal failure, demand-management origin story, Telco shadow-IT drop) used as evidence in place of data.

Key terms

  • Infrastructure-first thinking
    Designing a platform from the technology/architecture layer upward instead of from the user's need downward — the root failure mode, attributed to platform engineers' typical DevOps/infrastructure origin.
  • Randomeering
    Adding a tool to the platform because the engineer personally liked it (e.g. saw it at a conference), without validating need — a subtype of over-engineering.
  • Product mindset (vs. project)
    Treating the platform as an ongoing offering with a purpose, customers, and voluntary adoption, rather than a time-boxed deliverable with a deadline.
  • Iron triangle (feasibility / desirability / viability)
    A reframing of the classic scope/time/cost iron triangle: can you build it right (feasibility), do people want it (desirability), does it make business sense (viability) — with the claim that only two of three can be fulfilled well at a given resource level, and that pulling toward one flattens another (a metal band, not a rubber band).
  • 'We will' / 'you must' principles
    Two registers of guiding principle: 'we will' statements build collective ownership and unity ('we will drive transparency'); 'you must' statements are directive guidance for non-negotiables. A directive stated as an absolute command ('you have to do X, Y, Z') is not a principle at all.
  • Drivers (strategic / personal / market / analyst)
    Four sources of pressure on platform decisions besides technical merit: strategic (management/partnership mandate), personal (individual ethical/moral resistance), market (licensing changes, sovereignty), analyst/report (paid inclusion in industry reports).
  • Platform Engineering Purpose Canvas
    A workshop tool (free on Miro, from the speaker's book) that maps roles, targets, values, principles, KPIs, stakeholders and strengths to converge on an explicit platform purpose — distinct from a vision or mission, and revisable over time.
  • SPACE metrics
    Satisfaction, Performance, Activity, Communication, Efficiency — preferred over DORA because they can be scoped per person/team/system and better contextualize raw numbers (e.g. code volume vs. review time), at the cost of more setup effort.
  • Cognitive load (meeting/context-switch framing) Cognitive Load
    The disruptive residue of interruptions and context switches: a ~5-minute disruption can occupy the brain for up to an hour, and mood/topic carries over between unrelated back-to-back meetings.

Arguments

Platform teams should adopt an explicit product mindset (purpose, principles, voluntary adoption) rather than an infrastructure-first, project-based approach. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Most platform projects fail due to lack of purpose, unmet expectations, and low adoption, not lack of technology.
  • Infrastructure-first thinking neglects the person who will actually use the platform.
  • Forced or mandated adoption increases resistance and does not durably work because organizations lack real enforcement levers over skilled employees.
Implicit premises:
  • Product thinking (defined purpose, voluntary uptake, multi-stakeholder research) is the alternative discipline capable of producing adoption where mandate fails.
  • Platform engineers' typical infrastructure/DevOps backgrounds predispose them against product thinking, so the shift requires deliberate practiced role change, not just stated intent.
Inferences:
  • Therefore success in platform engineering is defined more by non-technical practices (purpose-setting, principles, measurement, community) than by technology choice.
  • Therefore 'success can't be coded' — the talk's own title claim.
Organizations should prefer SPACE and continuous DevEx feedback over DORA metrics as the primary lens for evaluating platform/team performance. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • DORA metrics are frequently weaponized by managers unfamiliar with engineering trade-offs to pressure teams into faster deploys, more features, and fewer failures simultaneously.
  • SPACE metrics can be normalized per person, team, or system, avoiding naive comparisons like raw code-review time against code volume.
  • Numeric correlations can be coincidental; only direct, repeated qualitative feedback surfaces the real user experience.
Implicit premises:
  • Metrics that are legible and simple to non-technical management (DORA) are more prone to weaponization than metrics that require interpretation and setup effort (SPACE/DevEx).
  • The extra cost of maintaining SPACE/DevEx measurement is worth paying to avoid the weaponization risk DORA carries.
Inferences:
  • Therefore leaders should invest in a continuously-owned SPACE/DevEx measurement practice even though it is more effortful than adopting DORA off the shelf.
  • Therefore DORA should be demoted to a secondary or supplementary signal rather than the default platform scorecard.
Platform teams should define explicit purpose and principles before building, and explicit depreciation criteria before retiring technical debt. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Platforms without a stated purpose ('why,' 'for whom') cannot be defended when management asks about value.
  • Ad hoc single-person decisions substitute for consistent direction unless principles exist.
  • Sunk-cost reasoning (which the speaker attributes partly to accounting/tax logic) keeps failing tools alive without a pre-agreed exit criterion.
Implicit premises:
  • A written purpose/principles/depreciation framework changes behavior mainly through legitimacy and communication, not through added authority or enforcement.
Inferences:
  • Therefore purpose-setting and depreciation criteria are best treated as one continuous discipline — defined up front, reused at retirement — rather than separate one-off exercises.

Narratives

  • Organizations buy a shiny developer portal (e.g. Backstage) first because 'Spotify does it,' deploy it, find it empty, spend five engineers and months configuring extensions, and still fail to feel the expected value.
    → Concrete instantiation of infrastructure-first / tool-before-purpose failure; frames the portal-before-platform pattern as the paradigmatic mistake the whole talk warns against.
  • The speaker's own 1.5 years working in demand management after his studies, forced to interview users, write requirements, and challenge what people 'really' wanted — described as personally painful and against his nature as an infrastructure person.
    → Authenticates the claim that product/user-research thinking is learned, not innate, for people with an infrastructure background; grounds the abstract 'infrastructure-first thinking' diagnosis in first-person testimony.
  • A large Telco customer's external IT service expenses dropped drastically shortly after the platform began solving even one or two identified problems, before it was finished.
    → Evidence that platforms suppress shadow IT and that partial, early value delivery matters more than completeness — supports the 'thinnest viable platform' and shadow-IT-as-metric claims.
  • Toyota tracks how many people use platform-provided documentation and finds nearly everyone in IT uses it, because it consolidated hundreds of previously scattered sources (repositories, websites, intranet pages) into one place.
    → Supplies a concrete example of an active-usage adoption metric and illustrates why consolidation itself can be the value delivered.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do platform engineering projects fail?
    Lack of purpose, infrastructure-first thinking, over-engineering ('randomeering'), unmet user needs, and unmanaged technical debt.
  • ✅ How should a platform be measured?
    Adoption/experience/outcome-style layered measurement — SPACE and continuous DevEx feedback preferred over DORA, plus shadow-IT tracking and time-to-market, started before the platform is built.
  • ✅ How do you decide what belongs on the platform and resist scope creep?
    Via explicit principles ('we will'/'you must') and by naming the driver category (strategic, personal, market, analyst) behind any given push.
  • ✅ How do you retire a failing platform investment despite sunk cost?
    Define depreciation criteria in advance, with the same discipline used to define purpose, and frame the retirement in terms stakeholders can accept rather than as an admission of waste.
  • ⭕ The talk claims purpose 'can change' — who has the authority to change it, what triggers a re-evaluation, and how is a changed purpose re-legitimized with stakeholders who bought into the original one?
    Not addressed; purpose revision is asserted as possible but no governance mechanism is described.
  • ⭕ When the four driver categories conflict — e.g. a strategic/partnership mandate collides with a team member's personal/ethical objection — how is the conflict actually resolved?
    The talk narrates exactly this scenario (a security expert refusing to include a tool for political reasons) but does not say how it was resolved.

Gaps

  • uninformedThe entire talk is grounded in the speaker's own consultancy experience and anecdote ('one larger Telco customer,' 'in my experience') with no named case studies, published data, or citations — including the claim that 'the latest studies' show AI projects 'failing more than being successful,' which names no study at all. The specific cognitive-load figure in p13 ('for every 5 minutes you are disrupted, you can add up to one hour where your brain is still processing') is likewise unsourced and reads like a garbled version of task-resumption/interruption-recovery research (commonly cited figures are on the order of ~20+ minutes to fully re-engage after an interruption, e.g. Gloria Mark's work) — the specific 5-minutes-to-1-hour ratio should not be repeated without checking the original source.
    Read next: Cross-check against corpus documents and any published platform-engineering surveys (DORA's own reports, PlatformCon state-of-platform-engineering surveys) that DO publish quantitative adoption/failure data, the way extract-compliance-platform-engineering-team#g1 already flags the identical evidentiary gap for a closely related InfoQ talk.
  • misinformedThe speaker explains the sunk-cost fallacy as caused by German accounting/tax rules requiring an asset to be 'used until you get all your taxes back,' calling it 'such a German thing.' This mischaracterizes sunk-cost bias, which is a well-documented, cross-culturally observed cognitive/loss-aversion bias (not primarily a tax-driven behavior), and it overstates depreciation-schedule mechanics as a legal usage requirement — accounting depreciation is a bookkeeping convention, not a mandate to keep operating an asset.
    Read next: Read the original behavioral-economics literature on sunk cost (Arkes & Blumer, "The psychology of sunk cost," 1985; Thaler on mental accounting) to see the bias documented as a general cognitive pattern, not a Germany-specific tax artifact — useful before repeating this explanation to others when advocating for depreciation criteria.
  • incompleteThe talk asserts platform purpose 'can change' but never describes who has authority to change it, what triggers a re-evaluation, or how a changed purpose is communicated and re-legitimized to the stakeholders who bought into the original one — a real gap given the talk's own claim that platform failure often stems from purpose never having been (re)negotiated in the first place.
    Read next: Corpus material on strategy-and-direction (compressed, personally-believed narratives) and on organizational governance of changing strategic commitments without losing team trust.
  • incompleteThe four driver categories (strategic, personal, market, analyst) are presented as a taxonomy but never resolved when they conflict — the talk narrates the exact scenario of a strategic/partnership-mandated tool colliding with a team member's moral objection ('I don't support the political orientation of where this company is located') without saying how the conflict was actually adjudicated.
    Read next: Corpus material on informal leadership and leader restraint for how leaders adjudicate genuine value conflicts within a team without either overriding the objector or letting one person veto a strategic commitment.
  • illogicalThe talk argues both that mandates and enforcement don't work ('companies cannot do any consequences... it's very worthless that someone scream at you') and that strategic/partnership drivers routinely force teams to adopt tools they don't want ('you have the partnership... so use it... but you have to use it'). These are in tension: if genuine enforcement is unavailable, it is unclear how strategic mandates actually secure compliance where product-style persuasion is said to fail, or why they would prove more durable than the mandate-driven approaches the talk elsewhere says never work.
    Read next: Look for the speaker's book (Platform Engineering for Architects) or a follow-up talk addressing how forced/partnership-driven tool adoption is actually operationalized when direct managerial coercion is explicitly described as ineffective.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • How Platform Engineering Using Golden Bricks Can Enable Fast and Smooth Delivery · g5 — Golden-bricks' Adoption/Experience/Outcomes scheme is flagged as measuring 'existence, not direction' — no mechanism for guiding which brick to build next. Körbächer supplies exactly that mechanism: continuous, open-ended user interviews plus SPACE/DevEx signals feed directly into deciding what feature to build next ('start with the thinnest viable platform... go step-by-step with the new features') and when to retire a brick (explicit depreciation criteria), turning the same three-layer measurement scheme into a design-direction input rather than only a C-level justification.
