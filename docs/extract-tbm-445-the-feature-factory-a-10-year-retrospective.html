TBM 445: The Feature Factory: A 10-Year Retrospective

John Cutler · email · 2026-10-15 Reader 🖥️

Ten years after his viral 'feature factory' post, Cutler retracts the binary framing: every organization is a feature factory to some degree, kept there by trust deficits and structural conditions (funding, data access, promotion systems, how the team was formed) that outcome vocabulary cannot fix and that outcome rituals can themselves turn into another performative proxy, so the practical path is small honest outcomes, conviction and economic framing from team formation, with AI amplifying whichever habits are already there.

practical · mixed — A reflective retrospective essay (narrative of the original viral post and what it did to him) that doubles as an argument: it revises a binary diagnostic (feature factory vs. outcome-oriented) into a spectrum and tells practitioners what to do instead ('get away from 0/1 thinking', invest structurally, act on conviction, stay sane). Loosely structured, self-described as written under a timer in a no-edit tool, so some points (performative outcome-centricity, AI) are explicitly flagged as not fully fleshed out.

Key terms

  • Feature factory Feature Factory (Output-as-Progress Mindset)
    A way of working in which someone in the company thinks of product progress as the list of features shipped. In the retrospective it is a mindset present to some degree everywhere (and a proxy), not a pathological type of company one is 'in' or 'out of'.
  • ___ factory
    The generalization that you can run a 'problem factory', 'OKR factory' or 'outcome factory' in a million ways: any agreed-upon proxy can be turned into a production line that never reaches the crux of how hard the work is.
  • Spectrum (not 0/1)
    Every problem is a nested solution to a higher-order problem and every outcome a tactical step toward a broader one, so no team is ever fully outcome-oriented; being a feature factory is a matter of degree.
  • Proxy
    An agreed-upon stand-in for value (features, specs, problem statements, metrics, OKRs) that a group adopts to make forward progress, especially under low trust or high coordination load; a social agreement as much as a measurement.
  • Performative outcome-centricity Feature Factory (Output-as-Progress Mindset)
    Shipping all the outcome frameworks and rituals (OKRs, JTBD, opportunity trees, business cases) to show you are VERY sure it will work, while rationalizing weak or unusable output; the same problem as before in different words.
  • Premature convergence Product Discovery & Experimentation
    Forcing early commitment (to a team form, a brand, a product boundary, a solution) before the context supports it; named as a result of the projects-to-products wave and prevented by autonomy.
  • Conviction
    A partly irrational commitment to a way of working or a future that data can boost but not fully substitute for; teams that beat the factory often do so from conviction forged by past screwups, and it can also drive a product over a cliff.
  • Promotion-driven development Feature Factory (Output-as-Progress Mindset)
    Work chosen and reported for what it does for promotion packets, endemic even in companies that are nominally outcome-driven.
  • Slop tornado
    The output of PMs incentivized by 'justify every outcome' business-case culture using LLMs to connect everything in seemingly realistic ways - plausible-looking artifacts no one will really read.
  • Multi-quarter bets
    Funding units with a real business case and a defined horizon, offered as an alternative to assuming each team will last forever in its current form.

Propositions

  • explicitEvery organization contains a feature-factory way of thinking somewhere (someone treats product as features shipped), so the real question is how to work within it and stay sane, not whether you are in one.conf: high
  • explicitBeing a feature factory is a spectrum, not a 0/1 state: every problem is a nested solution to a higher-order problem, so even teams running validated experiments remain a feature factory to some degree.conf: high
  • explicitBinary framings (outcomes vs. outputs, projects vs. products, feature factory vs. product-led) were useful rhetoric for getting executives talking but misdescribe work: organizations that are held up as product-forward do projects, products, outputs and outcomes all at once.conf: high
  • explicitThe projects-to-products wave caused real damage (everything renamed a product, over-hiring of product management, UX set back, 'operating models' installed as plug-and-play, platforms made second-class, premature product teams and layoffs), and the first principle organizations missed was the shift in funding and governance mechanisms; thinking in multi-quarter bets with a real business case is flatter, better grounded in economics and has fewer perverse incentives.conf: high

  • explicitOutcome-centricity has swung to the opposite failure: outcome, goal and problem machinery (OKRs, JTBD, Lean UX, opportunity framing) is so baked in that it rationalizes shipping unusable designs on flimsy research, and has left a gap between hand-wavy high-level math and prompts-and-specs with no hands-on tinkering or shaping in between.conf: medium

  • explicitTeams further down the stack (platforms) cannot rely on outcome orientation alone, because if others do not buy into the causal chain from their work to revenue and cost they are stuck; in that case it is better to run a feature factory and do experiments or measurement on the side.conf: high

Arguments

Feature factory is a matter of degree, and adopting outcome vocabulary does not take a team out of it. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Every problem is a nested solution to a higher-order problem; every input is an output to some degree; every outcome is a tactical step toward a broader, messier one
  • Near-term impact is locked into a bigger graph of ideas through linkages of varying certainty
  • Even product-forward companies live with execs saying 'ship faster' and tension between output and long-term outcomes
  • Platform teams must have their causal chain to revenue and cost accepted by others or be challenged on what they ship
Implicit premises:
  • Whether work counts as an 'outcome' depends on the level of abstraction at which someone chooses to evaluate it
  • There is always an authority above a team entitled to challenge the causal chain
  • A binary label is only useful as a rhetorical device, not as a diagnostic
Inferences:
  • Maturity is the altitude at which a team can defend its causal story, not escape from shipping
  • Platform teams' factory mode is rational rather than a failure of mindset
  • Binary maturity models for product orgs are misleading by construction
Teams default to the feature (or other tangible) proxy because trust is low, and outcome machinery without trust reproduces the same problem. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Low trust, low familiarity and many dependencies push people to proxies
  • Features are tangible and imaginable; failure is not palatable
  • The team HAS to agree on a proxy to move forward
  • Transactional project shops end up with a spec agreement even when they pitch outcomes
  • Teams focus on problems but never admit they didn't solve them; promotion-driven development is rife in outcome-oriented companies
Implicit premises:
  • Agreement on a concrete artifact is the cheapest way to produce shared commitment
  • Fear of failure is the binding constraint, not ignorance of better methods
  • Outcome frameworks are adopted for their reassurance value, not their learning value
Inferences:
  • Building trust or psychological safety is a prerequisite for moving along the spectrum, and cannot be skipped by introducing OKRs
  • A factory with trust may outperform an outcome ritual without it
Escaping a harmful factory requires structural investment and upstream design, not exhortation or mindset. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Customer access, data access, autonomy, training, designers and promotion structures are literally investments of money, time and political capital
  • Individuals have limited political capital and time for what may be one job in a career
  • The game is set when someone sells the org on starting the team and attaches a business case (or not)
  • It is very difficult to rewind to that first meeting with finance
Implicit premises:
  • Structural conditions dominate individual agency in determining team behavior
  • The people who formed the team had real alternatives at formation time
  • Money and organizational design are the real mediating variables, not culture or mindset
Inferences:
  • Intervention leverage is greatest at funding and team-formation decisions
  • Telling teams to 'be outcome-oriented' while leaving incentives, data and promotion systems unchanged is blame-shifting
AI will accelerate whichever direction an organization is already pointing, so using it to escape the factory depends on already-healthy habits. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI is decent at instrumentation, connecting signals, rapid prototyping and recontextualizing for feedback
  • A team used AI-written PRDs on a toxic doc culture and amplified all the bad parts
  • Synthetic personas let teams who never talked to customers talk to machines instead
  • Business-case culture plus LLMs produces slop tornadoes
Implicit premises:
  • Artifacts that already served as proxies (PRDs, business cases) become effectively free to produce with AI, so proxy inflation follows
  • Healthy habits are the scarce input and AI does not supply them
  • The same capability serves opposite ends depending on incentives
Inferences:
  • The relative value of hands-on shaping and direct customer contact rises as artifact production costs fall
  • AI adoption programs that do not address incentives will increase performative outcome-centricity

Narratives

  • The origin of the 2016 Medium post: written in a couple of minutes to prove to a Medium researcher that punchier posts earn more traffic than nuanced ones; it went viral, hit Hacker News, led to a job and about 1000 later posts.
    → Shows the binary framing was partly a product of attention dynamics, licensing the retraction and illustrating that simplified frameworks spread faster than the nuance they distort.
  • A team at a company frequently touted as product-forward laughs at the outcomes-vs-outputs discourse: they use projects and products, care about outputs and outcomes, and have execs demanding 'ship faster'.
    → Supports the claim that the binary is a false tension and that the tension itself is the job everywhere.
  • Cutler recalls high-performing teams reduced to unabashed feature-factory motion under low trust, and discovery-skilled teams that live in Miro boards but cannot ship.
    → Supports the proxy-under-low-trust argument and the claim that both poles have failure modes.
  • AppFolio entered an established domain, reverse-engineered the settled accounting features (feature factory for the win) while betting boldly on segmentation with fewer features; Dotwork bet that AI would make legacy long-tail features unnecessary or different.
    → Shows that features are sometimes right and that a mix of settled-feature shipping and bold bets is a legitimate strategy, with AI blurring what counts as a feature.
  • A team 'AI-PRD-ing it up': reasonable-sounding AI PRDs eliminate back-and-forth, everything seems clear, and a toxic doc culture is amplified; similarly teams that never talked to customers now talk to synthetic personas.
    → Exemplifies the claim that AI amplifies bad habits and inflates proxy artifacts.
  • A prototype 'built for fun' is declared by teams to be ready to ship ('OK, ship it. That's not a prototype!!!!').
    → Illustrates the loss of hands-on shaping between hand-wavy outcome math and prompts-and-specs.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Is 'feature factory' a useful binary diagnosis, and what happened when it was applied at scale?
    No: it is a spectrum, and the binary (and its product-vs-project cousins) fed reorganizations and mislabeling; the missing first principle was funding and governance.
  • ✅ Why do teams stay in feature-factory mode even when they know better?
    Low trust and heavy coordination push toward agreed proxies, and structural investments (data, customer access, autonomy, promotion structures, team-formation economics) are costly and hard to change.
  • ⭕ When are features simply the right answer?
    Where the domain has settled feedback and a short experiment loop exists, but 'where's the line' is explicitly left open, especially as AI redefines what a feature is.
  • ⭕ How can a team practically move along the spectrum, and how does it know where it stands?
    Only partial answers: shrink outcomes to the smallest observable step, hold conviction alongside signals, fund multi-quarter bets. No diagnostic or sequence is given.
  • ✅ What does AI do to the feature-factory dynamic?
    It amplifies existing habits in both directions; the escape potential is asserted rather than demonstrated.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe pivotal mechanism - trust is what lets a team drop the tangible feature proxy - is asserted but the post never says how trust gets built or whether running a well-oiled factory builds it (or entrenches it). 'Stay sane' is offered as the goal, but no path from proxy agreement to a trust-backed way of working is described, and the sibling question of path dependency from factory success (TBM 415) remains open.
    Read next: Trust-building and psychological-safety literature (Edmondson), Cutler's own writing on social contracts and minimally viable consistency, and innovation literature on competency traps / the innovator's dilemma applied to internal teams.
  • incompleteThe spectrum claim removes the binary diagnostic but supplies no replacement: how does a team or leader tell where on the spectrum it sits, what a healthy mix of 'just ship the settled features' and 'explore' looks like, or how the mix should change over time? 'Where's the line?' is asked rhetorically and left unanswered.
    Read next: Cutler's demand-mix framework (TBM 415), Cagan and Torres on discovery cadence, and Wardley-style evolution mapping for deciding which components are settled enough to just build.
  • illogicalConviction is endorsed as necessary at long horizons, and also described as something contrary evidence cannot dislodge (believers call it an exception) and that can drive a product over a cliff. The post gives no criterion for distinguishing productive conviction from sunk-cost denial, so the endorsement is unfalsifiable as stated.
    Read next: Annie Duke on resulting and quitting, escalation-of-commitment research, and Cutler's 'conviction backed by signals' framing in TBM 416.
    closed byThis will make you a better decision maker | Annie Duke (Thinking In Bets, former pro poker player) — Gives a criterion separating productive conviction from sunk-cost denial: conviction backed by pre-committed signals and kill criteria, plus the 'would you start this today' test.
  • incompleteThe AI section claims AI is 'pretty good' at instrumentation and 'decent' at connecting signals, prototyping and recontextualizing, and that it gives triple the shots on goal, but offers no evidence that AI actually moves teams from output to learning; the negative cases are anecdotes ('I heard recently of a team...'). It also does not explain why the favorable uses would win out against the incentive structures the post says dominate.
    Read next: DORA AI capabilities model and field studies of AI-assisted discovery/analytics workflows; evidence on whether AI shifts demand mix in practice.
