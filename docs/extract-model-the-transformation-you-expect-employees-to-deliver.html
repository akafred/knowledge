Model the Transformation You Expect Employees to Deliver

Antonio Nieto-Rodriguez · article · 2026-07-22 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Change Management & AdoptionGo to the Floor (Gemba)Learning Culture & Slack
Organizational transformations succeed or fail not because employees resist change but because senior leaders either do or do not visibly perform, in their own calendars and decisions, the same risk-taking, uncertainty-tolerance, and willingness-to-reverse-course they are asking employees to adopt.

practical · argumentative essay with embedded case studies — A consulting-derived prescriptive essay: it diagnoses a cause of transformation failure (leaders not modeling the behavior they demand), supports the diagnosis with cited benchmarking research and two contrastive case studies, then converts the diagnosis into a diagnostic instrument (the three tests) the reader can apply to their own leadership team. Practical throughout — every element serves the imperative in the title.

Key terms

  • Modeling the transformation
    A leader's own calendar time, decisions, and personal risk exposure visibly matching the behaviors (experimentation, risk-taking, course-correction) demanded of employees — distinguished sharply from communicating, funding, or governing the change.
  • The calendar test
    A specific diagnostic: what share of the senior team's actual monthly time (not stated priority) goes to the transformation versus running the existing business, and whether the CEO's own time is on the riskiest bet or only the safe ones.
  • The uncertainty test
    Whether the senior team has any shared, lived experience of operating together inside a genuinely unresolved bet, as opposed to reviewing and governing the transformation from outside.
  • The cancellation test
    Whether the senior team has publicly killed an initiative it previously championed, with lessons stated out loud — used as the most visible proof that changing course costs nothing when evidence changes.
  • Project-driven organization / project economy
    The author's broader framework (from Powered by Projects) describing an organizational form where value creation is increasingly project work ('change the business') rather than repeatable operational work ('run the business'), which is displacing the operational machine as the dominant structure.
  • Active and visible executive sponsorship
    Cited from Prosci's benchmarking research as the single biggest contributor to change success across 12 studies since 1998 — quantified, not rhetorical (79% vs 27% success rate by sponsor effectiveness).

Arguments

The primary determinant of transformation success is whether senior leaders visibly model the demanded behaviors themselves, not whether employees are willing to change. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • McKinsey: visible leadership modeling makes transformations 5.3x more likely to succeed; only 43% of leaders model.
  • Prosci: executive sponsorship quality has been the largest single contributor to change success since 1998, with a 79%-vs-27% success gap by sponsor effectiveness.
  • The most-cited obstacle to transformation success is absent sponsorship, not workforce resistance.
Implicit premises:
  • Correlational benchmarking data (leaders who model transformations succeed more often) can be read as evidence of a causal mechanism (modeling causes success) rather than a byproduct of some third factor (e.g., leaders confident enough to model behavior may also be better strategists or operate in less troubled organizations).
  • 'Visible modeling' and 'effective sponsorship' as measured by McKinsey and Prosci are close enough proxies for the same underlying behavior that the two studies can be combined into one argument.
Inferences:
  • If modeling behavior is both necessary and the largest measured lever, then interventions aimed at employee mindset or communication are lower-leverage than interventions aimed at leadership behavior.
  • Therefore diagnosing and fixing leadership behavior should precede or dominate diagnosing and fixing employee resistance.
Credit Suisse's cultural transformation failed because Horta-Osório failed to personally embody the accountability standard he was hired to instill. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Horta-Osório was recruited specifically to fix 'a cultural problem that translated into a lack of accountability' and stated the new direction was personal responsibility and accountability.
  • He personally breached Covid quarantine rules twice and drew scrutiny over private-jet use — conduct directly contradicting the standard he was hired to model.
  • He resigned nine months into the role, and the cultural transformation 'never recovered momentum.'
  • Credit Suisse ceased to exist as an independent institution fifteen months after his resignation.
Implicit premises:
  • The chairman's personal conduct, rather than the underlying governance and risk-control failures (Greensill, Archegos) that predated his appointment, is the operative cause of the transformation's failure to recover.
  • No credible successor or interim structure could substitute for the chairman's personal credibility once it was breached.
  • The 15-month gap between his resignation and the UBS takeover is short enough, and causally connected enough, to count as a consequence of the failed cultural transformation rather than an independent outcome of the pre-existing Greensill/Archegos losses and market confidence collapse.
Inferences:
  • If a symbolic personal breach can derail an already-launched cultural transformation, then leader credibility is a single point of failure the organization has limited ability to hedge against.
  • Therefore boards should treat personal conduct scrutiny of transformation sponsors as equivalent in importance to their strategic competence.

Narratives

  • Piyush Gupta's 15-year transformation of DBS Bank (from a bank customers nicknamed 'Damn, Bloody, Slow' to one of Innosight's ten most transformative organizations of the 2010s), achieved by personally visiting branches incognito as a customer, making experimentation a leadership KPI (1,000 experiments run in one year with most leadership participating), running a quarterly anonymous 'Tell Piyush' suggestion channel (about a third of suggestions leading to policy changes), and entering the bank's internal AWS DeepRacer AI competition himself, finishing in the top 100 among DBS's own employees.
    → Serves as the article's positive proof case: strategy content (digitize, mandate experimentation, build startup culture) was 'familiar in outline,' so the narrative's work is to isolate personal, visible leadership modeling as the differentiating variable that made the strategy 'stick.'
  • António Horta-Osório was recruited as Credit Suisse's chairman in April 2021 specifically to repair a culture of unaccountability following the Greensill and Archegos losses (the latter costing about $5.5 billion), publicly committing to 'a culture of personal responsibility and accountability,' then resigned nine months later after an internal investigation found he had personally breached Covid quarantine rules twice and drawn scrutiny over private-jet use; the cultural transformation never recovered, and Credit Suisse was forced into a UBS takeover fifteen months after his resignation.
    → Serves as the article's negative/contrastive proof case, structurally paired with DBS: it implicitly supports the proposition that a leader's personal conduct — not strategy quality, board mandate, or stated commitment — is what determines whether a transformation an organization badly needs actually lands.
  • An unnamed large industrial company spent nearly two years 'in transformation' with a sound strategy and an active program office but little to show for it, because the executive team reviewed the effort monthly rather than leading it; a new CEO then took personal ownership of the two hardest initiatives, moved them to the top of his own calendar, and worked in the sessions where the real trade-offs were made, after which the rest of the team followed within weeks and previously stuck decisions cleared.
    → Closing corroboration that converts the article's diagnostic claim into a before/after natural experiment within a single organization, implicitly supporting the proposition that modeling behavior (not new strategy or new resourcing) was the variable that changed between the stalled period and the unstuck period.
  • Three brief, unnamed vignettes: a European energy company CEO personally chairing the group's most uncertain diversification bet while letting safer programs run without him; a global pharmaceutical CFO moving her strongest controller onto an unproven AI initiative; a retail bank CEO joining the working sessions of a digital venture of uncertain survival and staying through mixed early results.
    → Generalizes the DBS pattern beyond one famous, extensively studied case, implicitly supporting the claim that visible personal modeling is a replicable leadership behavior observable across industries and company sizes, not an idiosyncratic feature of Gupta or DBS specifically.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do transformations actually fail, if not because of employee resistance?
    Because senior leaders do not visibly perform, in their own time and decisions, the behaviors (risk-taking, tolerance for uncertainty, willingness to cancel failed bets) they demand of employees, while continuing to govern through legacy forums that reward the opposite behaviors.
  • ✅ How can a leadership team tell, concretely, whether it is actually modeling the transformation rather than merely endorsing it?
    Apply the three tests: the calendar test (time allocation and whose bet the CEO owns), the uncertainty test (shared lived experience of an unresolved bet), and the cancellation test (whether anything previously championed has been publicly killed).
  • ⭕ Is Credit Suisse's collapse best explained by governance/risk failures (Greensill, Archegos) or by a failure of leadership modeling?
    The article names both but does not adjudicate their relative weight; it asserts the modeling failure is 'at its heart' without ruling out that the antecedent governance and risk failures were independently sufficient to doom the bank regardless of Horta-Osório's personal conduct.
  • ⭕ What should a leadership team do once it has failed the three tests — is there a remediation process, or only the diagnostic?
    Not addressed; the article ends on the diagnostic ('fail them, and you've already told your people...') without describing how a team moves from failing to passing, beyond the implicit suggestion (via the industrial-company case) that a single leader taking personal ownership of the hardest initiatives can unstick things quickly.
  • ⭕ Does the prescription scale below the CEO/chairman level — can a business-unit leader or middle manager 'model the transformation' with the same effect if the leaders above them are not doing so?
    Not addressed at all; every example in the article is a CEO or chairman-level actor.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article never specifies how a leadership team gets from failing the three tests to passing them, beyond one anecdote (a new CEO personally taking ownership of two hard initiatives). It gives no account of what happens when a team recognizes it is failing but the CEO who would need to change their calendar is the same person unwilling or unable to do so — the article's own logic (leaders cannot delegate this) implies there is no remediation path that does not run through the top person's own willingness, but this implication is never stated or examined.
    Read next: Read against literature on board-level accountability and CEO succession/removal for cultural failure (relevant given the Credit Suisse case itself ended in the chairman's own removal), and against change-management frameworks (e.g., Kotter, ADKAR) that specify sequencing and remediation steps rather than only diagnostics.
  • incompleteEvery case in the article — Gupta, Horta-Osório, the unnamed industrial-company CEO, the energy-company CEO, the pharma CFO, the retail-bank CEO — sits at CEO, chairman, or C-suite level. The article's own opening claim ('the bottleneck is at the top') is never tested against a case where the bottleneck is one level below the top: a business-unit leader or VP whose own boss is not modeling the transformation. It is left unaddressed whether visible modeling by a sub-CEO leader can still move their own team's behavior, or whether the effect requires the very top of the hierarchy to model it.
    Read next: Read alongside middle-management- or informal-leadership-focused accounts of change agency operating without top-level air cover, to test whether 'modeling' works bottom-up/mid-up or only top-down.
  • incompleteThe McKinsey and Prosci statistics (5.3x more likely to succeed; 79% vs 27%) are presented as if the causal arrow runs cleanly from leader modeling to transformation success. The article does not consider or rule out reverse causation (transformations that are already succeeding for other structural reasons — better resourcing, less internal politics, a healthier starting culture — may simply free leaders to model behavior visibly, rather than the modeling causing the success) or confounding (leaders confident and secure enough to visibly take personal risk may be systematically different, and more effective for unrelated reasons, from leaders who are not).
    Read next: Read alongside the original McKinsey and Prosci methodology write-ups to check whether either study attempts to control for confounds or establish direction of causality, or is purely correlational self-reported benchmarking.
  • uninformedThe article treats the Credit Suisse collapse as if the modeling failure were the decisive cause ('at its heart is Horta-Osório's failure to embody and model the transformation'), while its own text names the Greensill and Archegos losses (the latter alone costing about $5.5 billion) as having already 'laid bare' the cultural problem before Horta-Osório's appointment. It does not engage with the possibility that Credit Suisse's fate was already largely sealed by those prior losses and the resulting collapse in market and counterparty confidence, independent of whether the chairman personally modeled accountability afterward.
    Read next: Read banking-sector post-mortems on Credit Suisse's collapse (regulatory reports, the Swiss FINMA assessment) that weigh governance/risk-control failures against leadership/cultural factors, to establish whether the modeling-failure framing is proportionate to its actual causal weight.
