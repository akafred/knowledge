AI and the Looming Competition for Margin

Philipp Carlsson-Szlezak and Paul Swartz (BCG) · article · 2026-07-30 Reader 🖥️

AI Unit EconomicsFrontier Model Convergence / CommoditizationIndustrialization of AIJevons ParadoxMoats & DefensibilityTechnology Diffusion & Transformation Curves
AI productivity gains are inherently deflationary — because a widely diffused technology confers no durable competitive advantage, the industries best at converting AI into productivity growth will see the fiercest price competition and margin erosion, making consumers rather than firms the real winners, so executives must invest anyway (the risk of abstention is asymmetric) while expecting the payoff to be survival, not profit.

practical · mixed — Builds a theoretical economic framework (three pathways to productivity growth, all deflationary) and grounds it in historical case studies (autos, agriculture, internet retail), but the piece exists to tell executives what to do today — invest anyway, expect margin erosion, measure output not inputs — so it is ultimately practical/prescriptive.

Key terms

  • Same with less
    One of three productivity-growth pathways: firms use AI to reduce input costs for a given level of real output, gaining a cost advantage that spurs price cuts and price competition.
  • More with same
    Firms use AI to grow real output with steady inputs, creating a volume/supply advantage that drives price competition, especially when demand is inelastic.
  • New replaces old
    Firms use AI to build entirely new business models, offering a temporary escape from the immediate margin battle — until diffusion lets rivals copy the innovation and the battle for share and margin resumes.
  • Competition inhibitors / Competition amplifiers
    Paired vocabulary for the forces that slow (moats, low utilization, regulation, societal skepticism, market structure) or speed (size of the productivity gain, inelastic demand, non-scalable capacity) the translation of AI productivity into price competition.
  • Winner's curse
    Repurposed from auction theory to describe how being best at converting AI into productivity backfires: the firms and industries that succeed most at using AI are the ones that suffer the fiercest margin erosion.

Arguments

Productivity growth from AI converges on margin erosion regardless of which of the three pathways a firm or industry pursues. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI is widely diffused — everyone can access essentially the same models.
  • Each of the three pathways (same-with-less, more-with-same, new-replaces-old) creates a cost, volume, or business-model advantage.
  • Once a technology is widely diffused, any advantage it creates can be replicated by competitors.
  • When an advantage is replicable, competitors race to copy it, intensifying price competition to capture share.
Implicit premises:
  • The durability of a competitive advantage is inversely proportional to how widely diffused its underlying technology is — a claim asserted via examples (autos, agriculture) rather than argued from first principles.
  • Firms behave as atomistic price competitors rather than coordinating (tacitly or otherwise) to avoid a price war — the piece never considers oligopolistic restraint, collusion, or regulatory limits on price competition as a check on the model.
Inferences:
  • If the argument holds, first-movers who succeed at AI-driven productivity gains earn no durable reward for having moved first — the rational strategy shifts from 'invest to win' to 'invest to avoid losing,' a Red-Queen race the authors partially acknowledge in 'only relative gains count' but don't name as such.
AI's deflationary impact on margins will be slower and more uneven than the paper's own central thesis implies.
Explicit premises:
  • A more than tenfold increase in code volume is only producing a 1.3x increase in product releases (the input-to-output translation lags).
  • 50% of workers use AI but only 13% use it daily — low utilization caps realized productivity gains.
  • Regulators can block deployment outright (NYC declining to renew Waymo licenses).
  • 50% of Americans are more concerned than excited about AI (Pew, March 2026) — societal acceptance is not assured.
  • Some markets (e.g. medical care) are structurally resistant to price competition regardless of underlying cost changes.
Implicit premises:
  • These five frictions are treated as slowing the deflationary dynamic rather than durably blocking it in any given industry — but the piece never states whether or how a friction converts from 'delay' to 'permanent exemption.'
Inferences:
  • If some of these frictions are structural rather than transitional (e.g. medical care's market structure, or regulation in transportation), certain industries may never experience the margin battle the article's headline thesis predicts as near-universal — a possibility the piece doesn't rule in or out.

Narratives

  • Autos: over ~70 years, technology steadily replaced labor (falling employment share from ~2% to 0.6%), prices fell, and margins that hovered near 30-40% decades ago have been whittled to near-zero today.
    → Empirically anchors the 'same with less' pathway and the claim that widely diffused technology produces long-run margin erosion.
  • Agriculture: mechanization, fertilizers, and genetic modification cut labor inputs so far that farm employment fell to 1% of the US workforce and food's share of consumer budgets fell from 40%+ to 13%, despite recent grocery-price inflation complaints.
    → A second, independent long-run case for the same 'same with less' deflation dynamic, reinforcing that the pattern is general rather than auto-specific.
  • The internet's effect on retail: it cut inventory and supply-chain costs, which let scale players use their efficiency and tight margins to force out competitors — and those same scale players then built new, durable revenue streams around advertising and third-party marketplaces.
    → Presented as an illustration of all three pathways operating simultaneously — but on inspection it actually shows surviving scale players (e.g. Amazon-like retailers) ending up MORE profitable and durably advantaged via new moats (ads, marketplace), not converged toward zero margin. See gap g1.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Will AI's productivity gains show up as higher profits for the firms that achieve them, or will they be competed away?
    Competed away, via price competition, flowing to consumers as the 'real winners' — except where competition inhibitors (moats, regulation, market structure) block price competition from taking hold.
  • ✅ Should executives invest in AI given the prospect that success itself may erode their own margins?
    Yes — invest regardless, because the downside risk of not investing (existential, if AI proves transformative) is asymmetrically worse than the downside of investing (merely a disappointing, competitively-neutral ROI).
  • ⭕ Which specific industries or firms will actually escape the deflationary dynamic and sustain durable moats or monopoly profits?
    The piece says this outcome is 'less likely' in general and gestures at deep, hard-to-replicate deployments as the exception, but offers no criteria for identifying such cases in advance — and its own retail/internet example arguably shows scale players doing exactly this (see gap g1) without the piece noticing the tension.

Gaps

  • illogicalThe piece's own retail/internet case study undercuts its 'no durable advantage' premise: it describes scale players using AI-like efficiency gains to force out competitors and then building new, durable revenue streams (advertising, third-party marketplaces) — i.e., a winner-take-most outcome with growing, not eroding, aggregate profitability for the survivors. That is closer to the corpus's documented pattern of durable advantage migrating to a different layer (see moats, frontier-convergence) than to the universal margin-erosion story the example is used to support.
    Read next: Corpus material on moats and frontier-model convergence — e.g. how competitive advantage relocates to the harness/product/distribution layer rather than disappearing when the base technology commoditizes.
  • incompleteThe 'more with same' pathway explicitly conditions severe margin erosion on demand being inelastic, but the piece never examines which industries have elastic vs. inelastic demand for AI-driven output. The corpus's well-documented Jevons-paradox pattern for cognitive and software work (cheaper cognition/code has historically increased total demand for it, not just made existing output cheaper) suggests demand in exactly the industries most associated with AI (software, cognitive services) may be relatively elastic — which, by the article's own stated logic, should blunt rather than accelerate margin erosion there, a distinction the piece's mostly physical-goods examples (autos, agriculture) don't surface.
    Read next: Corpus material applying Jevons Paradox to cognition and software engineering — cheaper cognition increasing total demand for cognitive/engineering work rather than shrinking it.
  • incompleteThe prescriptive 'What This Means for Executives' section treats the deflationary thesis as urgent enough to mandate immediate, full-scale investment for every executive, without differentiating strategy by how fast or slow deflation is likely to arrive given the very frictions the piece itself lists (regulation, market structure, utilization rates). A firm in a friction-heavy sector (the piece's own medical-care example) gets the identical 'the race is mandatory' prescription as one in a friction-free sector, even though the auto and agriculture case studies show the same dynamic can take seven decades to fully play out.
    Read next: None specified by the author — an open strategic question about how urgency should scale with an industry's specific competition-inhibitor profile.
  • incompleteThe auto-industry chart's claim that margins fell 'to near 0%' doesn't specify which margin metric (gross, operating, net) is plotted or whether it's an industry-aggregate accounting figure vs. actual firm-level profitability — making the empirical anchor for the central thesis difficult to independently verify or compare against known auto-manufacturer profit margins.
    Read next: Primary BEA/BLS/BCG Center for Macroeconomics data cited as the chart's source.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • What Makes Chinese Companies So Competitive? · g2 — That gap flags that hyper-competitive excellence (China's dense industrial ecosystem) coexists with chronic overcapacity and margin-eroding price wars, without explaining the mechanism. This article supplies the general economic mechanism: widely diffused productivity-enhancing capability confers no durable advantage, so the very firms best at deploying it face the fiercest price competition and margin erosion — the 'winner's curse' — which is a general theory of why competitive excellence and margin erosion co-occur, though it doesn't address China-specific policy responses like anti-dumping tariffs.
