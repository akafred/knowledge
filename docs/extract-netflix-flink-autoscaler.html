Netflix Moves Toward Open Source Flink Autoscaler for 30,000+ Streaming Jobs

Leela Kumili · rss · 2026-09-07 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Compute & Energy InfrastructureCustom Infrastructure Operators (Extending Platform Primitives)Infrastructure EfficiencyPlatform EngineeringVendor Self-Reported Metrics
Reasoning about autoscaling at the level of individual dataflow operators (via true processing rate estimated from throughput and busy time) rather than the whole cluster is a strictly better model for scaling complex, heterogeneous, stateful streaming pipelines — good enough that Netflix, despite years of investment in its own cluster-level autoscaler, is migrating 30,000+ jobs to the open-source Apache Flink Autoscaler and building new internal control-plane infrastructure around it rather than continuing to extend its bespoke system.

practical · expository — A short technical news report that, while descriptive in form (recounting a historical progression from 2019 to now), functions practically: it implicitly argues that operator-level, true-processing-rate autoscaling is architecturally superior to cluster-level autoscaling for heterogeneous stateful streaming jobs, and that this superiority is now strong enough to justify migrating a mature, home-grown system to an open-source alternative.

Key terms

  • True Processing Rate
    An operator's actual sustainable processing capacity, estimated from measured throughput and busy time, used as a simpler alternative to critical-path analysis for computing the parallelism an operator needs.
  • cluster-level vs. operator-level autoscaling
    Cluster-level: one scaling decision applied to the total TaskManager count for an entire job. Operator-level: an independent parallelism decision computed per vertex in the job graph.
  • forward connected subgraph
    A chain of Flink operators linked by a FORWARD connection (no shuffle between them), which Netflix's implementation deliberately keeps together during rescaling to avoid unnecessary redistribution.
  • utilization target
    The configured busy-time threshold (Netflix: 0.45) at which the autoscaler decides an operator needs more/less parallelism — a knob that trades off resource efficiency against how often (and how disruptively) a job gets rescaled.
  • control plane Platform Engineering
    Netflix's own orchestration layer (a Spring Boot service using Temporal workflows) that wraps the OSS autoscaler and isolates per-job scaling decisions, as opposed to deploying the autoscaler directly via the Flink Kubernetes Operator.

  • explicitThe Apache Flink Autoscaler estimates each operator's true processing rate from throughput and busy time and computes required parallelism per vertex by walking the job graph, rather than making one scaling decision for the whole cluster.conf: high

  • explicitNetflix integrated the open-source autoscaler with its own internal control plane — a Spring Boot service using Temporal workflows to isolate per-job autoscaling decisions — rather than deploying it directly through the Flink Kubernetes Operator.conf: high

Arguments

Operator-level autoscaling (using true processing rate) is architecturally better than cluster-level autoscaling for complex, heterogeneous, stateful streaming pipelines. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Cluster-level autoscaling forces all operators in a job to share the same scaling decision.
  • Stateful pipelines with branches, joins, and terabytes of state have operators with genuinely different processing requirements.
  • The operator-level autoscaler computes per-vertex required parallelism from each operator's measured true processing rate.
Implicit premises:
  • A scaling decision that matches actual per-operator resource needs wastes less compute than one that must average across heterogeneous operators.
  • Reduced compute waste translates into lower cost without a compensating loss in throughput, latency, or state-recovery guarantees.
Inferences:
  • If operator-level autoscaling generalizes as reported, cluster-level autoscalers are likely the wrong default for any stateful pipeline with meaningfully heterogeneous operators, not just Netflix's specific workloads.
Migrating to, and building custom control-plane infrastructure around, an open-source community-maintained autoscaler is the right long-term choice for Netflix, even though it required substantial internal engineering to adapt. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Netflix modified JobManager metric collection, added server-side metric filtering, preserved forward-connected subgraphs, and added sink-backpressure handling to make the OSS autoscaler work at its scale.
  • Netflix plans to migrate its remaining internal autoscaling use cases to the open-source implementation.
Implicit premises:
  • A community-maintained approach that keeps improving (ongoing FLIP-271 work, Flink 2 disaggregated state) will outpace a bespoke internal system's rate of improvement over time.
  • The cost of integration engineering is a largely one-time or amortizing cost, whereas maintaining a fully bespoke autoscaler indefinitely is an ongoing cost that diverges further from best practice each year.
Inferences:
  • Netflix's original 2019 autoscaler and its Mantis/Atlas-based telemetry pipeline may eventually be fully retired, devaluing that accumulated institutional investment.

Narratives

  • The history of Netflix's own autoscaler — built around 2019 on Mantis/Atlas telemetry, reducing resource usage 25-45% — followed by the discovery of its scaling-unit limitation as pipelines grew more heterogeneous, followed by adoption of the newer operator-level approach.
    → Implicitly argues that even a successful, long-running internal system can become the wrong architecture as workload complexity grows, and that recognizing this — rather than continuing to invest in extending the original design — is the right response.
  • Vasiliki Kalavri's account of the DS2 project initially exploring critical-path analysis before adopting True Processing Rate as a simpler baseline, which 'worked really well' and subsequently became part of Flink's autoscaling work.
    → Implicitly supports the value of empirically testing a simple approach against a more complex one before committing to complexity — a preference for simplicity that turned out to generalize into production use, and a case of academic streaming-systems research (an OSDI paper) flowing directly into widely-deployed infrastructure.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How can a company autoscale complex, stateful streaming pipelines with heterogeneous per-operator resource needs, when cluster-level autoscaling forces one scaling decision for the whole job?
    Scale at the operator level using true processing rate estimated from throughput and busy time, walking the job graph to compute per-vertex parallelism (FLIP-271).
  • ✅ How do you integrate a general-purpose open-source autoscaler into an existing internal control-plane architecture and scale it to jobs with thousands of subtasks?
    Build a dedicated control-plane service (Spring Boot + Temporal) to isolate per-job decisions, and modify JobManager metric collection and filtering to handle up to 3,000 subtasks.
  • ⭕ How do you avoid destructive over-rescaling of large stateful jobs, where each rescale event is itself expensive due to state recovery?
    Partially addressed by lowering the utilization target (0.45 vs. 0.7 default) and preserving forward-connected subgraphs to reduce unnecessary redistribution; the deeper problem of state-recovery cost during rescaling is explicitly left as future work (Flink 2 disaggregated state).
  • ⭕ Does the reported 58% / $1.1M-per-year saving generalize beyond the one team it is reported for, and under what baseline or methodology was it measured?
