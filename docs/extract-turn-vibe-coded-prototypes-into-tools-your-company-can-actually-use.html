Turn Vibe-Coded Prototypes Into Tools Your Company Can Actually Use

José Parra Moyano, Karl Schmedders, Olivier Laplace, Benjamin Torben-Nielsen · article (HBR; re-saved via Chrome + Readwise Highlighter extension because the original feed capture 01m52c0wtjxvq038ec57ww1b8k was a 19-word RSS stub) · 2026-10-16 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Citizen DeveloperEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessInsourcing & In-House CapabilityModularity & CouplingNear-Zero-Cost CodeNon-Engineers BuildingPrototype-to-Production GapScope DisciplineVibe Coding
AI makes a working prototype nearly free to build, but turning it into a tool the company can rely on opens a 'deployment gap' that is closed only by involving technical experts from the start, keeping the tool's connections to other systems few, and holding its scope to the users it was built for (scaling the approach rather than the solution).

practical · argumentative — A practitioner-research HBR article built on the authors' two-year engagement with 11 companies (life sciences, banking, consumer goods, software; US and Europe), where at least one author led or advised each project. It argues for three managerial disciplines (engage experts early, limit connections, hold scope) and illustrates each with a failure case and a success case. Evidence is anecdotal and the cases are mostly unnamed (Roche is the one named company, and one author works there).

Key terms

  • Deployment gap Prototype-to-Production Gap
    The gulf between a promising AI-built prototype and a reliable solution used in everyday operations; it widens as users, situations and system connections multiply, and maintenance cost can exceed an off-the-shelf purchase.
  • Myopia
    The builders' (and executives') blind spot about how the AI-chosen architecture is structured, what deployment will cost and what maintenance will entail, because they supplied intent but never learned the design.
  • Dependency
    The set of obligations created by each data feed, integration or downstream process tied to the tool; more links mean costlier fixing, replacement or retirement.
  • Scope (creep after pilot success)
    The pressure to push a successful homegrown tool to more teams, regions and use cases, which requires functionality built by people who never saw the original design.
  • Scale the approach, not the solution
    Use a successful tool as a model for other teams to build their own narrowly scoped tools for problems they own, instead of widening the original tool.
  • Governing the zoo
    A consumer-goods multinational's policy: individuals and teams vibe-code tools for their own use and keep using them as is; only when others want the same does central software and AI staff decide whether to build a broader version, which professional developers build separately.
  • Last mile
    The final stretch from basic prototype to production reliability: complex regulatory requirements, multi-agent coordination and everyday reliability.

Arguments

Non-technical teams building with AI need engineers involved early, even if the engineers are not the builders. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • With AI deciding architecture, builders do not know how the tool is structured, what it will cost to deploy or what maintenance needs.
  • Late-arriving specialists find repair costlier than a rebuild.
  • Expert engineers spot what non-expert builders miss; Roche's review model works.
Implicit premises:
  • The architecture the AI chooses is rarely legible to its non-technical author and cannot be made legible cheaply by asking the AI itself.
  • Expert review is cheap relative to the cost of a failed production push (3 engineers to 18 analysts is affordable).
  • Review can be introduced without destroying the speed advantage that motivated vibe-coding.
Inferences:
  • Review capacity (engineers per builder) becomes the throttle on how many AI-built tools reach production.
  • AI-generated architecture documentation or automated review might substitute for some human review, a possibility the article does not consider.
Keep a tool's connections to other systems to a minimum. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Each connection creates an obligation and a failure surface.
  • The bank's connector-heavy build became unmanageable; the video pipeline that ends at human approval stayed easy to update.
Implicit premises:
  • Business value of a connector is estimable up front, so the 'value vs complexity' comparison can be made.
  • A human-approval stop is acceptable to the business process (it does not defeat the automation's purpose).
Inferences:
  • A narrower pipeline trades end-to-end automation for swap-ability, which is a deliberate architectural bet on model change.
Do not expand a successful tool's scope; scale the approach so each team builds its own narrow tool. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Extensions are built by people who do not understand the original design and so cannot fix failures.
  • The packaging company's payroll expansion failed; the Swiss software company's narrow tools survived two years.
  • Where builders are the users, the knowledge needed for a fix stays where the fix is needed.
Implicit premises:
  • The cost of N narrow tools is lower than one shared tool, which ignores duplication, inconsistency and the cost of N maintenance burdens.
  • Builders remain employed and available; knowledge locality does not decay with turnover.
Inferences:
  • 'Governing the zoo' only works if the central experts who evaluate promotion requests are resourced; otherwise requests pile up or shadow expansion resumes.

Narratives

  • A major European bank piloted seven coordinated agents with a Swiss startup, saw relationship managers gain an hour a day of client time, then built an internal team, spent six months and got only basic prototypes; the vendor was re-hired and chose to rebuild from scratch.
    → Supports the deployment-gap claim and the proposition that tool ownership without architectural understanding is costly; it frames all three failure modes.
  • Roche's 18-person data team uses AI to build dashboards and analyses, with three engineers reviewing every solution before production or spread beyond its builder.
    → Supports expert-involvement as a workable practice that preserves analyst speed.
  • A northern-European food and beverage company in-sourced video advertising with an agent pipeline that ends at human approval and replaced a video model within weeks.
    → Supports the claim that few connections preserve changeability.
  • A packaging company's warehouse shift scheduler was asked to take on payroll exceptions, union rules and three more sites; the original team fumbled and the tool survived only at its first location.
    → Supports the claim that scope expansion by non-authors leads to abandonment.
  • A Swiss enterprise software firm and a consumer-goods multinational keep AI-built tools narrow or reserved for own-team use, and route broader needs to central experts and professional developers.
    → Supports 'scale the approach, not the solution' and the governance boundary idea.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do AI-built prototypes that impress in a pilot fail to become reliable company tools, and how can organizations that build in-house avoid this?
    Three disciplines: involve engineers early (against myopia), keep connections few (against dependency) and hold scope to core users while scaling the approach (against scope creep).
  • ⭕ When should a company build rather than buy software now that AI makes building cheap?
    Only a conditional answer: buy is sometimes right; build only if someone understands the build well enough to avoid the three failure modes. No decision criteria or cost model.
  • ⭕ Who owns and maintains the many narrow, builder-owned tools over time as people move on?
    Implied: the builder-user keeps the knowledge local; no account of turnover, handover or retirement.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe evidence base is 11 companies where at least one author led or advised the project, mostly unnamed, with Roche as the only named example (and one author works at Roche). No cost, timeline or success-rate data are shown, no count of how many of the 11 stumbled versus succeeded, and no counterfactual (e.g., buying, or an engineer-built version) is compared, so the three remedies are plausible but not tested.
    Read next: Look for systematic studies of in-house AI-built tool outcomes (survey data on maintenance cost and abandonment, DORA-style capability research) and for independent case studies of build-vs-buy outcomes.
  • incompleteThe case for expert review assumes the AI-chosen architecture is opaque to its author, and treats the blind spot as fixed. It does not consider that agents can document, explain and test their own design, or that specs, tests and evals could narrow the myopia without a human engineer per six builders. The vibe-coding versus engineer-review dichotomy skips the agentic-engineering middle ground this corpus documents elsewhere.
    Read next: Read the spec-driven and agentic-engineering extractions (verification, harness, evals) as an alternative route to closing the same gap.
  • incomplete'Scale the approach, not the solution' is justified by knowledge locality (builders are users, so fixes stay local), but the article never addresses turnover, duplication across many narrow tools, data inconsistency, or who maintains a tool when its builder leaves. 'Governing the zoo' is described without any evidence of how well central triage works or how many requests it handles.
    Read next: Look at citizen-developer ownership and governance accounts (platform team models, internal-tool registries, FOSE talks) and at literature on internal tool sprawl and orphaned spreadsheets.
  • incompleteThe bank's failure is attributed to missing expertise, yet the account is one-sided and confounded: a new internal team, regulatory complexity and the vendor's own judgment (the vendor profited from the rebuild) are all in play. The article does not say whether a vibe-coding approach specifically caused the failure, or whether any internal build of this size would have struggled.
    Read next: Compare with insourcing and build-vs-buy accounts that separate capability-building from tooling, and with vendor-bias critiques of vendor-reported outcomes.
  • incompleteThe premise that ongoing maintenance can cost more than buying is asserted, with no unit economics: no comparison of token, hosting and engineer-review costs against license fees, and no estimate of when the crossover occurs.
    Read next: Look for AI unit-economics and total-cost-of-ownership analyses of AI-built internal tools versus SaaS.
