I'm done using AI

Brett Codes · article · 2026-08-10 Reader 🖥️

Agentic EngineeringIndividual AI AbstentionAI BacklashAI Environmental FootprintCapacity to Verify AI OutputDeveloper Experience & FlowEmotional Impact of AI WorkProfessional Identity
After using LLM-powered coding harnesses in earnest for over a year, the author concludes that the personal (mental-health, craft, deskilling), societal, and environmental harms of AI outweigh any productivity benefit it offers him, and that no moderate or selective level of use is viable — so he is stopping entirely.

practical · mixed — A first-person memoir/narrative structure in service of a practical conclusion (you — or at least I — should stop using AI). The autobiographical chronology and anecdotes (the ED visit, the hamster-wheel realization, the game-programming return) do the argumentative work that explicit premises only partly carry; the piece reads as testimony more than as a formal case.

Key terms

  • agentic programming Agentic Engineering
    The current generation of LLM-powered coding harnesses (e.g., Claude Code) that can be given a task and autonomously write, edit, and build software with minimal human editing — distinct from the earlier autocomplete-based (Copilot) or inline-chat (Cursor) workflows.
  • cared for
    A quality attribute of software determined by the degree of human attention, judgment, and craft invested in it — not by feature count, build speed, or test coverage.
  • net harm
    AI's aggregate negative impact (personal, societal, environmental) exceeds its aggregate benefit, for the author specifically and, in his stated opinion, for society generally.
  • delusional
    A state of impaired judgment produced by treating the AI chat as a trusted authority/friend for medical, career, and business decisions — leading to costly, unnecessary real-world actions (the midnight ED visit).

Arguments

I should stop using AI entirely, including for trivial/moderate use.
Explicit premises:
  • Using AI for coding caused depression, apathy, existential dread, and worse software.
  • Relying on AI chat for personal decisions was actively harmful (the ED anecdote).
  • AI carries severe environmental costs and is built on stolen training data.
  • The cost of using LLM tooling is way too high and not worth it for myself or society.
  • Attempted moderate/trivial-only use did not hold; he kept reverting to using it for everything.
Implicit premises:
  • Any level of use signals demand and is complicit in perpetuating AI's harms.
  • Personal well-being and craft/meaning take priority over productivity gains.
  • The harms are inherent to how current LLM tools work, not fixable by better discipline, tooling, or moderated use.
  • An individual's abstention is a meaningful and sufficient personal response to a systemic, industry-scale problem.
Inferences:
  • If moderate use still counts as demand-signaling complicity, the same logic would implicate almost any professional tool with comparable supply-chain or environmental costs — a generalization the author doesn't draw or examine.
AI made me a worse programmer. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • He stopped writing tests and code himself, becoming a reviewer and QA tester.
  • It's impossible to review the quantity of code AI produces.
  • A different AI is used to review the AI-written code anyway.
Implicit premises:
  • Hands-on production of code is necessary to maintain skill, understanding, and vigilance (a skill atrophies without practice).
Inferences:
  • If even the author — an experienced, motivated reviewer — cannot keep pace with AI-generated code volume, the 'human review as safety net' claim used to justify agentic coding is structurally, not just personally, unreliable.

Narratives

  • Sick at midnight, the author describes his flu symptoms to an AI chatbot, which tells him to go to the Emergency Department; he waits two hours, is told by the doctor he didn't need to come, and it costs him hundreds of dollars.
    → Concrete evidence for the claim that over-reliance on AI chat for real decisions is unhealthy, costly, and 'delusional' — the emotional anchor of the personal-harm argument.
  • At a previous job, a newly hired 'true believer' product manager began pushing AI-driven changes without understanding the broader system, and AI adoption 'spread like a virus' through the organization.
    → Implicitly supports the proposition that AI adoption inside organizations is often driven by conviction/hype rather than by engineers' own technical assessment.
  • By connecting Linear to Claude Code, the author had it build a non-trivial project start to finish without editing a line of code — faster than he could have done it himself, and 'barely had to think.'
    → Establishes his credibility (he has seen the technology at its most capable) while simultaneously functioning as the turning point that made the loss of authorship and craft viscerally clear to him.
  • During the low point ('the nadir'), he returned to game programming as a hobby — finishing a Playdate coding book, making small games, and building a 2D Lua game engine ('Usagi') in Rust, all without AI.
    → Functions as a natural-experiment comparison: coding by hand, without AI, restored the enjoyment and sense of ownership that agentic coding had eroded — direct evidence for the craft/care thesis.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Is AI coding assistance worth its personal cost, for me?
    No — the personal, societal, and environmental costs outweigh the productivity benefit.
  • ✅ Can moderate or selective AI use be a viable middle path, instead of full adoption or full abstention?
    No — tried it, reverted to using it for everything, and concluded that any use still signals demand and complicity.
  • ⭕ What will happen professionally if I stop using AI at work (will I be terminated)?
    Explicitly left open — 'I don't know what consequences will come from this decision... it's possible I will be terminated.'
  • ✅ Does a decision that is well-evidenced as personally right for him (given his specific mental-health response and job situation) generalize to a universal claim about AI being a net harm for 'society'?
    The author treats it as resolved in the affirmative ('AI is unquestionably a net harm... for myself or society'), but does not distinguish the two claims or argue the generalization.

Gaps

  • incompleteNo engagement with the possibility of disciplined, bounded AI use as a genuine middle path — e.g., practitioners who report AI making them better when paired with strict practices like TDD-with-the-agent, hand-coding the architectural foundation, or treating the agent as a skeptically-supervised junior. The author's 'all or nothing' claim is presented as an empirical fact about his own experience, not tested against how others have made moderate use work.
    Read next: Read against a skeptical-but-productive practitioner account (e.g. Charity Majors on AI for development) to see whether the all-or-nothing framing is a personal limitation or a structural feature of the tools.
  • illogicalThe piece slides between a personal, well-evidenced decision ('this was ruining my life,' 'I couldn't keep working that way') and a universal moral claim ('AI is unquestionably a net harm... for myself or society,' 'I do not like this world where people are using AI') without separating the two or arguing the generalization. The strong first-person evidence supports the personal conclusion; it does not by itself establish the societal one.
    Read next: Compare with extract-the-growing-ai-backlash, which at least attempts an (unweighted) list of societal harms rather than inferring them from personal experience.
  • incompleteThe environmental and 'stolen sources' (training-data) claims are asserted ('I started to learn more about the true cost of AI...') with no citation, data, or numbers — much thinner than the quantified treatment available elsewhere in the corpus (water/land/e-waste figures, the rebound effect).
    Read next: extract-ai-environmental-costs-un supplies the missing quantitative grounding for exactly this claim.
  • incompleteNo exploration of why his experience diverges so sharply from developers who report genuine gains from AI-assisted coding (the joy-gap split) — role, team size, codebase age, seniority, and personal disposition toward pace/craft could all plausibly explain the difference, but none is examined as a variable; his conclusion is presented as though it should generalize.
    Read next: extract-ai-joy-gap-developers maps exactly this polarization (greenfield/small-team joy vs. legacy/enterprise misery) and would let a reader place Brett's specific situation (small team, <10 developers, legacy TypeScript/Ruby codebase) on that spectrum.
  • incompleteThe demand-signaling logic used to justify total personal abstention ('using it less... means I'd still be...contributing to the harms') is never examined for its actual efficacy: one individual's non-use is very unlikely to measurably affect an industry-scale demand curve, and the piece never considers or rules out collective/regulatory levers (which other corpus material treats as the operative lever for reducing AI's harms) as more effective than individual abstention.
    Read next: extract-the-growing-ai-backlash#p8 argues responsible regulation, not individual choice, is what could have mitigated most of these harms — worth reading against Brett's individual-abstention framing directly.
