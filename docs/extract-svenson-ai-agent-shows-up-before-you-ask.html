The AI agent that shows up before you ask

Ernie Svenson · email · 2026-09-08 Reader 🖥️

AI Employee vs Tool FramingLong-Running & Managed AgentsPrompting as CommunicationVendor Self-Reported Metrics
An AI agent that lives inside the team's existing chat tool (Slack/Teams) and proactively checks in, follows up, and does unprompted work is a qualitatively different and more valuable kind of AI than a chatbot you have to remember to use, because it supplies the initiative and follow-through that busy, distracted humans otherwise fail to supply themselves.

practical · narrative — A first-person vendor-adjacent testimonial (Svenson writes an AI-adoption newsletter for lawyers and appears to have an affiliate/referral relationship with the tool). The entire argument is carried by one client anecdote (Sarah Persich's lead-tracking scorecard) plus Svenson's own personal-productivity anecdote; there is no theoretical claim about how or why the agent works, only a practical claim that readers should try it.

Key terms

  • proactive Long-Running & Managed Agents
    An agent that initiates contact and work without being prompted in the moment — it asks whether you did the thing, asks what would move it forward, and offers to do part of it, rather than waiting for a command or firing a static, ignorable reminder.
  • autonomous
    Used as a lesser, contrasting category: a tool you give a task to and it finishes it on its own, but only after being given the task.
  • capable colleague
    The frame Svenson wants readers to adopt for the tool: a fourth participant in an existing Slack workspace who reads everything, remembers everything, and works while the human sleeps — implicitly a peer, not a subordinate instrument.

Arguments

Readers should try Viktor (or an agent like it) because it will surface and drive work they would otherwise never get to. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • A real firm (via Sarah Persich) used it to grade every intake call against a rubric and got a full report back in ten minutes, work that would otherwise never happen.
  • The same agent then proactively extended itself to a new problem (leads slipping out of the case-management system) without being separately re-commissioned for that specific task.
  • It is embedded in Slack/Teams, so there is no separate onboarding friction.
  • The starting cost is low ($100 in credits) and the ongoing cost cited by one other user ($500/month) is modest relative to firm staffing costs.
Implicit premises:
  • A single client's positive experience, reported secondhand by someone whose business model includes selling deeper coverage of the same tool, generalizes to the reader's own firm and workflows.
  • The grading and reporting the agent produces is accurate enough to act on, even though its accuracy was never independently checked by anyone quoted in the piece.
  • The value of catching a lawyer 'cheating' (the closing anecdote) is a valid proxy for the tool's actual return on investment.
  • Ambient, always-on monitoring of team communications, call recordings, and case-management data by a third-party AI agent carries no worrying data-access, privacy, or vendor-lock-in considerations worth mentioning to the reader.
Inferences:
  • If the agent's usefulness comes chiefly from ambient access to Slack/Teams history, call recordings, and case-management data, then the amount and sensitivity of data it can see is a design choice with real risk, not a side effect — the piece never discusses what access was granted or what could go wrong.
  • If 'proactive' follow-up on unfinished tasks is genuinely more effective than reminders, the mechanism driving that (schedule? event trigger reading Slack activity? memory of prior conversations?) is the actual engineering artifact worth understanding — but it is never described.

Narratives

  • Sarah Persich gives Viktor her intake-team training rubric and points it at the firm's call recordings; ten minutes later she has a per-agent, per-call grading report that would have taken weeks by hand and therefore was simply never being done.
    → Carries the piece's central claim — that proactive/autonomous agents don't just speed up existing work, they surface work that was invisible because nobody would ever do it manually — without Svenson having to argue for it abstractly.
  • Svenson describes setting Slack reminders for himself, repeatedly snoozing them and feeling mildly guilty, versus Viktor which asks whether he did the thing, asks what would move it forward, and offers to help — 'I supplied resistance. Viktor supplied the push.'
    → Personalizes and generalizes the proactive/reminder distinction beyond the law-firm case, implicitly arguing that the mechanism (unprompted follow-up with an offer of help) works on individual procrastination, not just organizational blind spots.
  • The unnamed lawyer who introduced Svenson to Viktor is 'having a record year' and says using it 'felt like cheating.'
    → Closing emotional payoff that substitutes for a quantified outcome — implicitly supports the proposition that the tool materially drives revenue, without any figures.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How is a 'proactive' agent different from an 'autonomous' one that you give a task to?
    Autonomous means you assign a task and it finishes it; proactive means it initiates check-ins and offers to help without being asked in the moment.
  • ✅ What removes the usual friction of deploying AI across a team?
    Embedding the agent inside the chat tool the team already uses (Slack/Teams), so access, context, and permissions are inherited rather than rebuilt.
  • ⭕ What actually triggers Viktor's proactive check-ins — a schedule, a Slack-activity listener, a memory of prior commitments, a webhook off the case-management system?
  • ⭕ What data access (call recordings, case-management system, full Slack/Teams history) does the agent require to do this, and what governs or limits that access?
  • ⭕ How accurate or reliable is the agent's grading and reporting — does anyone check its call-grading output against a human reviewer, and how often is it wrong?
  • ⭕ Is $500/month (or the reader's likely equivalent) a good deal relative to a measurable outcome, or just 'not much' relative to unquantified staffing costs?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece never explains the actual trigger mechanism behind 'proactive' — whether Viktor polls on a schedule, listens for Slack/Teams events, holds a memory of open commitments it checks against, or something else. This is precisely the engineering detail (trigger source, state it must persist between check-ins, how it decides *when* to surface) that would let a practitioner evaluate or replicate the pattern, and it is entirely absent — replaced by anthropomorphic description ('it asks,' 'it offers').

  • incompleteThe agent evidently has standing access to a law firm's call recordings, case-management system, and full Slack/Teams communications in order to do this work. The piece treats this as a pure feature (visibility, follow-through) and never once discusses access scope, data retention, client-confidentiality exposure, or what happens if the agent's report or its underlying data access is wrong or leaked — an omission that is especially conspicuous for a law-firm audience bound by confidentiality obligations.
    Read next: Client-confidentiality and data-governance material for AI tools with standing access to firm systems; the corpus's data-sovereignty and agent-security concepts.
  • incompleteNo claim in the piece is independently verified: the call-grading accuracy, the ten-minute turnaround, the $500/month figure (attributed secondhand to a third party, 'efficient.app'), and the 'record year' outcome are all reported at one remove, by an author who monetizes deeper coverage of the same tool via a paid podcast/community. There is no disclosure of a financial or affiliate relationship, and no comparison to what a human team, or a competing tool, would have produced.
    Read next: The corpus's existing material on vendor self-reported metrics and methodology-free adoption testimonials as a recurring genre.
  • incompleteThe piece adopts the 'capable colleague, not a chatbot' framing approvingly and without qualification, treating the anthropomorphic frame itself as evidence of the tool's quality ('a fourth participant'). It never considers whether that framing might reduce the human oversight the agent's outputs (call grades, lead-slippage reports) actually need before being acted on.
