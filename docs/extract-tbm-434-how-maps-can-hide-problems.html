TBM 434: How Maps Can Hide Problems

John Cutler (The Beautiful Mess, Substack) · email · 2026-08-01 Reader 🖥️

AI as AmplifierOrganizational DebtOrganizational LegibilitySociotechnical ArchitectureWorld Model (Organizational)
Organizational coherence — how cleanly an organization's separate 'maps' (goals, teaming, funding, ownership, technology) line up with one another — determines how much context can be left implicit; when the maps stop lining up, organizations tend to build sophisticated coping mechanisms (translators, fixers, tools, and now AI) that relieve enough of the resulting pain to let the underlying misalignment go unaddressed, so a map's proper use is not to be trusted or loved but to be read for where the gaps are and which of them are worth closing.

practical · mixed — An extended analogy (the city with multiple map layers) carries a theoretical claim about how organizational coherence works, but the piece closes on an explicit imperative ('use it to refactor what you see') — the theory is in service of a directive about how to treat maps and misalignment, so it reads as practical/argumentative with an expository/narrative core.

Key terms

  • Coherent (organization)
    A state where strategy, structure, technology, and incentives map cleanly onto one another, so that any one representation (a team, a goal) reliably lets you infer the others without translation.
  • Map / map layers
    Any of the organization's distinct representations — goal hierarchy, team structure, funding model, customer segmentation, product taxonomy — treated as a literal cartographic layer that can line up with, or cut across, the other layers.
  • Refactor what you see
    Use the map's revealed discrepancies as a trigger to change the underlying organizational reality it represents — not merely redraw or improve the map itself.

  • explicitCoherence lowers the energy required to make sense of what is going on — for humans, and for humans using AI.conf: high
  • explicitIn an incoherent organization, insight gained from one map does not transfer to another map — each switch in frame requires reorienting, translating, and re-deriving context from scratch.conf: high

Arguments

Investing in tools and specialists that help people navigate organizational incoherence can be counterproductive, because it can remove the pressure that would otherwise force the incoherence itself to be fixed. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Incoherent organizations generate real, felt pain (constant surprise, having to reorient between frames).
  • Organizations respond to that pain by building translation/documentation/fixer capability and navigation tools.
  • That capability measurably lowers the pain.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizational change happens mainly under pressure from unaddressed pain; remove enough of the pain and the misalignment loses its forcing function.
  • The people investing in coping mechanisms are not weighing (or are discounting) the long-run cost of leaving the underlying incoherence in place — otherwise they would prioritize the structural fix over the workaround.
Inferences:
  • By this logic, the mere existence of elaborate coping infrastructure (fixers, dashboards, integration layers, cross-functional forums) is itself diagnostic evidence of unresolved incoherence — the more such infrastructure an organization has built, the more incoherent its underlying structure probably is.
Using AI to smooth conflicting organizational information into a single coherent-looking output is likely to make organizational decision-making worse, not better, even though it feels like progress.
Explicit premises:
  • AI can surface and compare an organization's different maps and can call out where they are incoherent.
  • AI cannot make the underlying conflicting structures, incentives, goals, and definitions actually line up.
  • When AI smooths over those contradictions, it produces the appearance of alignment.
Implicit premises:
  • An appearance of alignment reduces the felt urgency to resolve the real underlying conflict — the same mechanism as ordinary coping tools, applied specifically to AI as a summarizer/harmonizer.
  • Decision-makers act on the coherence an artifact (dashboard, summary, 'single source of truth' document) projects, not necessarily on the messier ground truth beneath it.
Inferences:
  • AI-generated organizational 'single source of truth' documents and dashboards deserve active skepticism — precisely because AI is good at manufacturing internally consistent narratives out of inconsistent inputs, such artifacts are a distinct risk for concealing incoherence rather than a neutral productivity aid.

Narratives

  • A consultant friend describes being 'completely blindsided' meeting with teams in an incoherent organization, having to sift through all their work just to make sense of it, and repeatedly asking 'You're doing what? Why? For who? Who made that call?'
    → Grounds the abstract 'less coherent organizations' section in a concrete practitioner voice, showing what losing your bearings actually feels like from the outside — supports the proposition that insight from one part of an incoherent org doesn't transfer to another.
  • The extended city-with-map-layers analogy, run twice: once showing streets/utilities/transit/zoning/school-districts lining up cleanly (coherent version), and once showing them cutting across each other — school districts splitting neighborhoods, buses and trains following different corridors, 'downtown' split across municipalities and utility providers.
    → Carries almost the entire argument; the claim that coherence lets you leave things implicit, and that incoherence forces constant reorientation, is argued through the analogy rather than demonstrated with organizational data.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do some organizations let people reason confidently from partial information while others force constant re-orientation?
    Because their underlying structures (goals, teams, funding, ownership) either map cleanly onto one another (coherent) or cut across each other (incoherent) — coherence, not information volume, is the variable.
  • ✅ Why don't organizations fix chronic structural misalignment even when people clearly feel its pain?
    Because sophisticated coping mechanisms (documentation, translators, fixers, tools) lower the pain enough that the forcing function for real repair disappears.
  • ✅ What should AI's role be with respect to organizational incoherence?
    Surfacing and comparing maps and calling out incoherence — not reconciling it; and when AI is used to smooth contradictions into a single narrative, it can actively worsen things by faking alignment.
  • ⭕ How do you actually decide which of the many misaligned gaps between maps are 'the most consequential ones' worth realigning?
  • ⭕ Is coherence something an organization designs and can therefore maintain deliberately, or something that erodes as a natural byproduct of growth and change and must be continually re-established?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article's operational core — 'notice where the maps have stopped lining up, decide which gaps matter, and continually bring the most consequential ones back into alignment' — is asserted as the goal but never demonstrated. There is no criterion, example, or method for how to actually identify which misalignments are 'consequential' versus tolerable.
    Read next: Look for a prioritization method for organizational-design interventions — e.g. Meadows on leverage points, or Cutler's own later pieces if he operationalizes this; also worth comparing against sociotechnical-architecture material (reverse-Conway maneuver) for how others rank which structural gap to close first.
  • incompleteThe piece treats incoherence as something to be discovered rather than as the predictable output of uncoordinated organizational growth — it never discusses whether coherence is something you architect once (a reverse-Conway-style deliberate design act) or something that must be continually re-governed as headcount, product lines, and reporting structures change over time.
    Read next: Pair with Conway's-law / reverse-Conway-maneuver material and with organizational-debt framing for the mechanism of how misalignment accumulates, rather than just how it's diagnosed once it exists.
  • incompleteThe claim that AI 'smoothing over contradictions... may exacerbate the underlying tensions while producing the appearance of alignment' — the single sharpest claim in the essay — is asserted with no example of a real decision it made worse, no mechanism for how the exacerbation compounds, and no way to distinguish AI-smoothing from AI-surfacing in practice.
    Read next: Look for concrete case studies of AI-generated 'single source of truth' documents or dashboards driving bad organizational decisions, or literature on automation bias / cognitive-surrender applied specifically to leadership and strategy artifacts rather than individual cognition.
  • incompleteThe piece does not engage adjacent, well-developed frameworks for exactly this problem — Conway's Law, DDD's bounded contexts (which are literally about where one semantic 'map' of a domain stops applying), or organizational network analysis — despite the map metaphor mapping almost one-to-one onto them.
