Agentic AIMulti-Agent Orchestration
The five AI capability classes — machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, AI agents, and agentic AI — form a strict dependency hierarchy, and it is teams skipping the foundational layers (1-4) to chase the buzzword at the top (layer 5) that explains why so many deployed "AI agents" fail in production.

theoretical · mixed — The post is mostly a definitional taxonomy (five layers of the AI stack) — expository in that it asserts what the AI stack *is*. But it pivots to an unsupported argumentative claim (skipping layers 1-4 is "exactly why" agents fail in production) that the taxonomy is built to license, so the form is mixed rather than purely expository. It also carries a thin practical corollary (know your layers before deploying agents) but never states what to do about it beyond a rhetorical question, so the practical thrust stays implicit and unelaborated.

Key terms

  • Machine learning (Layer 1)
    Systems fed labelled examples that learn patterns and predict — exemplified by fraud detection, recommendations, search ranking; framed as "old, but everywhere."
  • Deep learning (Layer 2)
    Neural networks that discover which features matter without hand-engineering; credited as the origin of embeddings and the reason semantic search works.
  • Generative AI (Layer 3)
    Next-token prediction scaled up until the model acquires language, reasoning, and code ability — the layer that "gave you ChatGPT."
  • AI agents (Layer 4) Agentic AI
    Generative AI equipped to act rather than only answer — tool use, function calling, memory — defined by doing the task instead of describing it.
  • Agentic AI (Layer 5) Agentic AI
    Multiple agents working together with real autonomy, planning, and self-correction toward a goal, with a human only "loosely in the loop" — explicitly distinguished from single AI agents by multiplicity and autonomy.
  • "The stack"
    The author's own framing device: treating these five capability classes as ordered, cumulative layers (like a technology stack) rather than as five separate, loosely related concepts.

Arguments

Most "AI agents" fail in production because teams skip layers one through four of the AI stack. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Each layer is built on the one below it (strict dependency hierarchy).
  • You cannot understand/use agents without understanding prediction, representation, and generation.
  • Most people are trying to adopt layer five while ignoring layers one through four.
Implicit premises:
  • The relevant "understanding" of layers 1-4 is conceptual/definitional (the kind this LinkedIn post itself supplies), not hands-on engineering skill with data pipelines, model training, or evals.
  • A team's conceptual gap about the lower layers is the (or a primary) causal mechanism behind specific production failure modes, rather than those failures being better explained by other known causes (unbounded agent loops, missing guardrails, weak evals, brittle tool schemas, context management, prompt injection, etc. — all discussed elsewhere in this reader's corpus).
  • "Falling over in production" is a uniform phenomenon with one dominant cause, rather than a grab-bag of distinct failure modes with different root causes.
Inferences:
  • If the stated cause is correct, the fix should be structured team education in ML/deep learning/generative AI fundamentals before agent deployment — the author never draws this out; the post ends in a rhetorical question instead of a prescription.
  • If the dependency hierarchy is real, layer 5 (agentic AI) should be strictly harder to get right than layer 4 (AI agents), yet the causal claim is stated about "AI agents" (layer 4) specifically — creating a slight mismatch between where the post locates the failure (layer 4, "AI agents") and where it locates the ambition (layer 5, "agentic AI").

Gaps

The rest of the post (the taxonomy itself) is plausible and even useful as a teaching device, but the causal punchline it's built to deliver is asserted, not demonstrated.

The closing question — "which layer does your team actually understand today?" — invites the reader to self-assess on literacy, but the claimed failure mode (agents breaking in production) is an engineering-competence problem, not a literacy problem. The post never distinguishes which kind of "understanding" its own prescription requires.
