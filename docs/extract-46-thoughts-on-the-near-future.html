46 thoughts on the near future

bayes · tweet · 2026-06-30 Reader 🖥️

The near future is defined by an already-underway, multi-order-of-magnitude 'algorithmic takeoff' in AI capability — extending from software into robotics, science, and physical production — that will compress vast economic and institutional change into a short, poorly governed window, producing genuine winners and losers, real risks to human agency and strategic stability, and no consensus (including from the author) on how to manage any of it responsibly.

theoretical · mixed — A numbered list of 46 discrete predictions organized under eight thematic headers (Intelligence; The intelligence supply chain; Robotics; Progress; Capital and Production; Culture and Psychology; Coordination; Power, violence, security, liberty). Individual items range from bare assertion ('X will happen') to occasional explicit chains of reasoning (the algorithmic-depth moat argument, the MAD argument, the jobs/disintermediation argument). There is no single connected essay-level argument; the unity is a shared worldview and timeline applied across many independent domains, not a step-by-step case for one conclusion.

Key terms

  • algorithmic OOMs (orders of magnitude)
    Discrete, multiplicative jumps in the efficiency of converting compute into 'intelligence output' from algorithmic (not hardware) progress alone; the author estimates 4-10 such jumps remain before the universe's physical/computational limits are reached.
  • early takeoff
    The claim that AI-improving-AI (recursive self-improvement) has already begun and is accelerating, distinct from a future hypothetical threshold.
  • algorithmic depth
    How much unexploited efficiency remains latent in deep-learning practice and theory itself, as distinct from raw compute scale — the variable that determines whether labs' research advantages are durable or catch-up-able via distillation.
  • Move 37 moment
    A generalized reference to AlphaGo's famous move 37 — a superhuman, initially incomprehensible insight — extended to predict that every domain of technical human endeavor will have such a moment, and that these will rapidly become unremarkable.
  • vulnerable world (Bostromian)
    Invokes Nick Bostrom's 'vulnerable world hypothesis' — that some technology levels enable catastrophic misuse by default — as a live, unresolved possibility for the current AI moment, requiring extreme surveillance/control ('panopticon') absent another solution.
  • permanent underclass
    A durable segment of humanity locked out of contributing economically to AI-automated production — defined by the author specifically as restricted agency rather than necessarily restricted income, distinguishing it from ordinary poverty.

Arguments

There are many algorithmic orders-of-magnitude of intelligence-production gains left to realize, and their eventual realization will look like a discontinuous, unanticipated jump relative to current expectations. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Institutions, markets, governments, militaries and companies are all interpreting AI's trajectory in terms of recent-past production efficiencies and regularities.
  • Even purportedly 'RSI-pilled' frontier labs seem to expect business-as-usual with 'agent in a loop' rather than a genuine phase change.
Implicit premises:
  • Algorithmic progress will continue on something like its recent trajectory rather than saturating soon.
  • The physical/computational limits of intelligence production ('what the universe will actually let us do') are much higher than current practice suggests.
  • The author's own numeric estimate (4-10 OOMs) is treated as a reasonable central estimate despite being explicitly labeled a guess rather than derived from cited data.
Inferences:
  • If most institutions are extrapolating from the wrong (too-conservative) baseline, then whenever the remaining algorithmic headroom is realized, it will appear as a sudden, unanticipated jump rather than a continuation of an already-priced-in trend.
Whether frontier AI labs retain a durable competitive moat is genuinely unresolved and depends on how algorithmically 'deep' deep learning practice turns out to be. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Distillation, data, and time can eventually catch up to raw compute scale, potentially slowly.
  • Automated AI experimentation is more distributable than full-scale training runs, since it doesn't require monolithic datacenter buildouts.
Implicit premises:
  • Research findings, once discovered, diffuse faster than compute budgets can be defended against replication.
  • No lab successfully 'locks down' its internal AI-researcher models to prevent this diffusion.
Inferences:
  • Conditional fork: if deep learning stays algorithmically shallow, secrets become cheap to discover and the moat erodes toward open source/distillation parity; if it deepens with scale, each increment of automation buys an increasingly unreachable advantage — and current evidence is treated as ambiguous between the two branches.
Mutually assured destruction (MAD) is not a structural given going forward. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • MAD as currently understood is based on 20th- and early-21st-century technology.
  • A roughly millennium's worth of technological change is coming in a short period.
  • Error-rate tolerance for achieving a decisive strategic advantage is very low and potentially infeasible.
Implicit premises:
  • Rapid, compressed capability change makes some form of decisive first-strike or defense-neutralizing advantage newly reachable within the relevant timeframe.
  • Existing nuclear-era deterrence logic does not automatically transfer to new capability classes (e.g., AI-enabled counterforce, missile defense, or cyber capabilities).
Inferences:
  • Rapid capability change → possibility of decisive advantage → the assumption underlying deterrence stability (that no side can achieve a clean advantage) breaks down.
Job survival under AI-driven automation depends on a job's degree of disintermediation, outcompetability, and fungibility, and what fraction of humanity retains employment is genuinely uncertain rather than confidently high or confidently zero. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Some jobs have zero intermediate layers between worker and consumer (TikTok influencer, doctor) and are harder to route an AI substitute around directly than jobs with many layers (factory worker).
  • Humans care about other humans, so relational and entertainment work may retain value even as the technical need for humans in production falls.
Implicit premises:
  • Legal/professional cartelization (e.g., medical licensing) can preserve some human roles independent of underlying AI capability.
  • Demand-side collapse (mass unemployment eroding purchasing power faster than productivity gains can fund UBI/UHI) is treated as a conditioning tail risk rather than the central scenario.
Inferences:
  • The layers-of-intermediation heuristic is used to predict, job by job, which categories of work are more or less likely to survive automation.

Narratives

  • Superhuman AI doctors available for $20/month plus a la carte testing, offering significantly improved medical technology, contrasted against the possibility that medical cartelization keeps 'being a doctor' a great profession regardless.
    → Makes the abstract disintermediation/fungibility argument about job survival concrete for a specific, high-status profession, illustrating how demand for even elite cognitive labor could collapse — or be artificially propped up by institutional capture rather than technical necessity.
  • A rhetorical question posed as a resource-allocation dilemma: are GB300 GPUs more valuable serving GLM5.2 or Fable, and is it more valuable doing non-frontier research in an academic lab or building 'Mythos 2' inside Anthropic?
    → Illustrates the opportunity-cost argument for why academic and open-source AI research may remain compute-starved even with capital in hand — the market allocates compute to wherever demand (and pay) is greatest, which currently means the labs.
  • A three-way comparison: a TikTok influencer has zero intermediate layers between them and their audience, a doctor also has zero layers, but a factory worker sits behind many layers of supply chain.
    → Supplies the concrete typology behind the disintermediation heuristic used to predict which categories of job survive AI-driven automation.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How accurately do current institutions, markets, and governments understand the trajectory of AI capability?
    The author asserts they are systematically extrapolating from the wrong (too-conservative) baseline and will be blindsided, but this is stated as a claim rather than demonstrated with evidence.
  • ⭕ What happens to human employment as AI and robots automate both cognitive and physical labor?
    The author explicitly frames this as an open question and declines to give a confident percentage, offering a disintermediation heuristic instead of a resolved answer.
  • ⭕ Can international coordination (treaties, GPU counting) durably control the spread and risks of advanced AI capability?
    The author says such coordination is 'likely a good idea' but explicitly doubts it will be achieved, because GPUs (unlike nuclear weapons) are too broadly useful to restrict by consensus.
  • ✅ Is a pause or slowdown of frontier AI development currently advisable?
    The author states a personal position against a pause at this time, citing disruption to other necessary parts of the transition and the reality of non-complying adversaries — reasons given, but not developed into a full argument.
  • ⭕ Could AI-driven change produce a stable but unjust world (a 'permanent underclass' and its 'overclass' mirror), and is this being adequately discussed?
    The author explicitly flags this as under-discussed, criticizes American politics for handling it poorly, and self-implicates in the wealth-doublespeak critique — but does not resolve what should be done about it.
  • ⭕ Will humans retain a justifiable basis to govern one another in a world with superhuman AI?
    The author raises the possibility that humans may have 'no justifiable rights to govern' but explicitly declines to resolve it, framing it as something that 'might become more important to think about.'
  • ⭕ Does mutually assured destruction (MAD) remain a stable strategic equilibrium as AI accelerates military and technological capability?
    The author asserts MAD 'is not a given' and calls it one of the most serious topics available, but offers no concrete mechanism, timeline, or path to a resolution.
  • ⭕ Will frontier AI labs end up under direct state (nationalized) control?
    The author offers a personal lean (skeptical of nationalization's compatibility with the American system) but explicitly says paths to it 'don't seem off-limits,' leaving the question open.

Gaps

  • uninformedThe central quantitative claim — 'many algorithmic OOMs left... maybe up to ten, with four to seven seeming more likely' — is stated with false numeric precision but sourced to nothing beyond the author's own intuition ('my guess,' 'I suspect'). No historical algorithmic-efficiency growth-rate data (e.g., the widely reported compute-efficiency doubling times tracked by groups like Epoch AI) is cited to calibrate whether four to ten further OOMs is plausible on any observed trend, and the piece never distinguishes measured compute-efficiency OOMs from the much fuzzier, unmeasured notion of 'intelligence output' OOMs it actually claims. This directly fails the definitional-rigor bar this corpus's Gary Marcus material applies to comparably sweeping claims (a claim this consequential 'carries a burden of definitional precision before it can be meaningfully evaluated at all').
    Read next: Compare directly against extract-gary-marcus-not-reached-singularity, which insists on defining terms before evaluating claims of this magnitude, and against any corpus item with actual scaling-law or benchmark data on measured algorithmic-efficiency trends.
  • illogicalThought 5 dismisses the concern that long-horizon agentic tasks require correspondingly long-horizon training by re-labeling the same empirical phenomenon (the METR agent-task-length curve) as 'error correction' rather than the 'error accumulation' framing it is rebutting, without offering an independent benchmark, ablation, or mechanism to distinguish the two readings. No alternative explanation (e.g., that recent gains reflect narrow task/tooling improvements rather than a generalized error-correction capability) is considered or ruled out before the stronger claim ('generalization in time exists... long tasks are not made of longness') is asserted.
    Read next: Cross-reference with any corpus material directly engaging LeCun's error-accumulation argument or presenting independent analysis of the METR time-horizon benchmark's drivers.
  • incompleteThe robotics-scale-up claim ('order of 100M robots/yr by 2030') leans on ~100M cars/year and ~1B smartphones/year as manufacturing-scale precedents, and separately treats the ~10M/year drone market as proof that 10M/yr humanoid robots is 'definitely achievable' — but drones (largely small, non-autonomous, low-complexity hardware) and humanoid robots (explicitly the subject under discussion) are not remotely comparable in actuator/sensor cost, manufacturing complexity, or software maturity. No current humanoid-robot production figures are cited to show how many orders of magnitude of scale-up '100M/yr by 2030' actually implies, leaving a several-orders-of-magnitude, ~4-year production ramp asserted with no supply-chain or capital-feasibility analysis behind it.
    Read next: No corpus material on robotics manufacturing currently exists to compare against; this is flagged as a standalone gap pending future extraction of robotics-specific sources.
  • incompleteThe job-survival heuristic (disintermediation/outcompetition/fungibility) — despite the author's own admission that 'this analysis is quite subtle and this paragraph is not going to do it justice' — is tested against only three anecdotal examples (TikTok influencer, doctor, factory worker) with no reference to labor-economics literature on historical automation waves (e.g., agriculture's employment share falling from roughly 40% to 2% of the US workforce over a century, or manufacturing employment trends since the 1970s) that would let a reader calibrate plausible timeframes or magnitudes for AI-specific labor disruption. This corpus's own extract-arvind-narayanan-ai-isnt-all-that-revolutionary reaches a more developed (though still incomplete, per an existing open gap) decide/execute/deliver decomposition of the same problem; bayes doesn't reach that level of mechanism, let alone cite data.
    Read next: Compare against extract-arvind-narayanan-ai-isnt-all-that-revolutionary#p4 and #p14, and the existing corpus gap noting Narayanan himself doesn't explain the wage mechanism by which scarce human time would command a livable wage.
  • incompleteThought 4 in Culture and Psychology diagnoses a wealth 'doublespeak' (people who say AI benefits everyone but wouldn't trade their own position for a random person's) and unusually applies this critique to the author himself. But the same reflexive scrutiny is not extended to the piece's own epistemics: nearly every uncertain judgment call across the other 45 points (how many OOMs remain, how fast robots scale, whether a pause is wise, whether nationalization happens) resolves toward 'faster/bigger/more capable than consensus expects' — also the reading most flattering to, and most aligned with the visible incentives of, someone with insider familiarity with frontier AI (the piece name-drops GB300s, Anthropic, GLM5.2, Fable, and 'Mythos 2' with specificity). The self-awareness about wealth-doublespeak is not turned on the analogous possibility that consistently forecasting acceleration is itself a predictable output of the author's own position and incentives.
    Read next: No direct corpus parallel exists; flag as a standalone critical gap about author-incentive disclosure in AI-maximalist forecasting content generally.
  • illogicalThe thread simultaneously asserts (a) that institutions, markets, and governments are systematically blindsided and unable to correctly track AI's current trajectory (thought 1), and (b) confidently prescribes fairly sophisticated institutional responses to a faster future — international treaties with GPU counting, a CERN-model-style pause calculus, and judgments about the feasibility of lab nationalization — without reconciling why institutions incapable of even tracking current progress would suddenly become capable of designing and enforcing these more demanding coordination mechanisms in time.
    Read next: Compare against extract-arvind-narayanan-ai-isnt-all-that-revolutionary#p9, which argues durably preventing dangerous-capability diffusion would require sustained, effectively authoritarian global control precisely because institutional coordination at this level is so hard to sustain.
