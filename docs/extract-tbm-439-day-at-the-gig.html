TBM 439: Day At The Gig...

John Cutler from The Beautiful Mess · email · 2026-09-11 Reader 🖥️

AI SlopContext EngineeringKnowledge BaseMental Models & UnderstandingMultiplayer vs Single-Player AIOrganizational Legibility
AI can accelerate the mechanical formalization of sensemaking artifacts a person has already understood, but it cannot solve the harder problem underneath — telling which of an organization's proliferating artifacts still reflect current reality — so genuine organizational context remains something you have to actively create through your own exploration and other people's tacit knowledge, not something AI can spoon-feed you.

theoretical · narrative — Cutler explicitly declines to turn the experience into 'a neat argument' and instead narrates one diary-style day. But the narration is in service of understanding, not action-guidance: the day's episodes function as evidence for a set of claims about how context is created, how AI does and doesn't help, and why artifact staleness is hard to detect — made explicit in the closing 'reflections' section. It is theoretical dressed as narrative: the propositions are mostly implicit, carried by the story rather than stated as advice.

Key terms

  • subway map
    A personal, hand-built network diagram (stops, lines, transfer points) that Cutler uses to model a product's end-to-end shape as a customer/data/artifact journey — a private sensemaking tool, not a literal transit map.
  • sensemaking artifacts
    The broad, proliferating class of self- or team-generated documents and diagrams (journeys, map types, one-pagers, atomic insights, goals, 'Jobs,' principles) produced in the course of understanding a domain, as distinct from execution artifacts like tickets.
  • live (artifact)
    An artifact that is continuously updated and still reflects current understanding — as opposed to a 'relic' or 'cave drawing,' a frozen artifact whose accuracy has quietly expired without anyone marking it so.
  • context battleground
    The marketing framing (which Cutler attributes to hearing others use it, e.g. around Notion) that treats owning/storing a user's or org's 'context' as the competitive prize — which he distinguishes from, and finds less useful than, the harder, unmarketed problem of context creation and preservation.
  • AI Slop Cascade
    Cutler's coinage ('a three-times-removed AI Slop Cascade') for context or knowledge that has passed through multiple layers of AI summarization/regeneration, each pass eroding groundedness — contrasted with context built through his own active exploration.
  • archaeology
    The effortful, self-directed work of reconstructing situated, tacit understanding of a product/org from a pile of artifacts plus conversations, when there is no single authoritative source to simply read.

Arguments

AI, as currently used, cannot solve the central onboarding problem of determining which artifacts in an organization's pile still reflect current reality. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Querying AI across his artifact pile yields mediocre ('meh') answers
  • It is very hard to tell which artifacts are 'live' versus relics even reading them directly himself
Implicit premises:
  • AI has no independent way to verify an artifact's current validity beyond signals already present in the artifact or its metadata
  • Confident, fluent AI output can mask staleness rather than reveal it (the 'glossy, coherent-sounding, but ultimately wrong' failure mode)
Inferences:
  • A trustworthy AI assistant over organizational knowledge would need explicit provenance/freshness/consensus signals that his current tools don't expose
  • The artifact-staleness problem is a documentation-governance problem that AI-generated content amplifies rather than resolves
Real organizational context must be actively created — through exploration, conversation, and 'archaeology' — rather than passively received from AI, and that active creation carries value beyond its output. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • He 'feels alive' trying to make sense of things
  • Ten minutes with a new coworker's tacit context has a different 'flavor' than ten minutes of AI breadth
  • Context-sharing sessions with veterans, where he takes furious notes, are described as 'extremely helpful'
Implicit premises:
  • Passively consuming AI-synthesized context does not produce the same understanding, ownership, or memorability as self-directed exploration
  • Part of what's valuable in a veteran colleague's answer is their implicit, silent judgment about what's still relevant or current — not just the facts they state
Inferences:
  • Onboarding approaches that hand new hires an AI-generated 'org brain' instead of time with veterans and self-directed exploration will underperform
  • The value of tacit-knowledge transfer scales with how much implicit relevance/currency judgment the veteran silently performs — something hard to elicit from an AI system that wasn't there to see the context change

Narratives

  • Across the day, Cutler builds a hand-crafted 'subway map' of the product, converts it to Notion tables with AI's help, then catches himself before letting Notion's AI extend the map itself from its own knowledge of other workflows, hitting 'UNDO. UNDO. UNDO.'
    → Shows rather than argues the boundary he implicitly draws between AI as a formalizer of understanding he already has and AI as an originator of understanding he hasn't personally verified — supports proposition 3 and Argument A.
  • He's flooded with heterogeneous artifacts (meeting transcripts, notes, Miro boards, usability-test videos, 'insert 10 other artifact types') and finds AI queries across them 'meh,' while a couple of live context-sharing sessions with veteran colleagues are 'extremely helpful.'
    → Narratively supports proposition 6 and Argument B — the contrast case that AI breadth and human tacit context are not interchangeable.
  • Mid-afternoon he hears about the Miro acquisition and notes it's 'one of my favorite ways to work with people' — a brief aside amid AI-tool frustration (PowerPoint's broken AI features, mediocre AI answers over his artifact pile).
    → A small but telling signal of which category of tool he values (collaborative, human-in-the-loop) versus the AI tools frustrating him that day — reinforces proposition 6's human/AI contrast, though thinly.
  • He has to use PowerPoint for the first time in years, doesn't recognize it, and finds its AI features unhelpful when he just wants to paste without formatting.
    → A minor narrative of AI-feature friction interrupting a simple mechanical task, reinforcing a broader skepticism toward AI feature bloat consistent with his 'rigorous adopter' stance.

Questions addressed

  • ⭕ How does someone with zero context make sense of a new product/organization's full end-to-end shape, when tickets, existing docs, and AI aren't built for someone parachuting in?
    The closest he gets is a hand-built personal artifact (the subway map) plus live conversations with veterans plus reading the code — not a systematic solution; he explicitly doesn't know if he'll keep the artifact or if anyone will ever look at it.
  • ⭕ How do you know whether an existing artifact (doc, insight, one-pager) still reflects current reality?
    He names the question directly ('One puzzle... is figuring out what is "still valid"') but offers no method beyond talking to people who've been around and doing his own archaeology — no heuristic or signal for staleness is proposed.
  • ⭕ Will his own subway-map artifact become exactly the kind of stale, un-understandable relic he's worried about inheriting from others once he moves on or forgets its private notation?

Gaps

  • incompleteCutler identifies that it's hard to tell which artifacts are 'live' versus 'relics' but offers no concrete heuristic or signal for making that determination — e.g. last-updated metadata, authorship, revision/consensus history, or explicit 'still valid as of' annotations. The piece's central practical problem is named vividly but left without even a candidate method.
    Read next: His own TBM 418 'Campfires, Trails, and Quests' — specifically its unclosed gap that trails/context pointers, unlike wikis, have no revert/staleness mechanism — or documentation-rot literature (Diataxis, 'Big Ball of Mud') for maintenance-liability frameworks.
  • incompleteHe notes that live context-sharing sessions with veteran colleagues are 'extremely helpful' where AI queries over similar underlying material are 'meh,' but never analyzes why — is it the veteran's live relevance-judgment, two-way questioning, social trust, or information that was simply never written down at all? The mechanism behind the contrast is asserted, not examined.
    Read next: TBM 418's campfires/trails/quests framework, or Nonaka & Takeuchi's SECI model of tacit-to-explicit knowledge conversion.
  • incompleteThe piece frames 'context battleground' AI marketing as reminiscent of past failed knowledge-management movements (source-of-truth, connected data) but doesn't engage with why those specific efforts failed — the analogy is asserted rather than argued, leaving open whether AI-era context tools would fail for the same reasons or genuinely different ones.
    Read next: Classic knowledge-management-failure retrospectives (e.g. Davenport on why KM initiatives stall) to test whether the parallel holds.
