AWS Will Run Its European Sovereign Cloud Without the Global Backbone for Several Hours

Steef-Jan Wiggers · rss · 2026-10-18 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Cascading Failure & System Resilience PatternsChaos EngineeringData SovereigntyTechnical vs. Legal Sovereignty
AWS's planned multi-hour disconnection of its European Sovereign Cloud from the global backbone is an honest, narrowly scoped demonstration of operational resilience, not of sovereignty; the real dispute is not overclaiming by AWS but the gap between what a few-hour test measures and what European buyers actually ask (legal reach, sustained autonomy, global-service dependencies).

theoretical · mixed — News report with an analytical thread: it describes an announced AWS test (expository) and then adjudicates among practitioner reactions (argumentative). It argues for understanding what the exercise does and does not prove, not for any action.

Key terms

  • Global Network backbone
    AWS's private network that moves operational data between AWS locations; one of the two systems cut during the exercise
  • Operation-critical
    A dependency without which the region cannot keep serving; AWS asserts the severed systems are not this, which is why the region survives and also why it uses them daily
  • Independent operation
    AWS's label for the region continuing to run for a few hours with the backbone and data channel severed, operated by EU residents on regional infrastructure
  • Technical vs legal sovereignty
    Dasus's split: (technical) does the service stop if the link to the US is cut; (legal) can US law order the provider to hand over data or stop serving you. Bjornestad restates it as 'can someone access my data' vs 'can someone shut me off'
  • Redundancy is not independence; continued operation is not sustained autonomy
    Haraldsen's distinction: surviving a few hours says nothing about drift, token/certificate/licence expiry or external control-plane dependence over longer periods
  • EU Cloud Sovereignty Framework / BSI C3A
    External criteria the exercise is designed to line up with, so customers can reuse results in compliance packages; explicitly not a certification

Arguments

The exercise is real resilience progress but does not demonstrate sovereignty. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Sovereignty has a technical part (survives US link cut) and a legal part (US-law compellability)
  • The test only addresses the technical part
  • The parent company remains US-owned, so the legal part still runs through the US
Implicit premises:
  • Ownership and corporate nexus, not data location, determine legal reach
  • A sovereignty claim must satisfy both parts, not either
Inferences:
  • A sovereign claim built purely on operational tests invites buyers to read 'resilient' as 'immune from foreign law'
  • Encryption and key custody, not network isolation, are the technical levers that touch the legal question
A few hours of continued operation is weak evidence of independence. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Time-dependent failures (drift, expiring tokens, certificates, licences) appear only after longer isolation
  • Dependencies on external control planes erode resilience over time
  • Global services such as IAM and DNS are unaddressed in the announcement
Implicit premises:
  • A region's local caches and credentials are sized for short backbone partitions, not extended ones
  • The tested interval is short because longer isolation is operationally or commercially expensive
Inferences:
  • The meaningful parameter is time-to-first-dependency-failure, which the exercise could report but AWS has not said it will
AWS is not overclaiming; the controversy is about audience expectations, not vendor misrepresentation. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AWS states the severed systems are not operation-critical
  • AWS makes no certification claim and does not claim year-round isolation
  • Even critics credit AWS with being careful not to claim more than it can prove
Implicit premises:
  • Vendor honesty in the announcement settles the overclaiming question regardless of how marketing and the press translate it downstream
  • Buyers' questions are legitimate even though they exceed what a test could answer
Inferences:
  • The risk of misreading falls on downstream summarisers and procurement, not on the primary text

Narratives

  • The 2021 AWS global outage, in which parts of AWS appeared to depend on us-east-1 and could not run elsewhere, recalled in comments, with Lundsten conceding that engineering the dependency away improves the architecture
    → Shows why the global-control-plane question is a real engineering concern and a credit to AWS if resolved
  • A Swedish municipal IT operations manager (Svenljunga Kommun) rather than a vendor poses the hardest technical question about duration and expiring dependencies
    → Supports the claim that buyer-side operators ask for sustained autonomy, not a few-hour demonstration

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What does AWS's planned disconnection exercise actually prove about the European Sovereign Cloud?
    That the region survives losing two non-critical systems for several hours; it does not prove legal sovereignty or sustained autonomy.
  • ✅ Is AWS overclaiming?
    No; AWS avoids certification and discloses the systems' non-critical status. The mismatch is with buyers' expectations.
  • ⭕ How do IAM, DNS and internet routing behave when the region is cut off?
    Left unanswered by the announcement; commenters asked and got no answer in the piece.
  • ⭕ Does the exercise outcome change the legal exposure of a US-owned provider?
    Commenters disagree; the author does not adjudicate the legal merits.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece is written before the test (an update is promised after October 24), so there are no results. It also gives no measurement plan: no stated metric such as time to first dependency failure, no mention of whether the test will be extended, and no independent observer. Haraldsen's duration question is raised but the exercise's length is defined by AWS, not by what would break first.
    Read next: AWS's follow-up post after October 24; the AWS security blog on independent operation; extended network-partition or Jepsen-style isolation studies for evidence on how long regional systems stay coherent when isolated.
  • incompleteGlobal-service behaviour (IAM, STS, DNS, internet routing, billing, support) while the backbone is down is neither described by AWS nor explored by the author, even though commenters raised it. This is the technical heart of whether the region truly has no operation-critical external control plane.
    Read next: AWS European Sovereign Cloud architecture whitepaper and service-by-service availability list; the AWS 2021 us-east-1 postmortem (InfoQ link) on global-service coupling.
  • incompleteThe legal half of sovereignty is argued only through LinkedIn comments, which conflict (Lundsten, Zomerman, Bjornestad), and two of the commenters have commercial or ecosystem interests (Elastisys sells an EU alternative; Bjornestad is an AWS Community Hero). No legal analysis of CLOUD Act reach over an EU-incorporated subsidiary of a US parent, and no treatment of encryption and key custody as a technical mitigation.
    Read next: Independent legal analysis (EDPB guidance, post-Schrems II commentary, EU e-Evidence regulation) and technical treatments of customer-held keys as a response to compelled access.
  • incompleteNo comparison to alternative models of sovereign cloud (EU-owned providers, joint ventures with licensed US technology) on the same two axes, so the reader cannot tell whether failing the legal test is specific to AWS's ownership structure or inherent to the approach.
    Read next: Comparative accounts of EU-owned and licensed-technology sovereign clouds; Stanford HAI work on how vendor-sold sovereignty deepens dependence.
