Presentation: Parting the Clouds: The Rise of Disaggregated Systems

Murat Demirbas · rss · 2026-07-30 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Compute-Storage DisaggregationModularity & CouplingPaxos Role Decomposition (Proposer/Acceptor/Learner)
Cloud economics (the differing cost, scaling, and statefulness profiles of compute versus storage) is forcing database architectures toward disaggregation, and this shift is not a novel invention but a rediscovery of Lamport's original three-role decomposition of Paxos (proposer/acceptor/learner) — so the tradeoffs, optimizations, and open problems of today's disaggregated systems can be understood, and addressed, through decades of prior consensus-protocol research.

theoretical · mixed — Primarily theoretical/expository — a survey of how and why database architecture has shifted to disaggregation — but built on an argumentative core (the Paxos-role analogy) and narrated through historical case studies (Aurora, PolarDB, TaurusDB) and forward-looking speculation (future work). Practical implications (which mitigations to apply, when to disaggregate) are secondary to the explanatory thesis.

Key terms

  • Disaggregation
    Architecturally separating compute and storage into independently-scaling components connected only via the network, replacing a design where both live in the same process/box.
  • Impedance mismatch
    The structural mismatch between compute (costly, fast-fluctuating demand, can be stateless) and storage (cheap, stable/growing demand, inherently stateful), which makes bundling them together economically wasteful.
  • Log is the database Compute-Storage Disaggregation
    Aurora's design principle: ship only the write-ahead/redo log to storage (not the full materialized state), and have storage nodes reconstruct pages from the log — cutting network traffic and data movement.
  • Log Store / Page Store
    TaurusDB's further split of the storage tier itself into a low-latency, durability-prioritized Log Store and a separate Page Store that materializes pages for buffer/read serving.
  • Proposer / Acceptor / Learner (mapped to compute / log store / page store)
    Lamport's three Paxos roles, which the author maps onto the disaggregated architecture: proposers generate order (compute), acceptors persist votes/the log (log store, durability), learners serve materialized state (page store, availability).
  • Ingrained fault tolerance
    The design principle that consensus/coordination should be built into the disaggregated database service itself, rather than delegated to an external configurator or coordination service that designates a primary node.

Arguments

Database architectures should decouple compute from storage (disaggregate). strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Compute is costly and fluctuates quickly in demand; storage is cheap and grows steadily.
  • Compute can be stateless while storage is inherently stateful.
  • Bundling them forces customers to overpay for whichever resource they don't actually need more of.
  • Disaggregation delivers elastic scaling (including scale-to-zero), I/O pooling, and fault isolation.
Implicit premises:
  • Cost-structure differences between two resources are a sufficient basis for architectural separation, independent of any added coordination cost the separation introduces.
  • The software-engineering virtue of 'separation of concerns' (usually applied to code modularity) extends cleanly to infrastructure-level cost concerns.
Inferences:
  • If economic mismatch is the operative justification, then most on-premises 'hyperconverged' compute+storage designs are similarly suboptimal, not merely a historical artifact of pre-cloud hardware limits — a generalization the talk doesn't draw.
Today's disaggregated-database engineers should treat decades of Paxos consensus-protocol research (flexible quorums, role-specific replica counts) as a reusable design resource.
Explicit premises:
  • Lamport described Paxos as three roles: proposer, acceptor, learner.
  • Disaggregated database architectures independently arrived at an equivalent three-way split: compute, log store, page store.
  • Paxos research shows acceptors need only 2f+1 while proposers/learners need only f+1, and flexible quorums can improve throughput.
Implicit premises:
  • Structural analogy in role count and role function is sufficient grounds to transfer specific quantitative results from Paxos theory to disaggregated database systems without further adaptation.
  • The safety properties Paxos roles guarantee (no split-brain via majority quorum) map cleanly onto the durability/availability properties actually implemented by commercial log/page stores, even though those systems (Aurora's 4-of-6 quorum, PolarDB's parallel Raft) were engineered independently of Paxos flexible-quorum theory.
Inferences:
  • If structural role-analogy licenses importing Paxos-era optimizations, it should presumably also surface Paxos-era correctness caveats and impossibility results (e.g., liveness/safety tradeoffs) for disaggregated systems — a symmetry the talk doesn't address.
Disaggregated database services should build consensus in ('ingrained fault tolerance') rather than depend on an external configurator to designate the primary node.
Explicit premises:
  • Current disaggregated architectures depend on an external service to determine which node is primary.
  • Every external dependency is a potential point of failure or coupling.
Implicit premises:
  • Internalizing consensus does not reintroduce the same coordination cost/complexity that externalizing it into a separate service was originally meant to avoid.
  • Removing one dependency net-reduces total system fragility rather than simply relocating the same risk.

Narratives

  • The three-phase history of database architecture: monolithic (Postgres/MySQL since the 1970s) -> cloud-ified monolith replicated via Raft/Paxos groups with two-phase commit for horizontal scale -> genuinely disaggregated architecture (Aurora onward).
    → Frames disaggregation as the current endpoint of a linear, inevitable historical progression rather than one design choice among several, reinforcing the unity's claim that cloud economics 'demands' this direction.
  • The step-by-step walk through Aurora ('log is the database', storage-side materialization surgery on MySQL/Postgres), PolarDB (compute-side InnoDB materialization, parallel Raft, RDMA via PolarFS), and TaurusDB (Log Store / Page Store split, custom scatter replication).
    → Supports the claim that 'log is the database' is the shared invention while showing real engineering variation, letting the audience see the pattern as convergent rather than copied.
  • Lamport's own remark in the Multi-Paxos paper — that the three roles can be compacted into one node 'if needed for efficiency' — used to explain how the field came to forget that Paxos was originally a disaggregated, three-role design, only to reinvent that decomposition decades later as disaggregated databases.
    → Supports the central Paxos analogy with a 'history rhymes / nothing new under the sun' framing that makes the connection feel discovered rather than constructed.
  • The self-correcting 'I lied to you a little bit' moment: after presenting fault isolation as an unqualified benefit earlier in the talk, the author narratively reverses to explain that disaggregation weakens fate-sharing and opens the door to metastable failures.
    → A staged rhetorical reveal that makes the downside land harder than if it had been presented flatly alongside the benefits earlier — supports the proposition that disaggregation's fault-isolation benefit is real but incomplete.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How does disaggregated database architecture relate to classical consensus protocols like Paxos?
    Resolved via the role-decomposition analogy: proposer/acceptor/learner map onto compute/log-store/page-store, and Multi-Paxos/Raft's compaction of those roles explains why the resemblance was lost.
  • ✅ What mitigates the network latency and bandwidth costs that disaggregation introduces?
    Buffering, prefetching, pipelining, faster fabrics (RDMA, CXL), custom protocol restructuring (e.g., overlapping two-phase-commit phases), log-traffic prioritization, scatter I/O, and pushdown compute to storage.
  • ⭕ Can database systems become self-assembling or auto-tuning to handle the burstier traffic patterns of agentic AI workloads?
    Explicitly flagged as an open question and future-work direction, not yet solved.
  • ⭕ Is the vendor-adoption trend cited as proof that disaggregation 'is already here' (Aurora 2015 through Amazon DSQL 2025) actually evidence that these systems deliver the claimed economic and performance benefits at scale, or merely evidence of adoption?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe talk's central analogy (Paxos's proposer/acceptor/learner = disaggregated compute/log-store/page-store) is argued from role-count and role-function correspondence, but never examines whether the actual consistency and safety mechanisms engineered into commercial systems — Aurora's specific 4-of-6 storage quorum, PolarDB's 'parallel Raft' — were derived from, or are even formally equivalent to, Paxos's quorum-based correctness guarantees. It is left unclear whether this is a rigorous technical lineage or a structural resemblance noticed after the fact and narrated as history.
    Read next: Compare the Aurora and PolarDB architecture papers' own consistency arguments against Lamport's Paxos correctness proof, to see whether their quorum choices were derived from consensus theory or arrived at independently by systems engineers optimizing empirically for latency and durability.
  • incompleteThe claim that 'decades of Paxos research... can be borrowed' to improve disaggregated database throughput is illustrated only by the author's own separate research prototype (compartmentalized Paxos, an 8x throughput result over the same codebase) — not by evidence that any of the three surveyed commercial systems (Aurora, PolarDB, TaurusDB) actually apply flexible quorums or role-disaggregated consensus in production.
    Read next: Read the compartmentalized Paxos paper (Demirbas et al. with UC Berkeley collaborators) and any public engineering retrospectives from the Aurora/PolarDB/TaurusDB teams to check whether flexible-quorum results have moved from research into production, or remain a proposal.
  • incompleteThe list of production disaggregated systems (Aurora 2015 through Amazon DSQL 2025) is presented as confirmatory evidence that disaggregation 'is already here' and validated, with no counter-examples, documented cost overruns, performance regressions, or teams that reverted from disaggregated to monolithic designs — a one-sided evidentiary base for what is framed as an economically inevitable trend.
    Read next: Look for independent benchmarks or critical retrospectives comparing Aurora/PolarDB/AlloyDB total cost of ownership and performance against self-hosted or lift-and-shift Postgres/MySQL, to test whether 'cloud economics demands disaggregation' holds outside an idealized cost model.
  • illogicalThe 'ingrained fault tolerance' principle (build consensus into the database rather than depend on an external configurator) is asserted without reconciling that internalizing consensus reintroduces the coordination overhead that motivated separating log stores and page stores from compute in the first place. The author himself concedes the shared-log/virtual-log research branch he praises still depends on an external metastore ('I'll live with it'), which undercuts the principle's own claim to full self-containment without resolving the tension.
    Read next: Read Mahesh Balakrishnan's shared-log/virtual-log work for how it explicitly handles this exact tradeoff, rather than fully internalizing consensus — it may show that full self-containment is not achievable in practice and 'ingrained fault tolerance' is aspirational rather than a settled design principle.
