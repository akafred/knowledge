This Is How To Live A Good Life: 5 Secrets From Ancient Wisdom

Eric Barker · rss · 2026-07-19 Reader 🖥️

AgencyIdentity-Based Goal SettingSocial Connection & RelatednessVirtue as Habituated Practice (Aristotelian Habituation)
Barker argues that living well is not a state to achieve or a fact to learn but a sustained practice — Aristotle's claim that character, self-control, and happiness (eudaimonia) all consist in habituated activity rather than insight, willpower, or declared intention — and treats this 2300-year-old framework as credible chiefly because contemporary psychological research keeps independently converging on the same conclusions.

practical · mixed — A practical self-help listicle (five 'secrets') structured around expository summary of Aristotelian ethics, but each section makes real argumentative moves (a quote/claim from Aristotle, paired with a modern research claim, reframing a folk assumption) rather than pure exposition, and the piece closes with an explicit meta-argument about why understanding an idea is not the same as living it.

Key terms

  • eudaimonia Virtue as Habituated Practice (Aristotelian Habituation)
    An actively pursued condition of 'flourishing,' achieved by exercising one's capacities well toward worthwhile ends — something you do, not a mood you have.
  • virtue / character
    A stable disposition built through repeated correct action, culminating in taking pleasure in doing the right thing rather than merely resisting temptation toward the wrong thing.
  • political animal Virtue as Habituated Practice (Aristotelian Habituation)
    Humans are constitutively social: identity, language, and flourishing are only achievable in relation to specific other people, not to 'humanity' in the abstract.
  • manipulation / rhetoric
    The study of persuasion techniques, including bad-faith ones, framed as a defensive and communicative competence rather than an endorsement of using them.
  • habituation ('repeatedly do') Virtue as Habituated Practice (Aristotelian Habituation)
    Repeated action as the causal mechanism of character formation, displacing both belief change and single decisive moments of insight.

Arguments

You should build character and a good life through repeated practice of the relevant virtuous actions, not through insight, willpower alone, or declared intention. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Aristotle: 'We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts.'
  • Modern self-control research finds habit-building and temptation-avoidance, not willpower, predict success.
Implicit premises:
  • Actions, repeated, causally determine the dispositions and self-concept a person ends up with — felt 'authenticity' is retrospectively constructed from habitual action rather than the reverse.
  • There is no neutral default: every action (including avoidance) is training some disposition, so passivity is not actually passive.
  • A narrow empirical finding about temptation-resistance strategies (habits/avoidance beat willpower) is treated as corroboration of the much broader Aristotelian claim that virtue is constituted by habituated practice.
Inferences:
  • Appeal to Aristotle's authority combined with selective citation of one convergent psychology finding, generalized into validation of the whole eudaimonia framework.
You should deliberately study manipulation and rhetoric even though you find them distasteful. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Manipulation exists in the world regardless of whether you choose to understand it.
  • Refusing to understand a threat does not protect you from it (poison/arsenic analogy).
  • Truth, stated plainly, is often less persuasive than an emotionally compelling falsehood.
Implicit premises:
  • Understanding a technique and endorsing or using it are cleanly separable — the oncologist-studies-cancer analogy assumes no dual-use risk.
  • The primary risk being guarded against is being victimized by manipulators, not becoming a more effective manipulator oneself once the techniques are understood.
Inferences:
  • Analogy from medicine (studying cancer to fight it) to social knowledge (studying rhetoric to resist it), treated as sufficiently similar to license the same 'study it to fight it' logic.
Ancient philosophical wisdom (Aristotle in particular) remains a reliable guide to living well today. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • A 2011-era self-control study matches Aristotle's account of virtue as habit rather than willpower.
  • A happiness-pursuit study matches Aristotle's eudaimonia-as-activity framing.
  • The Harvard Study of Adult Development matches Aristotle's claim that humans are political animals who need relationships.
Implicit premises:
  • Convergence between an ancient philosopher's intuitions and isolated modern findings is evidence of the philosopher's broader correctness, rather than evidence that the article selected only the findings that agree.
  • Aristotle's ideas survived roughly 2300 years of transmission and continued citation because they were true or valuable, rather than because of contingent factors in intellectual history (canon formation, translation, institutional preservation, syllabus inertia) — i.e., the ideas we still have access to from antiquity are a survivorship-biased sample, not a representative one.
Inferences:
  • Cherry-picked convergence (three matching studies, zero divergent or disconfirming findings considered) generalized into 'ancient wisdom, validated by science.'

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How do we live a good life?
    Via Aristotelian eudaimonia: habituated virtue, redirected (not merely resisted) desire, understanding social reality including manipulation, and sustained specific relationships.
  • ✅ Why do so many 'how to live better' essays fail to produce any actual change in the reader?
    Because understanding or agreeing with an idea is mistaken for having acted on it; only repeated action changes behavior.
  • ✅ Should happiness (as a mood) be pursued directly?
    No — direct pursuit of a happy mood is self-defeating; treat happiness as a byproduct of well-exercised activity instead.
  • ⭕ Does Aristotle's own historical and social context (aristocratic Greek male, a slaveholding and rigidly gendered society, teleological worldview) limit how directly his ethics of 'the good life' transfers to a modern egalitarian moral context?
  • ⭕ Where does 'studying manipulation to defend against it' end and 'becoming skilled at manipulation' begin — is there a line, and how would a reader know if they crossed it?

Gaps

  • illogicalThe article's central meta-argument — that modern research 'lands surprisingly close' to Aristotle, which validates his ethics — commits a selection bias: only convergent findings are cited (one 2011 self-control study, one happiness-pursuit study, the Harvard Study of Adult Development) while no divergent or disconfirming modern findings are considered, and no account is given of where Aristotle's ethics has been rejected, revised, or superseded by later moral philosophy or psychology.
    Read next: Look for modern findings or ethical positions that diverge from Aristotle (e.g., situationist critiques of virtue ethics — Gilbert Harman, John Doris — arguing character traits are far less predictive of behavior than situational factors) to test whether the 'convergence' really holds up once disconfirming evidence is included.
  • incompleteThe oncologist/poison analogy used to justify studying manipulation elides a dual-use asymmetry: understanding persuasion techniques also equips the reader to deploy them, unlike understanding cancer biology, which does not make you better at giving people cancer. The piece never addresses the risk of the reader becoming more effective at manipulating others, only the risk of being victimized by manipulators.
    Read next: Robert Cialdini's own later reflections on the ethical use of 'Influence,' and dual-use-knowledge literature in security research (the same tension appears in offensive-security education), for how a field handles teaching an attack skill responsibly.
    closed byWhen Persuasion Is (and Isn't) Manipulation — That gap asks whether studying manipulation/persuasion techniques (as Cialdini catalogs them) makes a reader more effective at manipulating others, a risk the oncologist/poison analogy leaves unaddressed. Marshall directly engages this tension: his answer is that technique-knowledge is ethically neutral and what determines misuse is intent, context, and relational stance rather than exposure to a taxonomy of tactics — though as this document's own gap g3 notes, that answer doesn't fully resolve the skill-transfer risk, since it still assumes the reader is disposed toward ethical use.
  • uninformedThe claim that 'psychology lands surprisingly close to Aristotle' rests on a single, unnamed 2011 study (linked only via a psycnet.apa.org DOI) with no reported researchers, sample, effect size, or replication status — treating one social-psychology finding from just before the replication crisis as settled confirmation of a philosophical claim, with no caveat about the state of that literature.
    Read next: Check the linked study's replication history and whether self-control-via-habit-avoidance findings (e.g., Roy Baumeister's ego-depletion-adjacent literature) have held up post-2015 replication efforts.
  • incompleteThe Harvard Study of Adult Development is invoked accurately as a real, long-running longitudinal study, but its purely correlational nature is not addressed — warm relationships correlating with health/happiness outcomes doesn't establish that Aristotle's specific mechanism ('eating salt together,' shared virtue-forming experience) is the operative pathway, as opposed to socioeconomic confounds, personality traits that independently cause both relationship quality and health, or reverse causation (healthier people sustain relationships more easily).
    Read next: Read the Harvard Study's own published analyses (Waldinger & Schulz, The Good Life) for how the researchers themselves discuss causal mechanism and confounds, rather than relying on the popular-summary framing used here.
  • uninformedBarker never engages passages in Aristotle's own texts, or secondary Aristotle scholarship, that complicate the article's implied 'flourishing is available to anyone who habituates well enough' message — notably Aristotle's own view that eudaimonia partly depends on external goods (health, wealth, good birth, good luck) not fully within an individual's control, which sits in real tension with a self-help framing that treats flourishing as purely a matter of individual habit choice.
    Read next: Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics Book I on the role of external goods and fortune in eudaimonia; Martha Nussbaum's 'The Fragility of Goodness' on luck's role in Greek ethical thought.
  • incompleteThe article asserts that repeated practice builds character but never specifies how much practice is required, over what timeframe, or what to do about backsliding or plateaus during early attempts — the mechanics of habituation (dose, response curve, relapse) are asserted as a general principle, not detailed as an actionable process.
    Read next: James Clear's Atomic Habits or the underlying habit-formation research (Lally et al.'s variable-timeline habit study) for the empirical shape of how long habituation actually takes and how relapse is typically handled.
