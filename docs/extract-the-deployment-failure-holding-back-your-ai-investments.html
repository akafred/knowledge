The Deployment Failure Holding Back Your AI Investments

Justine Hastings, Chad Syverson, Seth Zimmerman · article · 2026-10-21 Reader 🖥️

AI Value-Driver AlignmentCoordination CostEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessHuman as Orchestrator of AgentsMeasuring AI ImpactValue Streams & VerticalsAI Workflow Patterns
Firms are not seeing returns from AI because they deploy it inside individual functions, leaving untouched the 37% of work time spent coordinating across systems and the silo-optimal decisions that coordination failure produces; leaders should measure coordination cost, deploy AI across functions, reward firm-level results, and redefine roles around the cross-functional decisions AI makes possible.

practical · mixed — Economists' argument for how senior leaders should deploy AI, built from a 300-person time-allocation survey, a stylized SKU decision, and two practitioner anecdotes (Amazon HR analytics, Uber Agentic Pods). Propositions serve a five-step leadership prescription.

Key terms

  • coordination work
    Work that moves information across systems and functions so a decision can be made: pulling and cleaning data from several systems, reconciling conflicting numbers, refreshing recurring reports, cross-functional status meetings, reformatting analysis. Distinct from administrative work and substantive work.
  • deployment problem
    The reason AI delivers no measurable return: firms fail to capture complementarities by measuring inefficiencies, deploying AI built to address them across functions, and transforming roles accordingly, rather than lacking technology or capability.
  • complementarities
    Organizational choices where doing more of one thing raises the payoff from doing more of another (data, decisions, structure), so AI value appears only when they move together.
  • cross-functional spillover
    A decision that improves one function's results and imposes costs on another that are not included in the original decision (cheapest freight lane raises inventory elsewhere; labor-budget cuts raise turnover and safety costs).
  • productivity J-curve
    Measured productivity can fall at first as firms absorb the cost of complementary change, then rise as the investments pay off; the PC era is the cited precedent.
  • reward the firm's win, not the silo's
    Credit AI return to firm-level outcomes rather than the function hosting the tool, so cross-functional systems get funded.

Arguments

AI investment has not paid off because it is deployed within functions instead of across them. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Employees spend 37% of time on coordination work across systems and functions.
  • Provided AI tools are designed to work within a single system.
  • Coordination spillovers make silo-optimal decisions wrong for the firm.
  • AI built across systems can remove the coordination cost and change decisions.
Implicit premises:
  • Survey respondents' reported time shares reflect real work and real waste.
  • The coordination work AI would remove is mostly assembly, not judgment or negotiation.
  • Firm-level returns will follow once decisions are made with full information.
Inferences:
  • Single-function copilot ROI studies systematically understate or misallocate AI value.
  • AI vendors selling per-function tools face a structural mismatch with the largest value pool.
A senior leader above the functions must own cross-functional AI deployment. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • No single function owns the value created across functions.
  • Return credited to the hosting function keeps funding within-function tools.
  • Roles, budgets and incentives are built around each function's own coordination work.
Implicit premises:
  • A senior leader has the authority and information to override function-level incentives.
  • The obstacle is lack of a funding and measurement owner, not active resistance by function heads.
Inferences:
  • Cross-functional AI is a governance and org-design project that AI teams cannot deliver alone.
  • The same logic implies a cost-allocation or P&L change before a technology change.
Leaders should measure outcomes and coordination inputs, not AI adoption. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Logins and prompts measure tool use, not whether work improved.
  • You cannot manage what you have never counted; categories like 'analysis' hide data assembly.
Implicit premises:
  • Coordination-share and cycle-time measures are themselves hard to game and valid proxies for firm outcomes.
Inferences:
  • Coordination-share metrics can themselves become targets (Goodhart) once tied to funding.

Narratives

  • A consumer-goods company deciding whether to cut a low-performing SKU: finance sees profit, manufacturing sees changeover costs, planning sees safety stock, marketing sees cannibalization, and no one owns the decision so the SKU survives.
    → Concrete demonstration that coordination cost produces silo-rational, firm-irrational decisions and that cross-system AI changes the decision.
  • Justine Hastings' Amazon HR team replaced months-long manual causal analyses of people programs with an AI system drawing on a common data source, cutting delivery to days.
    → Existence proof that rule-governed cross-system analysis is automatable and frees experts for judgment work.
  • Uber's two-week 'Agentic Pods': AI-proficient engineers embedded with finance, legal and HR observe workflows across systems then build agents around them; a pacing report falls from two days to 10 minutes.
    → Models the deployment method (observe cross-system work, then build) and supports feasibility.
  • Personal computers spread in the 1980s but measured productivity gains appeared only in the 1990s after processes were redesigned.
    → Historical analogy for the productivity J-curve and the primacy of organizational change over technology.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why does widespread enterprise AI use not show up in productivity or financial results?
    Deployment within silos leaves the large pool of cross-system coordination work and silo-optimal decisions untouched.
  • ✅ Where should a senior leader point AI first?
    At measured coordination cost: decisions made with partial information because assembling the full picture is expensive.
  • ✅ Who should own and be credited for cross-functional AI value?
    A senior leader, with firm-level return measurement and role redefinition; no function can do it alone.
  • ⭕ Why have functions not already solved their cross-functional coordination problems with data and human effort?
    Attributed to cost and difficulty of assembling the picture; power, incentives and data ownership are not examined.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe causal case rests on a 300-person self-reported time survey and two anecdotes, one from the author's own former employer and one relayed from a tweet about Uber's CTO. No firm-level outcome data is given showing that cross-functional deployment raises productivity or EBIT, and one author now builds enterprise AI systems, a commercial interest the article states but does not weigh.
    Read next: Firm-level studies of enterprise-wide vs function-level AI deployment (the NBER executive survey data; DORA value-stream management findings; case-control studies of cross-system agent rollouts).
  • incompleteThe political economy of cross-functional AI is untouched. The SKU story is itself an ownership, incentive and decision-rights problem, yet the remedy is mostly informational (remove assembly cost) plus an appeal to a senior leader. Cutler's argument that AI cannot create decision rights, resolve contested ownership or clear incentive misalignment would predict that the unowned decision stays unowned.
    Read next: Read against Cutler's 'AI Won't Save the Kingdoms' and DORA's value-stream management guidance; research on organizational design for cross-functional ownership.
  • incompleteWho is answerable when a cross-functional AI system, not a person, effectively makes the SKU-style decision? The prescription says to expand autonomy 'as it earns trust' but gives no accountability model, even though the decision crosses every function's budget and the system's errors are no longer visible in any single silo.
    Read next: Accountability and answerability for AI-made cross-functional decisions; the moral-crumple-zone literature.
  • illogicalReconciling conflicting numbers is also how organizations notice errors and surface conflicts between functions. Treating it purely as waste to automate ignores that part of coordination work is error-correction and negotiation, whose removal could entrench a single AI-defined view of 'the' correct number.
    Read next: Sociology of data reconciliation and 'coordination theater' vs real coordination (Cutler); verification capacity work in the corpus.
