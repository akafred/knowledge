The AI-Native SDLC playbook

Louis Claxton · article · 2026-08-21 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent Governance (Internal)Agent HarnessAgentic SDLCFeedback LoopsIntent DebtOrchestration Tax / Human Review BottleneckPull Requests & Async Code ReviewSmall Teams & Compression
Once agentic coding collapses the build phase to hours, the traditional SDLC's human-speed controls (committees, meetings, line-by-line review) become the bottleneck and stop matching reality, so organizations should rebuild the lifecycle as a non-linear loop of six stages (plan, design, build, test, deploy, maintain) where every stage commits a version-controlled artifact the next stage reads, human judgment concentrates at artifact-acceptance gates rather than at the point of production, and governance is enforced as code (hooks, branch protection, managed settings) rather than through periodic human review bodies.

practical · mixed — A vendor (Anthropic Applied AI team) playbook: expository framing of why the traditional SDLC breaks down, followed by six prescriptive 'plays' (one per lifecycle stage), each with concrete steps, worked examples, governance considerations, and measurement indicators. Argues for action throughout, not just understanding.

Key terms

  • AI-native SDLC Small Teams & Compression
    A reimagined software development lifecycle that keeps the traditional control objectives (accountability, traceability, policy compliance) but enforces them with new mechanisms — a non-linear loop with AI embedded at each point, where an accepted artifact automatically triggers the next stage.
  • intent.md / spec.md / plan.md (the artifact chain)
    A sequence of version-controlled markdown artifacts — captured intent, requirements-and-design spec, implementation plan — each produced by a human-agent session, each reviewed and committed before the next stage begins, together forming the audit trail of who asked for what and who approved it.
  • Plays
    Modular, independently adoptable units of practice, each covering what changes, how to get started, concrete implementation steps, governance considerations, and how to measure success; organized under six stages but not required to be adopted in stage order.
  • Closing the loop
    The practice of letting a deterministic monitoring script detect a control-band breach in production and invoke Claude, without a person in the invocation path, to diagnose the issue and write it back into the pipeline as a new intent.md.
  • Harnessability (implicit contrast) Agent Harness
    Not used by this author, but relevant: the piece never uses the word 'harness' at all, instead decomposing the same underlying concept into named components (CLAUDE.md, skills, hooks) treated as separate plays rather than as parts of a single named layer.

Propositions

  • explicitThe traditional SDLC's process-heavy controls (approval gates, reviews, handoffs, PRDs, estimation rituals) were designed to maximize efficiency and accountability in an era when writing and implementing code was the most time-consuming and expensive stage — an assumption that no longer holds once agentic coding collapses that stage.conf: high
  • explicitWhen the build phase collapses to hours, the bottleneck moves to the human-speed stages on either side of it (plan, review/test, deploy); controls stop matching reality because reviewing each line by hand made sense when a person wrote it, but cannot keep up once agents write most of the diff.conf: high
  • explicitThe AI-native SDLC is a loop, not a linear flow: every stage ends by committing a version-controlled artifact (intent.md, spec.md, plan.md, the diff and its tests, the PR with review findings, the incident record) that the next stage reads and that automatically triggers it, and the resulting chain of commits is itself the audit trail of who asked for what, what the agent produced, and who approved it.conf: high
  • explicitCapturing an idea as intent.md — written by the originator in their own words, with Claude's help, then corrected and committed by that same originator — replaces the traditional path where an idea passes through backlog entries, story points, and refinement meetings, losing fidelity to the originator's intent at each handoff.conf: high

  • explicitRequirements and design can collapse into a single prompted agent session because the organization's policies (brand, security, compliance, UX, encoded as skills) are applied live while the spec is being written, rather than discovered in a separate review weeks later.conf: high
  • explicitNo code should be implemented without an accepted plan; Claude Code's plan mode enforces this structurally, since the agent cannot edit files until the engineer accepts the plan, which moves design review to a point where changing course is still a matter of editing a document rather than a diff.conf: high

  • explicitSeparation of duties in AI-assisted PR review is preserved structurally rather than by policy alone: the agent that wrote the code has no way to approve it, because approval always requires a human through branch protection, however extensive the AI review passes are.conf: high

Arguments

Governance in the AI-native SDLC must be enforced as code (hooks, branch protection, managed settings) rather than through periodic human review bodies. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Traditional governance mechanisms (committees, change boards) operate on a human cadence — weekly or monthly meetings.
  • Agents can produce changes continuously, far faster than that cadence.
  • Hooks and managed settings apply identically to every action, regardless of who or what triggered it, and individual engineers cannot widen or bypass them.
Implicit premises:
  • Governance that cannot keep pace with production velocity effectively stops functioning as governance even while nominally still 'in place.'
  • Deterministic, automatic enforcement is more trustworthy at scale than enforcement that depends on a person remembering, or having time, to check.
Inferences:
  • An organization's existing review-committee cadence becomes a de facto governance gap the moment agents start shipping changes faster than the committee meets.
Human review effort should concentrate at artifact-acceptance gates (intent.md, spec.md, plan.md, PR) rather than at the point of code production itself. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Each stage's committed artifact is reviewable as a document before the next, more expensive stage begins.
  • Changing course by editing a document is cheap; changing course after code has been written and tested is not.
  • The committed-artifact chain is what the whole process audits, so review concentrated at the gates is what the audit trail actually records.
Implicit premises:
  • The classic cost-of-change curve (cheaper to fix earlier) transfers unchanged from human-authored to agent-authored work.
  • Document-stage review substitutes adequately for diff-level review in catching subtle correctness problems, not just scope or requirements problems.
Inferences:
  • The CI/CD shift-left principle generalizes beyond automated checks to human attention itself.

Narratives

  • A single running example — a claims-status self-service feature — is threaded through the worked examples in Stages 1 through 3: the same change accrues an intent.md, then a spec.md, then a plan.md, each shown as a literal file excerpt.
    → Makes concrete the claim (P3) that the committed-artifact chain is the audit trail, by showing one real change traced end to end rather than describing the chain abstractly.
  • A full managed-settings.json worked example for 'a regulated enterprise,' explicitly annotated line by line with what each setting buys in control terms (deny lists, sandboxed network egress, credential denial, managed-only hooks/MCP servers/plugin marketplaces, a minimum version floor) — deployed by the platform team via MDM so individual engineers cannot edit or override any of it.
    → Makes concrete what 'governance enforced as code rather than through committees' (P12/A1) looks like at the infrastructure level, going well beyond the single-hook examples used elsewhere in the piece.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How should the SDLC's controls be redesigned once writing code is no longer the slow, expensive step?
    Rebuild the lifecycle as a loop of six stages, each committing a version-controlled artifact that triggers and is read by the next stage, with governance embedded as hooks and managed settings rather than periodic review.
  • ✅ Where does human judgment still belong once agents do most of the work end to end?
    At artifact-acceptance gates (reviewing intent.md, spec.md, plan.md, the PR) and in judging intent and risk, not in mechanically checking each edit.
  • ✅ How does an organization keep legacy systems of record (Jira, ServiceNow, change boards) coherent with new markdown-first artifacts?
    Explicitly designate one source of truth per artifact — the repo, the legacy system, or a cross-linked pair — rather than letting two systems silently diverge.
  • ⭕ Does the described transformation actually produce better, safer, or faster software when measured, as opposed to merely being plausible in principle?
    The piece specifies leading and lagging indicators for every play, but presents them prescriptively (what you should measure) and never reports the resulting numbers from a real deployment, despite claiming the practices are drawn from daily work with customers.

Gaps

  • incompleteEvery play redefines the human's role (from author to reviewer, from implementer to orchestrator, from on-call responder to incident-queue triager) but the piece never addresses the human cost of that shift — skill atrophy from no longer writing code directly, morale effects of reviewing AI output all day, or how an organization trains new engineers who never practiced the skills the plays assume a senior reviewer already has.
    Read next: Look for practitioner accounts (not vendor playbooks) of how teams that have run this kind of pipeline for a year or more describe the experience of the reviewer-not-author role, and whether junior engineers develop judgment without first doing the work themselves.
  • incompleteThe guide is written from a single-team, single-repo vantage point (one intent/ folder, one REVIEW.md, one set of skills) even though it recommends org-wide managed settings; it never addresses how multiple teams' independently evolved intent.md templates, skills, and REVIEW.md policies stay compatible, or what happens when one team's skill or hook conflicts with another's as code or ownership crosses team boundaries.
    Read next: Platform-engineering and multi-team governance literature on keeping independently-evolving, repo-local policy artifacts from diverging across an organization.
  • uninformedThe piece is presented as consolidating 'real best practices our Applied AI team executes on a daily basis for our customers,' but every quantitative claim (leading/lagging indicators, 'elapsed time falls from weeks to hours') is prescriptive guidance on what to measure, not a reported result; no outcome data from an actual deployment appears anywhere in the piece.
    Read next: Look for independent case studies, post-mortems, or DORA-style empirical measurement of organizations that adopted this specific stage structure, as distinct from vendor-authored playbooks.
  • incompleteThe piece diagnoses, at the individual level, that unstructured agent use creates an orchestration tax paid through shallow review or cognitive surrender — but never asks the organizational mirror question: whether the governance apparatus it prescribes (REVIEW.md passes, hooks, managed settings, eval suites, per-stage indicators) can itself become a new review-queue bottleneck once a tech lead or platform engineer has to maintain all of it across many repos and teams.
    Read next: Read Addy Osmani's orchestration-tax and intent-debt pieces for the individual-level version of this dynamic, then ask what the team/platform-level equivalent of 'the correct number of parallel agents equals what you can review' is for governance artifacts themselves.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • The New SDLC With Vibe Coding: From ad-hoc prompting to Agentic Engineering · g3 — Claxton's playbook is the detailed, stage-by-stage answer the gap asks for: it walks through exactly how team/org structure evolves — the product owner reviews intent.md and spec.md, the engineer reviews plan.md and the diff, the tech lead sets REVIEW.md policy and tunes it monthly, the release manager authorizes production deploys via a hook, and on-call shifts from reactive paging to triaging an autonomously-generated intent.md queue in Stage 6 — with governance embedded as hooks and managed settings rather than left as an unaddressed bullet point.
