You’re losing valuable time and money every day. Here’s how to stop.

Daniel Altman (guest post on Thinking in Bets, Annie Duke's Substack) · email · 2026-10-12 Reader 🖥️

Decision-Making Practices
Economics, understood as the science of wellbeing, gives ordinary people a benchmark for decisions that lets them recover real value in both time and money (shown by a back-of-envelope grocery example, with bigger savings promised in the author's book), provided decisions are judged by what they do for wellbeing rather than by income or money alone.

practical · mixed — Guest post by economist Daniel Altman (author of 'The Best Decisions You'll Ever Make'), published in Annie Duke's Thinking in Bets newsletter; the Reader author field names the newsletter, not Altman. Key terms

  • Economics as the science of wellbeing
    Economics is fundamentally about maximizing satisfaction/happiness/welfare ('the good stuff'), with money only instrumental to that.
  • Benchmark / frame of reference
    A reference standard from economics against which a decision is judged while the decider weighs all other factors; it guides but does not decide.
  • Value of time
    A person's time is priced at an hourly rate ($30) and counted alongside cash savings, as foregone side-hustle income or enjoyment.

Propositions

  • explicitSmall everyday choices (when and where to shop, whether to use a loyalty program) carry recoverable value, around 5-10% of a grocery bill and 5-10 minutes per trip.conf: high
  • explicitTime saved should be priced at an opportunity-cost rate (here $30/hour) and added to cash savings when evaluating a decision.conf: high
  • explicitApplying economic tools to everyday decisions yields about $375 per year in time and money from the grocery example alone, and thousands from big decisions (car, house, retirement).conf: medium
  • explicitEconomics is at heart the science of wellbeing, and money matters only insofar as it buys satisfaction.conf: high
  • explicitDeciding on wellbeing rather than money changes the optimal choice: for example, plan retirement spending around the healthy years you can actually enjoy rather than a constant annual income, and consider family size by the effect on your own happiness.conf: medium
  • explicitEconomics does not have all the answers but supplies a benchmark that makes decisions fall into place more easily and brings clarity.conf: high
  • implicitDecision quality is judged by the expected value or wellbeing the decision creates ex ante, and can be optimized with known tools, rather than by an uncertain outcome.conf: medium
  • implicitThe hourly value of freed time is the same at the margin for every minute saved, and the saved time is actually redeployed to something valuable.conf: medium

Arguments

Readers will gain about $375 a year (and more on bigger decisions) by applying simple economic reasoning to decisions. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Shopping on delivery days and off-peak hours improves selection and saves 5-10% of the bill and 5-10 minutes per trip.
  • Phone-based loyalty programs add savings while clumsy ones are not worth using.
  • Time is worth $30 an hour.
  • A person shops fifty weeks a year.
Implicit premises:
  • The supermarket's delivery schedule is knowable and a weekly shopper's schedule is flexible enough to follow it.
  • Any freed time would genuinely be converted to income or enjoyment at $30/hour.
  • Savings percentages generalize from the author's example to typical households (no data are given).
  • A $100 weekly bill is representative.
  • A small example scales to big decisions in the same way.
Inferences:
  • The same calculation would yield much smaller or negative net gains for someone with a rigid schedule, a lower bill or no use for the saved time.
  • Economic benchmarks are most valuable on repeated, frequent decisions, where small per-decision gains compound.
People should make decisions on the basis of wellbeing rather than money, and economics offers the benchmark for doing so. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Economics is the science of wellbeing.
  • Money matters only when it buys satisfaction.
  • Retirement income needs differ across ages; family-size happiness may be limited by the least happy child.
Implicit premises:
  • Wellbeing is measurable and comparable enough across options to serve as a decision benchmark.
  • Standard economics (as taught) already defines utility in ways that make this operational.
  • Individuals can forecast their own future satisfaction accurately (affective forecasting).
Inferences:
  • If wellbeing is hard to forecast, the economics benchmark inherits the forecasting error and needs the outside-view and pre-commitment tools decision science offers.
  • Wellbeing-optimizing and money-optimizing can recommend opposite choices, so the choice of objective is itself a high-stakes decision.

Narratives

  • The supermarket-trip walkthrough: the reader is told to shop on delivery days, early in the day, and with a phone-based loyalty app, then the savings are totalled.
    → Shows concretely that tiny economic reasoning yields real savings, supporting the claim that the same approach scales to big decisions.

Questions addressed

  • ⭕ How can ordinary people use economics to make better decisions with their time and money?
    Gestured at through one illustration and a wellbeing reframe; the actual tools are deferred to the book.
  • ✅ What should decisions optimize for: money or wellbeing?
    Wellbeing; money is instrumental.
  • ⭕ How should a decider handle uncertainty about outcomes and about their own future preferences?
    Not addressed.

Gaps

  • incompleteDecisions are treated as deterministic optimization: no discussion of uncertainty, outcome variance, luck, or judging a decision by its process rather than its result. The grocery example has no uncertainty at all, so nothing tests how the benchmark behaves when probabilities, base rates or hindsight enter, which is exactly where decision-science authors locate the difficulty.
    Read next: Compare with Annie Duke's decision-quality-vs-outcome material (extract-annie-duke-how-to-make-better-decisions-svic) and read the book itself to see whether the economists' toolkit includes expected value under uncertainty and forecasting.
  • incompleteThe $375 figure rests on stipulated savings rates, a flat $30/hour time value, and the assumption that saved minutes are redeployed productively; no data or sensitivity analysis is offered, and the marginal-time valuation is the swing factor. The headline 'you lose time and money every day' is not demonstrated beyond one example.
    Read next: Look for empirical work on the real value of small time savings and on behavioral effects of valuing time (time-is-money framing), plus a critical review of the book.
  • incompleteHow wellbeing is to be measured, forecast and compared across options (retirement years, number of children) is not explained, nor how economics 'as the science of wellbeing' reconciles with people's known errors in predicting their own future happiness.
    Read next: Affective-forecasting and hedonic-adaptation research; Duke's pre-commitment/future-self material for how to decide on behalf of a changing self.
