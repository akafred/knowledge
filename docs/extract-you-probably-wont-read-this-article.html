You Probably Won’t Read This Article…and That’s OK

The collapse in the cost of producing credible-looking work — papers, code, bug reports, blog posts — across every domain is the latest instance of a recurring historical pattern in which cheap production overwhelms a scarcity-based trust signal and forces a new socio-institutional gate to be invented, but for AI specifically neither leaning on existing human-centric gatekeeping institutions (which entrenches incumbents) nor building an AI-based gatekeeper (which inherits the same blind spots and incentive problems as what it gates) is a clean solution.

theoretical · mixed — Primarily theoretical — the author is diagnosing a dynamic (why credible-looking content is flooding every domain) and searching for a conceptual understanding of what comes next, not prescribing a specific action. The mode mixes exposition (the cost-collapse diagnosis), narrative (the printing-press/journal history), and argument (evaluating two candidate solutions and rejecting both).

Key terms

  • gate Credibility Gating Institutions
    Any socio-institutional mechanism — scarcity, a curated journal, peer review, a credential, a citation count, an algorithmic ranking — that stands between production and consumption, rationing scarce attention and vouching for quality.
  • credible-looking
    The surface appearance of rigor and quality (citations, data, p-values, plausible prose, proper structure) that AI can now produce almost for free, and which does not reliably correlate with actual soundness — a deliberate split between appearance and substance.
  • the baby (in 'throw the baby out with the bath slop')
    The genuinely good, often undiscovered work that risks being filtered out along with AI slop if a gate is too restrictive or too entrenched — his coined variant of the idiom names the specific risk of over-correcting.
  • scarcity gate Credibility Gating Institutions
    The pre-modern default gate, in which the sheer difficulty and cost of producing something (a hand-copied book, a hard-won degree) functioned as an imperfect proxy filter for whether it was worth attention.

Arguments

A new socio-institutional gate is needed to replace the collapsed scarcity/credibility signal. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The cost of producing credible-looking work has collapsed across domains (p1).
  • Historically, whenever production cost collapses, old gates fail and new institutional gates get invented (p3).
  • Existing digital-era gates (popularity/engagement proxies) were already imperfect and are now overwhelmed by both AI volume and their own perverse feedback loops (p4, p5).
Implicit premises:
  • Some rationing/signaling mechanism is necessary in principle for any attention economy — pure abundance without gating cannot self-sort into worth-reading and not-worth-reading.
  • Quality is not directly perceivable at the moment of choice, so a third-party proxy signal is structurally required.
Inferences:
  • If the historical precedent holds, whatever gate comes next will itself eventually be overwhelmed by cheaper ways of gaming it — implying gates are a permanent arms race rather than a stable equilibrium, a conclusion the author gestures at but never states outright.
Neither of the two obvious candidate solutions — lean on human-centric gates, or build an AI-based gate — is a clean answer. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Leaning on human gates entrenches incumbents and undoes the democratizing benefit of cheap production (p6).
  • An AI gate inherits the blind spots of the model it's built from (turtles-all-the-way-down) and creates a new deception-arms-race incentive between generator and gatekeeper (p7, p8).
Implicit premises:
  • A viable gate must both preserve broad access (avoid incumbency entrenchment) and reliably discriminate quality (avoid inheriting blind spots) — the author treats these as necessary joint criteria for 'clean' without ever stating them as explicit criteria.
Inferences:
  • Since each named candidate fails a different one of these two criteria, a workable gate — if one exists — probably has to be a hybrid or something structurally different from either, an inference the author gestures toward ('Maybe the answer lies somewhere between the two avenues') but never develops.

Narratives

  • Daniel Stenberg (curl maintainer) documenting a rising tide of plausible-but-mostly-worthless AI-generated bug reports — cheap to produce, expensive to validate.
    → Grounds the abstract cost-asymmetry thesis (p1, p2) in a concrete, verifiable case and sets the article's opening emotional register ('drowning').
  • The author's own experiment prompting Claude Code to research an alignment idea, which produced a full, professional-looking LaTeX paper (real experiments, p-values, figures, mostly-real bibliography) that was substantively poor but would take a domain expert only 2–3 minutes to spot as slop.
    → Personalizes and self-implicates the author in the dynamic he critiques, and supplies a first-hand, calibrated cost estimate for the validation side of the cost asymmetry.
  • The centuries-long history of 'too much to know' complaints — Conrad Gessner (1545) on the 'confusing and harmful abundance of books,' Barnaby Rich (1613) on 'the multiplicity of books,' Ann Blair's historical framing, and Schopenhauer's tirade against 'the immense number of bad books' — culminating in the 1665 invention of the curated journal by Oldenburg and de Sallo.
    → Implicitly supports the argument that 'gate collapse → new gate invented' is a recurring historical pattern rather than a novel AI-era problem, licensing the analogy to today.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why are open-source maintainers and academic journals suddenly overwhelmed by low-quality submissions?
    Because AI has collapsed the cost of producing credible-looking work while the cost of validating it stayed constant, so volume floods past whatever gate previously worked.
  • ⭕ What new gate should replace scarcity/human-judgment gates now that AI floods them?
  • ⭕ Is AI-driven credibility collapse categorically different from earlier information-abundance crises (printing press, industrialized printing), or just a faster instance of the same pattern?
    The author flags caution ('AI is a truly novel technology in its radical generality') but never actually settles the question either way.
  • ⭕ Who would build, own, or be incentivized to build the next gate?
