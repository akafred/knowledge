Cory Doctorow deserves to be the one skeptical voice in an otherwise optimistic reading list because his critique is disciplined by tracing where money and accountability actually flow rather than by reflexive contrarianism, and that discipline yields two portable diagnostic tools — centaur vs. reverse centaur, and the accountability sink — for judging whether a given AI tool serves the person using it or the vendor selling it.

Key terms

  • centaur / reverse centaur
    A diagnostic pair for classifying any human-AI pairing: a centaur is a person who stays in charge and does better work with a tool's help; a reverse centaur is a person who serves the tool, scrambling to keep pace with software that sets the terms.
  • accountability sink
    A structural arrangement in which a human formally 'in the loop' (e.g., signing off on an AI's output) absorbs legal/professional liability for a decision they did not really originate or fully control, while their presence functions as insulation for whoever sold or deployed the system.

Arguments

Doctorow is worth trusting as the dissenting, skeptical voice on AI. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • He punctures hype and is rigorous.
  • He has no financial stake requiring him to soften his views (marked by his years at the EFF).
  • He traces the mechanism by which things could unravel rather than making date-bound predictions.
  • A good share of the time, the mechanisms he identifies do come unraveled.
Implicit premises:
  • A demonstrated track record of correctly identifying failure mechanisms is a reliable signal of analytical quality, independent of whether any specific prediction was confirmed on schedule.
  • Firsthand, decades-long familiarity with someone's output (since 2002) is itself credible evidence of their reliability, not merely anecdote.
Inferences:
  • If mechanism-tracing without date-setting is the standard for trustworthy AI skepticism, commentators who instead make falsifiable, date-bound collapse predictions should be weighted as more brittle — a comparison the piece never draws explicitly (e.g., against other AI-bubble commentators in this corpus).
The centaur/reverse-centaur distinction is a useful evaluative tool for any AI tool decision. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • A centaur stays in charge and does better work than alone.
  • A reverse centaur serves a machine that sets the pace.
  • The same software can produce either outcome, so the deciding factor is how it's used.
Implicit premises:
  • The user has enough agency over how a tool is used for the question to be actionable — the framework presumes usage is a free individual choice, not one structurally imposed by an employer's metrics, an SLA, or the tool's own interface incentives.
  • The question is answerable through the user's own self-reflection, without needing external verification or measurement.
Inferences:
  • If the same software can go either way, then at scale it is organizational policy and tool design — not just individual usage discipline — that determines which regime actually prevails; the piece stops at the individual heuristic and doesn't follow this to the organizational level.
The accountability sink explains why 'human in the loop' can fail as a safeguard even when the human is diligent. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • The radiologist signs off but the AI made the call; the vendor's terms of service ensure liability lands on the signer.
  • Attention drifts when a system is right most of the time — a task-structural feature, per Bainbridge (1983), not an individual failing.
Implicit premises:
  • Legal and contractual liability rules — who is deemed to have 'signed off' — are what create the sink; it is not solely a psychological/attentional phenomenon.
  • The person placed in the loop typically lacks the bargaining power to renegotiate the vendor's terms of service that assign them the liability.
Inferences:
  • If the accountability sink is jointly structural (contractual allocation of liability) and cognitive (vigilance decrement), then adding more review steps without changing either the liability allocation or the task design will not fix it, and may create a false sense of safety — the piece gestures at this ('isn't much of a plan on its own') but does not develop it into a policy recommendation.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why trust a skeptic when everyone else on the recommended list is optimistic?
    Because Doctorow's skepticism is disciplined by mechanism-tracing and freedom from financial stake, not by reflexive contrarianism.
  • ✅ How can you tell whether a given AI tool helps you or exploits you?
    The centaur/reverse-centaur question: does it let you do better work, or does it just make you keep up? — offered as a heuristic, not a rigorous decision framework.
  • ✅ Why does 'I always review it' fail as a safety plan for AI-assisted work?
    Because the accountability sink (liability assignment via signature) combines with vigilance decrement (attention drift when a system is usually right) to let errors through even diligent review.
  • ⭕ Once you accept the accountability sink and vigilance-decrement mechanisms are real, what should you actually do differently in practice?
    Not addressed — the piece names the mechanisms and points readers to Doctorow's own site/book, but stops short of any concrete practice or policy change beyond 'be more careful about who benefits.'

Gaps

  • incompleteThe centaur/reverse-centaur test presumes the user has free choice over how a tool is used, but many real deployments (metrics-driven review quotas, AI-embedded workflow software, employer mandates, interface defaults that nudge pace-setting) structurally impose the reverse-centaur mode regardless of individual intent. The piece never addresses who actually controls the answer to its own diagnostic question, or what a worker with no control over tool configuration is supposed to do with it.
    Read next: extract-mosseri-ai-tailwind-authenticity#g4 raises the mirror-image gap (no criteria for which workflows to hand to AI at an organizational level); also relevant: workplace-power and algorithmic-management literature on who actually sets the pace of AI-mediated work.
  • incompleteThe claim that Doctorow's identified mechanisms 'come unraveled' 'a good share of the time' is asserted with no track record, count, or falsifiability check — an ironic gap given that the piece's own stated standard for trustworthy commentary is that it traces mechanism rather than merely asserting outcomes.
    Read next: A systematic retrospective checking specific Doctorow mechanism-claims (enshittification predictions, AI-bubble timing claims) against what actually happened.
  • illogicalThe piece treats Doctorow's freedom from financial stake in AI's success as a mark of trustworthiness, but doesn't address that Doctorow, like any commentator with a public brand and a new book to sell, has a reputational/commercial interest running in the opposite direction: his identity and income depend on being the reliable hype-puncturer. This is the same blind spot the corpus already flags for a different AI skeptic.
    Read next: extract-marcus-mythos-overblown#g4 flags the identical gap for Gary Marcus ('does not disclose his own potential bias as a long-standing AI skeptic... His brand depends on debunking AI hype') — read together as a general pattern: skeptic-brand incentives are a symmetric case of the same structural-incentive logic the corpus applies to hype (vc-hype-incentive).
  • uninformedThe lawyer-specific accountability-sink claim (liability lands on whoever signs, regardless of who really made the call) is asserted without engaging actual bar-discipline opinions or case law on AI-assisted legal work — there are now numerous documented sanctions cases for AI-fabricated citations that would let the claim be checked against how courts and bars are actually allocating fault (e.g., whether reliance on an AI tool is treated as aggravating, mitigating, or irrelevant).
