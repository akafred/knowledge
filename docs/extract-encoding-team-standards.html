Encoding Team Standards

Rahul Garg · article · 2026-03-31 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent SkillsAgent Context Files (CLAUDE.md / AGENTS.md)GuardrailsIntent DebtOrganizational Legibility
A team's standards for how AI should generate, refactor, secure, and review code must stop living as tacit judgment in senior engineers' heads or as unread documentation, and instead be encoded as small, versioned, repository-resident AI instructions — because an instruction's execution is its application, which turns consistent team judgment into a side effect of the normal workflow rather than a separate step that depends on someone remembering and applying it under pressure.

practical · argumentative — A practitioner essay (part of a martinfowler.com series on reducing AI-development friction) that diagnoses a specific failure mode and argues for one concrete fix, illustrated with anecdotes from the author's consulting practice and closed with explicit adoption/calibration guidance.

Key terms

  • Executable governance
    Standards encoded as AI instructions whose execution constitutes their application — governance that happens as a side effect of the normal workflow rather than a separate, discipline-dependent step.
  • The Consistency Problem
    Two developers on the same team, using the same AI tool, same codebase, and same project context, producing materially different results — not because the AI's knowledge differs, but because the instructions given to it differ by person.
  • Four-element instruction anatomy (role definition, context requirements, categorized standards, output format)
    A specific, recurring structure the author claims any well-built executable instruction should have, regardless of its purpose (generation, refactoring, security, review) — categorized standards in particular encode the team's priority judgment (must/should/nice-to-have or critical/important/advisory), not just a flat list of knowledge.
  • Standards as shared infrastructure
    A prompt on an individual's machine is a personal productivity hack; the same prompt, versioned in the team's repository and reviewed through pull requests, has the same status as linting rules or CI/CD pipelines — a team-owned artifact, not personal preference.

Arguments

The AI-output consistency problem is a systems problem requiring a systems solution (executable instructions), not a skills problem solvable by training, documentation, or pairing. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Training juniors, writing better documentation, and more pairing 'all help, but they are slow and they do not scale.'
  • The relevant knowledge already exists on the team — it simply has no vehicle for consistent distribution.
Implicit premises:
  • Scaling consistent judgment across a growing team requires decoupling the judgment from any single person's memory and attention.
  • Any mechanism that still depends on a human remembering and applying a standard under time pressure (checklists, wikis) will fail the same way prior codification efforts have — 'that gap is where most codification efforts quietly fail.'
Inferences:
  • Only a mechanism whose execution IS the application — an instruction the AI runs — can close the documentation-to-practice gap that doomed prior codification attempts.
  • This implies skills-based interventions are not wrong, just insufficient on their own — they remain necessary for judgment calls that cannot be codified, but cannot by themselves solve the scaling problem.
Instruction-writing effort should be weighted toward earlier points in the workflow (generation, refactoring) rather than concentrated only at review-time, because the same team judgment applied earlier produces fewer downstream issues than the identical judgment applied only at the end. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Generation-time instructions 'prevent misalignment rather than catching it after the fact.'
  • 'Review-time is the last opportunity to catch misalignment.'
Implicit premises:
  • Misalignment caught later costs more (rework, additional review cycles) than the same misalignment prevented earlier.
  • Applying judgment earlier does not simply relocate the same amount of friction to an earlier stage, but strictly reduces total friction.
Inferences:
  • A team with the capacity to build only one instruction should generally prefer a generation or review instruction over a narrower refactoring-only instruction — matching the article's own explicit calibration advice.
  • This creates an implicit priority ordering (generation > refactoring/security > review > CI) for where to invest first, though the article does not state this ordering directly.

Narratives

  • On one project, the process of extracting a shared security instruction revealed that two senior engineers had quietly held different thresholds for what counted as a 'critical' versus 'important' security concern — a disagreement that had never surfaced because each had been reviewing different pull requests.
    → Supports the proposition that the extraction/interview process has value independent of the resulting artifact — it forces previously tacit, unexamined disagreement between senior engineers into the open.
  • After a resulting review instruction was adopted, a less experienced developer's first use of it flagged a missing authorization check on a newly added endpoint — exactly the kind of issue that had previously only been caught when a senior happened to be the reviewer.
    → Supports the proposition that these instructions make inexperience less costly rather than making the developer more experienced — illustrating the mechanism's practical payoff concretely rather than abstractly.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why does the same AI, on the same codebase with the same project context, produce materially different results for different developers?
    Because the instructions given to the AI (what to generate, check, flag, reject) vary by person, not because the AI's knowledge of the project varies.
  • ✅ Is this consistency problem best solved by more training, pairing, or documentation?
    No — explicitly argued to be insufficient because these approaches are slow and do not scale; a systems solution (executable instructions) is proposed instead.
  • ✅ Does every team need this?
    No — explicitly scale-calibrated via a heuristic (visible output-quality variance, or work routing through a few people who know how to prompt); 'teams of five may not need this, teams of fifteen almost certainly do.'
  • ✅ How do teams keep these instructions from becoming unmaintained, abandoned documentation?
    Repository placement plus the normal pull-request review workflow make drift visible in the ordinary course of work, without requiring a dedicated audit.
  • ⭕ Does the AI reliably execute a categorized-priority instruction as written, every time, under every context load?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article assumes that once a team standard is encoded as an executable instruction, the AI reliably executes it as written. It never engages instruction-following limits — an instruction competing for context/instruction budget against other project priming documents, or an AI silently failing to apply a 'categorized standard' under pressure — that this corpus's own AGENTS.md-bloat and context-budget literature treats as a real, measured failure mode.
    Read next: AGENTS.md/CLAUDE.md instruction-budget and bloat literature (e.g. studies measuring whether context files produce a measurable correctness gain) to test whether 'executable' instructions are reliably executed at scale, or degrade the same way bloated context files do.
    closed byStop Using /init for AGENTS.md — Supplies exactly the measured instruction-budget/bloat evidence that gap called for: two 2026 studies quantify how AGENTS.md content competes for context/instruction budget and silently degrades (cost +20%+, success -2-3% for auto-generated/redundant content), confirming that 'encoding a standard as an executable instruction' is not reliably executed at scale once the file grows — it degrades the same way bloated context files do.
  • incompleteNo discussion of what happens when instructions conflict with each other (e.g., a generation instruction's architectural-compliance requirement versus a refactoring instruction's 'avoid premature abstraction' guidance), or at what point a growing library of small, single-purpose instructions itself becomes a new coordination and maintenance burden analogous to the 'documentation graveyard' problem the article claims repository placement solves.
    Read next: Look for guidance or case studies on managing a growing library of composable AI instructions/skills at scale — conflict resolution, deprecation, and ownership once dozens of small instructions exist rather than one or two.
    closed byCoding Agent Development Workflows — Tune's single-source-of-truth fix (pointing every agent's and tool's own config, including a third-party tool like CodeRabbit, at the same shared convention/ADR files) directly resolves the 'instructions conflicting across tools' half of this gap with a concrete, working mechanism — though it does not address the gap's other half, the self-conflict risk as a library of many small instructions grows.
  • incompleteThe two central anecdotes (two seniors with different severity thresholds; a junior catching a missing auth check) are both single, author-reported incidents from the author's own consulting practice. No measurement is offered of defect rates, review-quality variance, or time-to-competency before versus after adopting these instructions across a real team over time.
    Read next: Look for quantitative studies measuring review-outcome or defect-rate variance across developers before and after adopting shared, versioned AI instructions, rather than anecdotal before/after accounts.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • The Agentic P&L: Beyond the Empire of Headcount · g5 — Gives one concrete, demonstrated method for the tacit-to-explicit conversion that gap found merely assumed: structured interviews with senior engineers, organized around pointed questions (what triggers rejection, what's checked instinctively, what separates clean from over-engineered refactoring), whose answers map directly onto instruction structures — with a documented case where the interview process itself surfaced a previously invisible disagreement between two seniors. Still single-practitioner, anecdotal evidence rather than the systematic knowledge-management literature that gap called for, so it narrows rather than fully closes the gap.
  • How Fast-Growing Companies Can Make Better Decisions · g5 — Offers a working counter-case to the bare assumption that gap flagged: this article shows tacit judgment partially captured and made explicit through a specific interview-to-instruction pipeline, with a concrete before/after example (a junior catching a missing authorization check). It doesn't address whether the resulting codified standard transfers across different contexts/teams, which is the deeper transferability question the gap raises, so it narrows the gap for within-team codification specifically rather than closing the cross-context transfer question.
