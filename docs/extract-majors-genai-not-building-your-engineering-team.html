Generative AI Is Not Going To Build Your Engineering Team For You

Charity Majors · article · 2024-06-10 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Durable, Domain-Owning Teams (vs. Fluid Teams)Junior Hiring EconomicsSmall Teams & CompressionSociotechnical ArchitectureSystemic Apprenticeship & Skill TransmissionTalent Density
Generative AI accelerates the easy, narrow, disposable part of software engineering (writing code) but does nothing for the hard part (building and operating the sociotechnical systems code lives in), so it cannot substitute for hiring and apprenticing junior engineers — teams, not individuals, are the real unit of software ownership, and starving the junior pipeline is cannibalizing the industry's own future senior engineers.

practical · argumentative — Argues against a specific prevailing claim (generative AI is making junior engineers unnecessary) toward a specific recommended action (keep hiring and training junior engineers, deliberately and continuously).

Key terms

  • Senior engineer
    Not a function of coding output or speed, but of the ability to understand, maintain, explain, and manage a large body of software in production over time, translate business needs into implementation, and teach — built through roughly seven-plus years of daily practice on a team alongside more experienced engineers.
  • Sociotechnical systems
    Software, tools, and people together — understanding a system requires familiarity with the interplay between code, users, production, infrastructure, and continuous change over time; these systems are complex, chaotic, nondeterministic, and subject to emergent behavior.
  • Apprenticeship industry
    A characterization of software engineering: you cannot learn the job from books or a CS degree alone; competence is built almost entirely through sustained, supervised, on-the-job practice over years.
  • Barrels vs. ammunition (implicit contrast, not named in this piece)
    n/a — not the author's term; included here only as a cross-reference point. Majors' own frame is 'team as the unit of productivity,' explicitly rejecting the idea that an individual's seniority alone determines their value to a team.

Arguments

Generative AI will not reduce the need to hire and train junior engineers, and treating it as a substitute is a mistake with long-term costs to the industry. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Writing code — the part AI accelerates — is the easy, non-bottleneck part of software engineering.
  • The hard, scarce capability (understanding and operating complex sociotechnical systems) is built only through years of supervised on-the-job apprenticeship, which requires there to be junior engineers in the pipeline in the first place.
  • Teams, not individuals, are the unit of software ownership, and functional teams require a genuine range of skill levels including juniors.
  • The true cost comparison between hiring a senior and hiring a junior is closer than commonly assumed once ramp time is properly accounted for on both sides.
Implicit premises:
  • Current junior engineers are the pool from which future senior engineers are drawn; there is no other viable supply mechanism for senior-level judgment.
  • The industry-wide, collective-action problem (every firm wants someone else to train juniors) will not resolve itself without explicit advocacy, because market incentives alone externalize the training cost.
Inferences:
  • If the premises hold, an organization that stops hiring juniors now because AI appears to substitute for their output is making a bet against its own future senior-engineer supply — a bet whose cost will only become visible 5-7+ years later, well past the tenure of the executives making the decision.

Narratives

  • The author's own origin story: homeschooled in rural Idaho, no computer until 16, a series of non-technical majors, who nonetheless entered the software industry as a complete novice and learned everything on the job during an era of low hiring standards and plentiful jobs — contrasted with her sister's two-year struggle to land a first engineering job 'a few years ago.'
    → Establishes both personal authority and the concrete, felt stakes of the 'bottleneck is hiring' argument — it supports the proposition that the industry's entry ladder has already narrowed sharply, independent of AI, and that AI-driven junior-hiring freezes would make an already-hard problem worse.
  • Kent Quirk's description of generative AI as 'an excitable junior engineer who types really fast.'
    → Supplies the central analogy the middle of the piece builds up and then dismantles — the narrative device lets the author concede AI's genuine resemblance to a junior engineer in one narrow respect (untrustworthy output) before showing the analogy breaks down everywhere else (no feedback loop, no apprenticeship, no team-culture contribution).
  • A vignette of a team staffed entirely with staff/principal engineers: competing for the 'fun' architecture work, picking technical fights, chronically under-documenting.
    → Concretely supports the proposition that single-level teams are dysfunctional, by showing the specific behavioral pathology (contention, over-engineering, under-documentation) rather than just asserting it abstractly.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Will generative AI make it unnecessary to hire and train junior software engineers?
    No — AI accelerates code-writing, which is the easy, non-bottleneck part of the job; the apprenticeship pipeline that produces senior judgment still requires junior hires.
  • ✅ What actually makes an engineer 'senior,' if not the ability to write code quickly?
    Judgment, systems understanding built over ~7+ years of practice, and the ability to teach — not output.
  • ✅ Should every company hire junior engineers?
    No — only companies with runway, slack, experienced management, a roadmap, and a willing mentor should; a bad junior-hiring experience is worse than none.
  • ⭕ If AI is especially good at exactly the tedious, repetitive, tightly-scoped code that intermediate and junior engineers traditionally cut their teeth on (web forms, login pages, boilerplate), where do new engineers get the volume of practice reps the author's own apprenticeship model says they need?
    Not addressed. The piece praises intermediate engineers for writing 'reams and reams' of exactly the kind of code it elsewhere identifies as AI's sweet spot, without reconciling the two claims.

Gaps

  • illogicalThe piece simultaneously argues (a) that generative AI is especially good at tedious, predictable, tightly-scoped code, and (b) that junior and intermediate engineers develop their skill precisely by writing large volumes of that same kind of code ('reams and reams,' web forms, login pages 'for the 1000th time'). If AI increasingly absorbs exactly those reps, the piece's own apprenticeship timeline (seven-plus years of daily practice) is put under pressure that the piece never acknowledges or addresses.
    Read next: Look for later work (including the author's own 2026 piece on AI skepticism, extract-charity-majors-stop-being-skeptical-ai-development) that revisits whether AI-written code changes what apprenticeship requires, or whether the practice-reps juniors need shift from writing code to reviewing/debugging/understanding it.
    closed byThe Bet On Juniors Just Got Better — Majors' piece leaves unresolved how juniors can still develop skill through 'reams and reams' of tedious code if AI increasingly absorbs exactly that work. Beck's augmented-coding model answers this directly: AI collapses the mechanical search/exploration part of a task (which API to use, syntax lookups) rather than the judgment part, and the time saved is reinvested in deliberate reflection (tradeoffs, alternative approaches, tests, performance) -- so the practice-reps shift from writing boilerplate to evaluating and extending AI-surfaced options, rather than disappearing, provided management deliberately manages for that outcome.
  • incompleteThe 'first job is murder' bottleneck is framed almost entirely through a US, Big-Tech-adjacent lens (top colleges, feeder systems, connections) without addressing how the dynamic plays out in different labor markets, education systems, or company sizes outside that frame — e.g. apprenticeship or vocational-training traditions in other countries that structure entry-level hiring differently.
    Read next: Look for comparative accounts of entry-level engineering hiring outside the US Big-Tech pipeline, or vocational/apprenticeship-model traditions (e.g. German Ausbildung, or Norwegian fagbrev-style models) applied to software.
