Is Your Organizational Culture Too Nice?

Ron Ashkenas, Gali Cooks · article · 2026-08-19 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Change Management & AdoptionGiving Feedback (Performance & Coaching)Niceness Culture (Comfort Over Candor)Psychological Safety
Organizational cultures of "niceness" — which prioritize harmony, comfort and consensus over candor, accountability and results — measurably compromise performance, and leaders can reverse this by modeling peer candor at the senior-team level (starting with the CEO), demonstrating the results of rigor through data-backed pilots, and redesigning operational review and governance meetings so they require substantive debate rather than ratify decisions as a fait accompli.

practical · mixed — Argumentative-expository core (three named strategies) carried almost entirely by consulting case narratives; the piece explicitly argues for action ("here are three strategies to pursue"), not merely for understanding.

Key terms

  • niceness
    An organizational (not just individual) orientation that prioritizes others' comfort and avoiding difficult conversations or decisions over candor, accountability, and results.
  • good (vs. nice)
    Leaning into hard conversations and decisions in service of organizational performance, per the authors' earlier companion piece.
  • fait accompli meeting (FAM) Niceness Culture (Comfort Over Candor)
    A review or decision meeting where, from the outset, everyone treats the outcome as predetermined — orchestrated, over-rehearsed presentations that make it socially costly for a senior leader to raise objections without embarrassing the presenter.
  • rigorous culture
    An organizational culture that holds people accountable to high standards and structurally encourages substantive challenge and debate rather than polite consensus.

Arguments

Senior leadership teams should adopt structured, confidential peer feedback processes — with the CEO going first — to shift their team's culture from niceness toward rigor. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Culture cascades from senior leaders' modeled behaviors down through managers to the whole organization.
  • When the CEO models openness about their own shortcomings, it signals the goal is collective improvement, not evaluation or blame.
  • In the offsite case, a planned 90-minute session stretched to three hours of substantive, previously-avoided debate once the CEO went first.
Implicit premises:
  • Leaders follow the visible behavior of their most senior peer more readily than abstract instructions to "be more candid."
  • Confidential, structured feedback — rather than ad hoc criticism — is what makes candor psychologically safe enough for leaders who "have the same psychological and emotional reservations about being tough" as anyone else.
Inferences:
  • Organizations with no formal senior-team feedback process are structurally biased toward niceness by default, independent of any individual leader's intentions or values — a generalization the authors imply but don't state.
Operational review and governance meetings should be redesigned around key unanswered questions, to prevent them from becoming "fait accompli meetings." strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • FAMs occur when presentations are "carefully orchestrated... meticulously prepared and practiced... in a way that makes it difficult for a senior leader to raise questions or objections without embarrassing someone."
  • When leaders fail to raise tough questions, these become "project review theater" rather than genuine working sessions.
Implicit premises:
  • The social cost of publicly embarrassing a presenter (after visible rehearsal, in front of peers) outweighs, for most individual leaders, the value of raising an objection — niceness is a locally rational choice for the individual even when collectively harmful.
  • Refocusing the agenda on unanswered questions is sufficient to overcome that social cost, without further structural change such as anonymous input or external facilitation.
Inferences:
  • The existence of a regular review-meeting cadence is not evidence that decisions are actually being scrutinized — a point relevant to any organization that measures itself by meeting frequency rather than meeting content, which the authors imply but never state directly.

Narratives

  • An unnamed firm where virtually every employee "met" or "exceeded" expectations in year-end ratings, avoiding tough performance conversations but producing mediocre results and lost market leadership to more aggressive competitors.
    → Supports p1/p6 — shows niceness culture manifesting concretely in performance management and its competitive cost.
  • A sales force pressured to sell too many low-margin, increasingly commoditized products, where no senior leader wanted the tough conversation with long-standing customers about changing buying patterns, producing disappointing sales and margins.
    → Supports p1 — a second, independent instance of niceness (avoiding hard customer conversations) directly costing financial performance.
  • "Overly large and ineffective" nonprofit boards that failed to hold CEOs accountable for almost any results, producing good relationships but poor organizational performance.
    → Supports p1/p8 — extends the niceness-costs-performance claim from interpersonal management to governance.
  • Ron's leadership-team offsite: confidential peer interviews before the event, followed by the CEO going first in owning her own conflict-avoidance, turned a planned 90-minute session into three hours of open challenge and specific behavioral commitments.
    → Supports argument 1 — demonstrates the CEO-goes-first mechanism working in a concrete, detailed instance.
  • Herman Miller's Aeron chair, whose culture of maximal customer choice produced 140 million possible configurations and rising costs, until a new VP used data showing only ~4,000 configurations were ever ordered (400 constituting the majority of sales) to convince marketing to halve the customization areas — improving margins without losing sales volume.
    → Supports argument 2 / the "show evidence" strategy — demonstrates a data-driven pilot overcoming a comfortable-but-costly organizational default, without relying on the peer-feedback mechanism.
  • A nonprofit CEO search where the board deliberately avoided a large, low-friction search committee, instead forming a small committee with explicit norms encouraging debate, structured interviews and clear evaluation criteria — ultimately recommending a single, unanimously approved candidate.
    → Supports argument 2 / p8 — a governance-level case of replacing a "nice" default process (broad committee, informal consensus) with a rigorous one.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What accounts for differences in financial and mission performance across otherwise-comparable organizations?
    Attributed substantially to whether the culture is "nice" (avoids candor/accountability) or "rigorous" (embraces it) — though the authors are explicit this comes from their own consulting experience, not a controlled comparison.
  • ✅ How does an organizational culture actually change, given that each individual leader shares the same psychological resistance to being tough?
    Structured peer feedback starting with the most senior leader, small data-backed pilots, and redesigned review-meeting agendas.
  • ⭕ Is there a point at which candor/rigor becomes its own dysfunction — punitive callouts, weaponized "radical candor," or debate fatigue?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe central causal claim — that niceness culture measurably compromises financial performance and rigor culture measurably improves it — rests entirely on the authors' own consulting anecdotes plus a single Bain survey about behavior prevalence (not about performance outcomes). No controlled comparison, industry benchmark, or correlational study directly connects "niceness" to performance metrics.
    Read next: Organizational-culture research with actual performance correlations (e.g., Kotter & Heskett's culture-and-performance work, or Denison culture-survey studies) to test whether the niceness/performance link holds outside these authors' own client base.
  • illogicalThe Bain statistic ("only one in five of the highest-performing teams consistently exhibit these behaviors") is used to argue candor is rare and valuable, but on its face it also shows four in five top-performing teams do NOT consistently exhibit these behaviors — i.e., high performance is evidently possible without them most of the time. The article doesn't reconcile this tension.
    Read next: The original Bain "At the Top, It's All About Teamwork" study, to see whether Bain's own analysis treats these behaviors as necessary, sufficient, or merely correlated with top-quintile performance.
  • incompleteNo discussion of the failure mode where "rigor" curdles into punitive callout culture, defensiveness, or debate fatigue — no treatment of how much candor is enough, or how leaders should calibrate between corrosive niceness and corrosive harshness.
    Read next: Kim Scott's Radical Candor (care personally + challenge directly) and the corpus's psychological-safety literature on the difference between candor and blame.
