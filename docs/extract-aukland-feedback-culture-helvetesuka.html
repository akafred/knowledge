The best teams win not by being nicest but by being most honest with each other immediately and without softening — and most Norwegian workplaces underperform not from too much conflict but from too much silence.

practical · mixed — A LinkedIn post arguing for a specific organizational practice (build a direct, blunt feedback culture); the argument is carried mainly through a single personal narrative and a statistic, then generalized into a maxim, and closes with a speaking-engagement pitch.

Key terms

  • Helvetesuka ("Hell Week")
    A special-forces-style endurance training week (instructors drawn from Forsvarets spesialstyrker) that the author underwent, used as the site where the feedback-culture lesson was learned.
  • å si ifra som det er, uten pynting ("say it as it is, without dressing it up")
    Immediate, unsoftened, literal statement of what one actually observes or thinks about a teammate or plan — the specific behavior he is advocating for, distinct from generic 'honesty' or 'feedback.'
  • tilbakemeldingskultur ("feedback culture")
    An organizational norm, built deliberately (as instructors built it in his team), where blunt, immediate feedback is expected and safe to give — framed as a transferable asset from elite sport to business.
  • stillhet ("silence") vs. konflikt ("conflict")
    A deliberate contrast: he argues Norwegian workplaces' problem is the absence of stated disagreement/observation (silence), not its presence (conflict) — reframing what looks like harmony as actually a performance deficit.

Arguments

Norwegian organizations should deliberately build a direct, blunt feedback culture rather than prioritizing politeness. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Silence about problems is widespread among Norwegian workers (1 in 4), driven by fear of job consequences and perceived disloyalty.
  • Silence is a performance problem, not merely a wellbeing problem.
  • The author's own elite-training team's performance improved markedly once politeness was replaced by immediate, blunt feedback.
Implicit premises:
  • What builds team cohesion and performance under extreme physical/mental stress (special-forces-style training, elite sport) generalizes to ordinary knowledge-work and organizational settings.
  • The short-term discomfort of direct feedback is a small, tolerable cost relative to the performance and clarity gained.
  • Teams that avoid stating disagreement openly do not thereby become safer or more harmonious — they merely displace the cost into ambiguity and wasted energy spent guessing.
Inferences:
  • If silence itself — not the underlying problems being silenced about — is a primary performance drag, leaders should treat 'permission to be blunt' as a designed team practice rather than an incidental cultural trait.
  • Because 39% of non-reporters cite fear of career consequences, closing the gap plausibly requires structural, leadership-guaranteed safety, not just individual courage or willpower.

Narratives

  • The Helvetesuka arc: the team starts out overly polite and avoidant, performing poorly; special-forces instructors impose the rule 'say it exactly as it is, immediately'; the team begins bluntly confronting each other ('You're falling behind,' 'That plan won't work,' 'I have nothing left right now'); the discomfort lasts about three seconds and then becomes liberating; team performance and clarity improve.
    → Carries the entire thesis experientially — it *shows* that honesty beats politeness for team performance and that the emotional cost of bluntness is small and short-lived, without the author having to argue this abstractly.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do teams underperform even when everyone gets along and is polite to each other?
    Because politeness suppresses the direct information team members need (who is struggling, which plans won't work), forcing them to waste energy guessing instead of acting on clear information.
  • ⭕ How should the discomfort of blunt feedback be handled so directness doesn't curdle into a harmful environment?
    He asserts that manner-of-delivery and what happens in the minutes afterward ('dette er viktig') are what separate a good hard environment from a bad one, but never specifies what good delivery or good repair actually consists of.
  • ⭕ Is 'not reporting reprehensible conditions' (the cited statistic) the same phenomenon as 'not giving a teammate blunt performance feedback' (the anecdote)?

Gaps

  • illogicalThe post conflates two different phenomena: 'kritikkverdige forhold' (reprehensible/reportable conditions — closer to whistleblowing territory, e.g. safety or ethics violations) in the cited Ledelsesbarometer statistic, with routine peer performance feedback in the anecdote and thesis ('you're falling behind'). The 39%-fear-of-consequences and 33%-disloyalty findings describe barriers to reporting misconduct upward under real power asymmetry and retaliation risk — a materially different risk calculus than a peer bluntly critiquing an equal's performance during physical training with no formal hierarchy between them. Treating both as the same 'silence problem' may overstate how directly the Helvetesuka lesson transfers to the survey's actual finding.
    Read next: Read alongside whistleblowing and psychological-safety literature that separates upward disclosure of misconduct (protected by policy, law, anonymity) from lateral peer-to-peer candor (protected by team norms and trust) — e.g. Amy Edmondson's psychological safety research, which explicitly distinguishes speaking up about errors/concerns from routine task feedback.
  • incompleteThe post asserts that 'how it's said' and 'what happens in the minutes afterward' are what separate a good hard environment from a bad one ('dette er viktig') but never explains what good delivery or good repair actually consists of — it's flagged as the crux and then left unaddressed, doubling as a hook for his paid-speaking pitch rather than a resolved point.
    Read next: Read HBR's management-tips article on giving feedback (extract-hbr-management-tips-giving-feedback), which specifies concrete guardrails — no shaming, a behavior/why/next-step structure, a deliberate pause, and fast repair when feedback lands badly — for exactly this gap.
  • incompleteNo engagement with selection/context bias: the Helvetesuka cohort is self-selected for extreme resilience and volunteered into a short, bounded, high-trust context with external instructors imposing the bluntness norm from outside the group. It's left unexamined whether 'say it exactly as it is, immediately' scales to an ordinary employment relationship with power asymmetry, job security concerns, and no external referee — precisely the setting of the 39%-fear-of-consequences respondents cited earlier in the same post.
    Read next: Read organizational-culture research on how psychological safety is actually built and scaled outside high-selection, high-trust cohorts — e.g. extract-is-your-organizational-culture-too-nice (senior-leader modeling as the actual scaling mechanism) or Edmondson's team-level psychological safety studies.
