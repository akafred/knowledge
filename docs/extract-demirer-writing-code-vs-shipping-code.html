Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools

Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, Liyuan Yang · pdf · 2026-05-27 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Code Was Never the BottleneckDORA & Engineering MetricsMeasuring AI ImpactOrchestration Tax / Human Review BottleneckPull Requests & Async Code Review
Across three successive generations of AI coding tools, large task-level productivity gains (up to 180% more commits) attenuate sharply as they propagate up the software production hierarchy to final shipped output (only 20-30% more releases), because AI and downstream human effort are strong complements rather than substitutes (estimated elasticity of substitution of about 0.25) — so increasing automation of code-writing alone yields only bounded gains in what actually reaches users, a pattern that also shows up in aggregate GitHub activity and across four app marketplaces, where new app releases have risen but end-user usage has not.

theoretical · mixed — An NBER working paper: large-N empirical measurement (matched event studies over 100,000+ GitHub developers plus four app-marketplace panels) combined with a formal hierarchical production model used to interpret the empirical attenuation pattern.

Key terms

  • weak-link hypothesis / bottleneck hypothesis
    When stages of a production process are complements rather than substitutes, automating one stage has a bounded effect on total output because the stage with the least capacity caps the whole chain — applied here vertically, within a single software production process, to the layers code/commits/PRs/releases.
  • elasticity of substitution (between upstream AI output and downstream human effort)
    The key structural parameter of the model: how substitutable AI-generated output at one layer is for human effort at the next layer. Estimated at 0.25 — close to Leontief (no substitutability) — indicating strong complementarity.
  • augmentation vs. automation
    Two distinct, mutually exclusive channels through which AI enters a layer in the model: augmentation multiplies the value of a human's own effort (human still does the work, faster); automation produces output directly, but that output is still Leontief-complementary with human review effort — unreviewed AI output is modeled as unproductive.
  • production hierarchy
    The ordered sequence of software-development outputs, each aggregating the layer below it: lines of code → files → commits → pull requests → projects/repositories → releases.
  • task-level productivity vs. final output
    Task-level productivity = how much more of a narrow unit of work (e.g. commits) a developer produces after adopting a tool. Final output = what is actually shipped (releases) and, beyond that, what reaches and is used by end users (app usage).

Arguments

Large task-level AI productivity gains in software have so far translated only partially into shipped, used software. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Commit-level gains of up to 180% (cumulative across tool generations).
  • Release-level gains of only 20-30% for the same adopters.
  • App-marketplace release counts are up since mid-2025, but usage within the first three months is flat across all four marketplaces studied.
Implicit premises:
  • Shipped and used output, not lines-of-code, commits, or even pull requests, is the economically relevant measure of software productivity.
  • The attenuation pattern is not simply a measurement artifact of AI-generated verbosity or changed work granularity (addressed via placebo and robustness checks).
Inferences:
  • Teams and tool vendors should not treat upstream activity metrics (lines of code, commits, even PRs) as a reliable proxy for value delivered, since the paper's central finding is exactly that this proxy increasingly diverges from shipped, used output as AI capability rises.
The attenuation is best explained by strong complementarity (not substitutability) between AI and human effort at each stage of a hierarchical production process, rather than by AI being unable to perform upstream work well. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • A formal nested-CES hierarchical model makes the elasticity of substitution between AI-generated upstream output and downstream human review/integration effort the parameter governing attenuation.
  • Fitting the model to the observed attenuation yields an estimated elasticity of 0.25 — close to a fixed-proportions (Leontief) relationship.
Implicit premises:
  • Each production layer requires the output of the layer below as an input, and human review/integration capacity does not itself scale with AI output.
  • The model's stylized structure (corner-solution augmentation vs. automation, Leontief review requirement) is an adequate simplification of actual software production.
Inferences:
  • If the elasticity of substitution between AI output and human downstream effort were to rise — e.g. future models produce code requiring less review, or review/integration is itself automated — the same upstream gains would propagate further into shipped output; the current attenuation is therefore a function of where AI currently sits in the complementarity structure, not a fixed ceiling.

Narratives

  • Robert Solow's 1987 observation that 'you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics,' invoked to frame the paper's app-marketplace findings.
    → Positions the flat-usage, rising-small-audience-share finding as a recurrence of a decades-old, unresolved debate about whether micro-level technology gains ever show up in macro/aggregate output — lending the paper's result historical weight and signaling the authors see this as a structural pattern, not an AI-specific quirk.
  • The short history of AI coding tools (Section 3.2): GitHub Copilot's 2022 autocomplete, the early-2025 emergence of sync agents (Claude Code, Cursor, GitHub Copilot Agent Mode), and the May 2025 launch of fully autonomous async agents (GitHub's async agent, Codex, Claude Code in async mode).
    → More than background — this timeline is the implicit justification for the paper's event-study design: because the three tool categories arrived in genuinely distinct calendar waves, matching each adoption against a one-year-earlier control cohort and comparing effects 'across generations' is a meaningful empirical exercise rather than an arbitrary categorization.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How do productivity effects of AI coding tools evolve across successive tool generations?
    Effects grow monotonically across generations: roughly 40% (autocomplete) → 140% (plus sync agents) → 180% (plus async agents) cumulative effect on commits.
  • ✅ Do task-level AI productivity gains translate into proportional increases in final output?
    No — they attenuate sharply moving up the production hierarchy, consistent with the weak-link/O-ring hypothesis, with an estimated elasticity of substitution of about 0.25 between AI output and human downstream effort.
  • ⭕ Does flat marketplace usage despite rising app-release counts reflect lower-quality marginal apps or a separate consumer-side discovery/adoption bottleneck?
    The authors explicitly state their data cannot distinguish between the two explanations.
  • ⭕ Is the attenuation pattern the paper documents (AI's gains absorbed as work-in-progress at the review/integration layer) sustainable for the humans actually sitting at that bottleneck — their workload, review backlog, or wellbeing?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe paper models the review/integration layer's human capacity as fixed and treats attenuation purely as an output/throughput phenomenon, with no direct measure of review time, backlog growth, or reviewer burnout — even though its own mechanism (surplus upstream output passing through unchanged human capacity) is exactly the setup other sources in this corpus (Faros, DORA) use to describe rising reviewer burden and burnout risk from the same AI-driven volume increase.
    Read next: Cross-reference against telemetry-based studies (Faros, DORA) that directly measure review time, PR backlog, and incident/defect rates under rising AI-authored code volume.
  • incompleteThe paper explicitly cannot distinguish whether flat marketplace usage reflects lower-quality marginal apps or a consumer-side discovery bottleneck, but it doesn't engage with a third possibility its own data seems to hint at: that lowering the fixed cost of producing a 'minimally publishable' app may simply attract projects (personal tools, internal experiments, portfolio pieces) that were never intended to find a broad audience, and that counting these as market 'supply' in the first place may be the wrong frame.
    Read next: Look for research that segments new app releases by evident intent (commercial vs. personal/experimental) rather than treating all new listings as attempts to reach a broad audience.
  • uninformedThe paper explicitly excludes enterprise and internal-only software — likely where most professional, team-based software engineering work actually happens — from its analysis. Its production-hierarchy model and 0.25 elasticity estimate, derived from public-repository and consumer-marketplace data, may not transfer to internal systems with different review norms, continuous-deployment cadences without a discrete 'release' artifact, and different definitions of 'shipped.'
    Read next: Look for internal, enterprise-scale telemetry studies (e.g. DORA's survey-based team archetypes) to see whether the same attenuation/elasticity pattern holds inside organizations rather than on public GitHub and consumer app stores.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • Is AI coding on average 17.3x faster or 1.3x faster? Both. — LinkedIn post by Fabrice Bernhard · g5 — Gives a direct empirical/causal answer to this exact Lean question for software specifically: AI's upstream code-writing gains are real and large (up to 741% more lines), but they are largely absorbed as additional work passing through the fixed-capacity review/integration bottleneck rather than converted into proportional shipped output (only 20% more releases) — closer to classical Lean's 'inflates WIP upstream' prediction than to 'frees capacity to attack the bottleneck,' with an estimated elasticity of substitution of just 0.25 indicating strong complementarity and weak pass-through.
