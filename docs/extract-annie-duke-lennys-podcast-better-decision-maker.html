Decision quality improves less by learning about biases than by changing the procedure around the decision: make the implicit explicit (written, rubric-based, forecast-based judgments), gather each person's opinion independently before the meeting, reserve the meeting for discussing disagreement, decide outside the room, shorten feedback loops by tracking intermediate signals, and pre-commit to kill criteria — because intuition and group dynamics are unreliable in ways you cannot detect unless the judgment is externalised and checkable.

practical · mixed — A host-led interview whose argumentative spine is practical (change how groups collect opinions, evaluate decisions and stop projects) and whose evidence is mostly anecdote and consulting experience: a poker-player-turned-decision-coach recounts Kahneman's hiring work, her First Round Capital engagement, a sales-team premortem and the Glitch-to-Slack story. The conversational framing (parenting, Kahneman tribute) is loose; the transferable content is a small set of procedures. Speaker attribution: Annie Duke is the guest; Lenny Rachitsky is the host (the transcript has no speaker labels, so attribution is inferred from content).

Key terms

  • Discover, discuss, decide Independent-First Group Elicitation
    Duke's decomposition of what people think a meeting is for. Discovering what each person thinks and deciding what to do should both happen outside the meeting; only discussion belongs in it.
  • Nominal group Independent-First Group Elicitation
    A group that, at the moment of eliciting opinions, works independently and asynchronously: members contribute in parallel without seeing each other's input, then the compiled responses are shared.
  • Forced rank (with rationale) Independent-First Group Elicitation
    Asking each person to order a list of ideas, features or priorities from best to worst and to attach a 3-5 sentence written rationale explaining why they are in that order.
  • Don't reply all
    The instruction that responses go only to the organiser, so that nobody sees anyone else's opinion before submitting their own.
  • Alignment AI Alignment & Safety
    The goal that everyone in a group agrees after a meeting. Duke calls the word 'stupid' because it does not exist: ten people with different opinions will not leave the room agreeing, and making agreement the goal makes the meeting coercive.
  • Curious, not coercive
    Contributors speak to convey why they believe something, not to convince others; interrupting, saying 'you're wrong' and demanding agreement are named as coercive moves that make people tribal.
  • Endowed (to the decision)
    People who were heard and who see the true spread of opinion feel ownership of a decision even when it is not what they wanted.
  • Nevertheless
    A leadership phrase that pairs 'I hear you' with a decision that does not change: 'I've heard both of you... Nevertheless... this is what the decision is going to be.'
  • Resulting Resulting (Outcome vs. Decision Quality)
    Judging the quality of a decision by its outcome. Examples: crediting yourself for a well-behaved child, or a successful early investor assuming skill.
  • Make the implicit explicit
    Externalise the model, weights, definitions and forecasts you are already using implicitly so they can be examined, discussed and checked against outcomes.
  • Decision rubric / mediating judgments
    A rubric whose components are intermediate judgments (for example competitive landscape within 'market') made before the overall judgment, with shared definitions and a 1-7 scale. Mediating judgments are 'basically an implied definition' of the overall category.
  • Feedback loop (as a choice) Feedback Loops
    The length of a feedback loop is chosen, not given: by tracking necessary-but-not-sufficient or correlated intermediate signals you can close the loop on a 10-year decision in months.
  • Necessary but not sufficient / correlated with the outcome
    The criterion for choosing intermediate signals to forecast and track, such as 'funds at Series A' for a venture exit.
  • Pre-mortem Premortem
    Imagining a future failure and generating the early signals that would have predicted it. By itself it rarely changes plans; its best use is to supply kill criteria.
  • Kill criteria Strategic Quitting & Kill Criteria
    A pre-committed set of signals that, if observed, triggers a stated action (stop, pivot, or a specific test question), decided before launch.
  • Pre-commitment
    Deciding in advance what you will do on seeing a signal, so you do not rely on 'acting rationally' in the moment (as with climbers who ascend Everest into a blizzard).
  • Sunk cost Strategic Quitting & Kill Criteria
    Letting past investment drive the decision whether to continue. 'Waste is a prospective problem, not a retrospective one': if you would not start it today, what you put in from now on is the waste.
  • Mental time travel
    Asking how you will think about the situation in a week or in ten years, to escape the focusing effect of the present moment ('Nothing is as important as it seems when you're thinking about it' — Kahneman).
  • Adversarial collaboration
    Seeking out someone who disagrees with you and co-designing the study that would settle the dispute (Kahneman's practice).
  • One decision-maker model / vote forum
    The decision is taken by a single accountable person after reading the independent inputs, or by a private vote in which members cannot see each other's votes.

Arguments

Opinions, forecasts and ideas should be collected independently and asynchronously (written, force-ranked, with rationale) before the meeting, and the meeting should be used only for discussing disagreement. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • In-room elicitation produces cross-influence; the loudest, most confident or most senior person has outsized influence.
  • The most confident person is not always right.
  • About 80% of typical meeting time is spent agreeing in different words.
  • Independent input reveals the true spread of opinion (the junior's 'four weeks' that would never be spoken after a senior's 'three months').
  • Seeing the real spread makes people accept decisions that are not exactly what they wanted.
Implicit premises:
  • Individuals conform to what they hear first, especially from seniors, and not always consciously.
  • People will submit honest independent judgments even when the organiser or a senior reads them.
  • A written rationale is a good enough proxy for the reasoning that the discussion would have drawn out.
  • The extra cost of writing and reading beforehand is small relative to the decision's stakes.
Inferences:
  • The same logic applies to AI ensembles: judges or advisors that see each other's outputs (or share training) lose independence.
  • Whether the independent-first procedure helps depends on how correlated the members' errors already are; with identical sources, independence adds less.
  • Reversible low-stakes decisions may not justify the overhead (Duke does not discuss this).
Do not aim for alignment or decide in the room; have one decision-maker (or a private vote) decide after hearing everyone, and accept that people will not all agree. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • People with different opinions will not leave the room agreeing.
  • If agreement is the goal, the meeting becomes coercive; coercion makes people tribal and defensive.
  • Being heard (via reflecting back) gives a sense of ownership independent of getting your way.
  • Seeing the real spread of disagreement makes the lack of unanimity understandable.
Implicit premises:
  • There is a legitimate decision-maker who will actually weigh the input rather than just collect it.
  • Perceived procedural fairness matters more to acceptance than outcome.
  • Participants trust the facilitator's reflections are honest and not performative.
Inferences:
  • The model gives an explicit locus of accountability, which makes it feasible to track the decision-maker's calibration over time.
  • A leader who 'listens' but never changes anything will exhaust the goodwill the procedure builds.
There is no such thing as a long feedback loop: decisions with decade-long payoffs can be evaluated within months by forecasting and tracking intermediate signals. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • A decision is a prediction about how the world will unfold, and that unfolding passes through observable steps.
  • Some of those steps are necessary (never has a billion-dollar exit failed to fund at Series A) or correlated with the outcome.
  • A forecast of those steps (for example probability of funding at Series A) can be recorded at decision time and scored in 16 months or less.
  • Poker, supposedly the fast-feedback domain, is in fact so noisy that evaluating skill takes very many hands.
Implicit premises:
  • The chosen intermediate milestones are valid proxies for the final outcome and not driven by herd behaviour or market sentiment.
  • There are enough decisions to score for the accuracy statistics to be meaningful.
  • Decision-makers will accept being scored.
Inferences:
  • Any slow-payoff decision (hiring, product bets, architecture, strategy) can be given a leading-indicator forecast at decision time.
  • Optimising for the proxy risks Goodhart effects, which she does not discuss.
Making what is implicit explicit (definitions, rubrics, ratings, forecasts) is the single most important way to improve decision quality, because it lets you find out when your intuition is wrong. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Intuition is sometimes right and sometimes wrong, and confidence feels the same either way.
  • Kahneman's structured-hiring work raised hit rate from about 50% to 65% when intuition followed the rubric.
  • At First Round, explicit ratings and forecasts allowed accuracy feedback and showed some table-pounded factors were not predictive at all.
  • Implicit judgments cannot be examined, discussed, tested or improved.
Implicit premises:
  • A rubric can capture enough of the expert's actual model for the comparison to be fair.
  • Experts are willing to commit to explicit ratings and be scored.
  • The sample of decisions is large enough, and outcomes observable enough, to learn from.
Inferences:
  • Expertise does not exempt anyone from the need for explicit judgments: the surprising finding is that real experts pound the table equally about predictive and non-predictive factors.
  • The first rubric is a snapshot of intuitions; its value comes from the revision loop.
A pre-mortem is best used to set pre-committed kill criteria rather than to refine a plan. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Pre-mortems rarely change plans or behaviour on their own (Duke, Schweitzer and Gandhi).
  • Once things launch, people are very slow to quit because of sunk cost and other biases.
  • Pairing each early warning signal with an action decided in advance replaces in-the-moment judgment (sales team: no demo plus price-only means kill; RFP written for a competitor means ask directly, then pursue or kill; no decision-maker in the room means offer executive alignment, then pursue or kill).
Implicit premises:
  • The signals people imagine in a hypothetical failure are real predictors of failure.
  • People and organisations will honour pre-commitments when the signal arrives.
  • Leadership will not punish someone for following the pre-agreed stop rule.
Inferences:
  • Kill criteria also protect the person who stops, since the criteria were agreed with leadership beforehand (the salespeople 'get in trouble for it' otherwise).
  • False-positive kill signals (stopping a deal that would have closed) are not tracked; a loop of review is needed.
If you are thinking about quitting, you are probably already past the point at which you should have quit. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The decision to stop is made under the same uncertainty as the decision to start, and people refuse to walk away until they know for sure.
  • Sunk cost, endowment over what you built, identity and others' judgments all bias against stopping.
  • People only quit when it stops being a decision (Thaler).
  • Walking away early invites questions; being forced out does not.
Implicit premises:
  • Late quitting is more common and more costly than quitting too early (she asserts the data 'is pretty strong' without citing it here).
  • The costs of premature quitting (the grit counter-argument) are smaller on average than the costs of persisting.
Inferences:
  • Defaults should be moved toward stopping, and criteria for stopping should be set in advance.
  • Teams should ask the 'would we start this today?' question periodically instead of only when in trouble.
Knowing about biases is not enough; the benefit comes only from using structured practices, and few do. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Kahneman himself said he could not use his knowledge of biases in daily life.
  • The methods that work (rubrics, structured interviews, independent elicitation) are not 'supernatural' to human decision-making.
  • People think much more highly of their intuition than they should, and other people usually have more insight into our situation than we do.
Implicit premises:
  • The gap between knowing and doing is a failure of adoption and not of method.
  • Organisations can overcome that adoption gap with external structure.
Inferences:
  • Bias lists and decision courses are low-yield compared to process installation, which suggests where consulting effort should go.

Narratives

  • Kahneman's adversarial collaboration: after Kahneman and Deaton found money stops buying happiness beyond about $75,000 (2010), Matthew Killingsworth found happiness keeps rising with income. Instead of a feud, Kahneman, Killingsworth and Barbara Mellers joined to design a study to resolve it. Duke adds that Kahneman volunteered 'I wish priming wasn't in the book. It doesn't replicate.'
    → Shows the epistemic stance (seek out who disagrees, ask why am I wrong) that the procedural advice tries to institutionalise.
  • Parenting and resulting: Duke's oldest child was uninterested in alcohol or drugs and she felt smug about her parenting; her next child went through high school differently, which she recognised as temperament. With four children she dropped each baby on its head once when it unexpectedly rolled over, and learned that mistakes are inevitable and parenting has little influence on temperament.
    → Personal illustration of resulting (reading skill into outcomes) and of not blaming yourself for events you could not foresee.
  • Mental time travel with her kids: when they were upset about being grounded, she told them it would be a great story to tell their children at Thanksgiving when they were forty; and, for choosing between video games and studying, asked how they would feel about the choice when the test came back.
    → Supports the claim that escaping the present's focusing effect is a general decision tool.
  • 'Nevertheless': Duke grounds her children after finding red solo cups in the backyard; they argue it is unfair. She listens, says 'I hear you' and then 'nevertheless, you're grounded for two weeks'. The same move applies when employees disagree with a leadership decision.
    → Models the separation of being heard from getting your way, later generalised to the group-decision procedure.
  • Kahneman's hiring work: moving from 'I know a great product manager when I see one' to an articulated rubric and structured interview, and only then using intuition, raised the hit rate from roughly 50% to 65%. Duke notes that her conversations with Kahneman were mostly him asking how to get anybody to do it.
    → Evidence for the value of making implicit models explicit, and for the claim that adoption is the real problem.
  • A roadmap or timeline meeting where everyone shouts estimates: the senior person says 'three months' and a junior who thought 'four weeks' conforms, so the room never hears the lower estimate. With independent submissions the four-week view appears and can be examined.
    → Illustrates cross-influence and the narrowing of visible disagreement in group elicitation.
  • A remote-work policy discussion Duke facilitated: someone argues the policy should not be uniform across functions because IT must be in office while engineering barely matters; Duke reflects it back ('what I heard you say is...') without agreeing, the speaker confirms or corrects, and the facilitator moves around the room the same way. Participants end up feeling heard and endowed to the decision.
    → Demonstrates the facilitation technique and why it produces ownership without agreement.
  • Venture firms in 2018 told Duke 'we just know a good founder when we see one' and 'our feedback loops are a decade'. She answered that poker is much noisier than they think and that investing does not mean going to sleep like Rip Van Winkle for ten years: Josh Kopelman at First Round engaged when she pointed out that no billion-dollar exit failed to fund at Series A, so funding at A is a necessary and highly correlated signal that arrives in months.
    → Origin story for the 'no such thing as a long feedback loop' claim and her engagement with First Round and Renegade Partners.
  • Uber's early investor: someone who was early in Uber and is now a celebrity investor has no incentive to learn whether the investment reflected insight into a pain point or simply a friend's startup; the unexamined long feedback loop protects their reputation.
    → Shows the psychological safety of a long feedback loop and why outcome does not reveal decision quality.
  • First Round Capital's rubric: before Duke arrived, the partnership recorded who voted yes or no. She introduced 1-7 ratings on market, team, founder and product with shared definitions and mediating judgments, plus forecasts such as probability of Series A. After five years, they can test partner accuracy against hundreds of outcomes, tell partners where their judgments are predictive, and revise the rubric from data. Some table-pounded factors proved predictive; others were not predictive even for the person pounding the table.
    → Worked case of making the implicit explicit and closing the loop; shows that intuition is sometimes right and sometimes wrong.
  • A sales team's pre-mortem: asked to imagine an RFP/RFI lead worked for six months that died, team members listed early warning signs (RFP written with a competitor in mind, customer won't demo and only discusses price, no decision-maker in the room after a few meetings) and attached actions: kill; ask directly about competitors and then decide; offer executive alignment and kill if declined.
    → Concrete instance of turning a pre-mortem into kill criteria with pre-commitment, and of protecting salespeople from blame for dropping deals.
  • Climbers on Everest who keep going into a blizzard even though they should not; Thaler's remark that people quit only when they have already fallen into the crevasse.
    → Image for why in-the-moment rationality cannot be relied on and why decisions to stop must be pre-committed.
  • Glitch and Slack: Stewart Butterfield's game Glitch had critical acclaim, $6 million in the bank, top investors (Andreessen Horowitz, Accel) and about 5,000 diehard users playing over 20 hours a week, but 95-99 people tried it for five minutes for each diehard. Paid marketing for six weeks in late 2012 grew users 6-7% week over week, yet on a Monday morning Butterfield wrote to his investors and cofounders that he had woken 'with the dead certainty that Glitch was over': back-of-envelope math (31 weeks to break even even with flat acquisition cost, which would rise) showed it was not a venture-scale business and his equity-paid employees were not being treated fairly. He shut it down, two days later proposed the internal communication tool the team loved as the next product, investors rolled their money over, and it became Slack.
    → Dramatises that stopping is a forecast about the future ('would I start this today?'), that quitting can be correct when nobody else sees it, and that quitting frees attention for better opportunities.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How much better can someone become at making decisions if they study all of this?
    A lot, but only if they actually apply it; Kahneman's structured-hiring result (50% to 65%) shows the size of the gain, and the obstacle is that almost nobody does.
  • ✅ Which interventions have the greatest practical effect in organisations?
    Independent elicitation (discover and decide outside the meeting; discuss only) and making assumptions, rubrics and forecasts explicit.
  • ✅ How do you get people to feel included and heard under a single decision-maker model?
    Shift from a coercive to a curious mode: independent input first, reflection back by the facilitator, and 'nevertheless' from leadership.
  • ✅ How can decision quality be assessed in the short term when the outcome is long-term (investing, business bets)?
    Choose intermediate necessary-or-correlated signals, forecast them explicitly at decision time and score them as they resolve; there is no long feedback loop unless you choose it.
  • ✅ What are the best uses of a pre-mortem?
    Use it to set kill criteria with pre-committed actions rather than to fine-tune the plan.
  • ⭕ Is it true that if you are thinking about quitting you should already have quit?
    Duke affirms it (the data are 'pretty strong') and explains the biases, but the full treatment is deferred to a follow-up episode about her book Quit.
  • ⭕ Does the 'discover independently' procedure cost too much when decisions are small or reversible, and does the written format disadvantage some people?
    Not raised.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe evidence for the independent-elicitation procedure is Duke's consulting experience and anecdotes (a few companies adopt it and find it 'ginormous'); no data on decision outcomes, no control, no comparison with structured-discussion alternatives, and no engagement with the formal literature on nominal groups, Delphi methods or Kahneman's own decision-hygiene work, so the effect size is unknown.
    Read next: Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, Noise (decision hygiene, the mediating assessments protocol); the Delphi method and nominal-group literature; Sunstein and Hastie, Wiser; Janis on groupthink.
  • incompleteNo treatment of when the procedure is worth its overhead or where it breaks: reversible low-stakes decisions, small teams that already trust each other, written rationales visible to a boss who may shape what people write, fast-moving situations, and asymmetries in who writes comfortably. The single decision-maker model also leaves open how the decider is held accountable for ignoring the input.
    Read next: Bezos on one-way versus two-way door decisions; research on speaking up and psychological safety under power asymmetry; evaluations of written-memo cultures.
  • incompleteThe 'there is no long feedback loop' argument assumes intermediate milestones (Series A funding) are valid proxies for decision quality. But funding at A also reflects other investors' herd behaviour, market cycles and signalling, so forecasting it may measure sentiment-reading rather than company quality; the 'no billion-dollar exit without a Series A' claim is a survivorship-prone, necessary-condition argument and not evidence of predictive power. Goodhart-style distortion once partners are scored is not discussed.
    Read next: Goodhart's law and construct-validity treatments of proxy metrics; Tetlock on forecasting accuracy and scoring rules; Kahneman on validity of subjective expert judgment.
  • incompleteThe headline quitting claim, that by the time you think about quitting you are late, is asserted from strong 'data' that are not shown, and the opposite error (quitting too early, persistence that pays off) is not weighed; no guidance is given for setting kill criteria so they are neither arbitrary nor so tight that promising projects are killed. The topic is explicitly deferred to a future episode.
    Read next: Duke, Quit (and a follow-up episode); Duckworth on grit; Christian and Griffiths on optimal stopping; escalation-of-commitment research.
  • incompleteIndependence is argued for human groups, but nothing connects it to AI-assisted decision-making: panels of LLM advisors or judges that share training data, or agents that see each other's outputs, lose the independence her procedure depends on. Her claim that people exaggerate their own insight is also relevant to AI-generated rationales.
    Read next: Corpus material on LLM-as-judge ensembles and same-model blind spots; work on correlated errors in model ensembles; the corpus extractions on AI advisory boards.
