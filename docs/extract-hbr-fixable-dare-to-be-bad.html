Dare to Be Bad at Something (From Fixable)

Fixable (TED Audio Collective / Pushkin Industries), hosts Anne Morriss and Frances Frei; syndicated via the HBR Podcast Network / Cold Call feed · podcast (HBR Podcast Network cross-post of a TED-produced show, Fixable) · 2026-09-23 Reader 🖥️

Burnout & RecoveryDecision-Making PracticesHierarchy & Org ChartsStrategic Underperformance (Dare to Be Bad)Strategy & Direction-Setting
To excel where it matters most, individuals and organizations must deliberately choose — guided by a strict, customer-derived ranking of what matters — not only what to stop doing, but what to keep doing while doing it badly, and leaders must have (and visibly model) the emotional courage to sustain that choice under pressure.

practical · mixed — A conversational, practical argument for action (make deliberate trade-offs; have the courage to underperform on purpose) built mostly through anecdote and worked example (MacBook Air, Southwest, working moms, the hosts' own family) rather than formal argument, with an explicit step-by-step method threaded through the dialogue (rank customer value, defend the top, sacrifice the bottom).

Key terms

  • impossible triangle Strategic Underperformance (Dare to Be Bad)
    A borrowed construction-industry heuristic (cost, quality, speed — pick two) used as shorthand for the claim that no offering can be best-in-class on every dimension simultaneously.
  • defy gravity Strategic Underperformance (Dare to Be Bad)
    The hosts' shorthand for an organization (usually in services) acting as though trade-offs don't apply to it — believing it can be great at everything because there's no literal physics forcing the acknowledgment the way weight/materials do for hardware.
  • moment of truth Strategic Underperformance (Dare to Be Bad)
    A specific, emotionally charged customer interaction (the Southwest grandmother's letter) that tests whether an organization actually holds its trade-off discipline under real pressure, not just in the abstract.
  • strategic no / contextual no
    A refusal that is transparently justified by the organization's chosen, ranked trade-offs — as opposed to a 'mean no' (arbitrary, uncaring) or a 'guaranteed yes' extracted by escalating past people who understand why the no exists.
  • attribute maps Strategic Underperformance (Dare to Be Bad)
    The artifact produced by ranking, top to bottom, what a customer (or family member) values — used to decide what to invest in versus sacrifice.
  • nobility of effort vs. nobility of excellence
    Two mutually exclusive personal strategies under finite capacity: exhausting yourself trying to stay adequate at everything ('nobility of effort', ending in what Frei calls 'exhausted mediocrity') versus deliberately excelling at a few chosen things while being openly bad/absent at the rest ('nobility of excellence').
  • simulate the customer
    A group exercise where team members predict, before talking to real customers, what those customers value most — used both to build fluency for the real conversation and, more importantly, to expose that different people/factions in the org hold different, overconfident, and often contradictory assumptions about what customers want.

Arguments

An organization (or person) should deliberately choose to underperform on specific, low-ranked dimensions of its offering rather than trying to be adequate everywhere. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • You cannot be best-in-class on every dimension simultaneously (MacBook Air: lightest-weight ambition forced no internal CD-ROM drive).
  • 'Cost, quality, speed — pick two' generalizes this as a domain-general heuristic (the impossible triangle).
Implicit premises:
  • Resources and attention are finite and fungible across the dimensions of an offering — investment on one axis is definitionally extraction from another.
  • 'Greatness' is inherently comparative and scarce, not independently achievable on every axis at once.
Inferences:
  • Any strategy or roadmap claiming to improve on every dimension at once, without naming an explicit sacrifice, is either hiding a trade-off or hasn't done the work.
Leaders must personally and visibly absorb the social/emotional cost of an organization's trade-off (the Kelleher CC-the-company move), not merely approve it privately. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • If leaders waver even slightly, it 'gets amplified through the rest of the organization.'
  • The front line calibrates how seriously to take a stated trade-off by watching leadership's actual behavior under pressure, not by reading policy.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizational discipline is transmitted socially, downward, from visible leader behavior — not sustained by written policy alone.
  • Any quiet exception granted at the top erodes discipline throughout the organization, regardless of how individually justified that exception seems.
Inferences:
  • A trade-off enforced only privately by middle managers, without visible top-level reinforcement, will erode over time even if the written policy never changes.
Organizations should determine what to be great at by starting from a strict, customer-derived ranking of value attributes, not from internal capability, preference, or habit. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The customer's ordering of what they value determines what's worth defending versus sacrificing.
  • Internal self-assessment is unreliable, especially in head-to-head competitive industries (Coke vs. Pepsi over/under-grading bias).
Implicit premises:
  • Organizations have systematically biased self-perception that internal deliberation alone cannot correct.
  • Ranking (a strict order), not just a flat list of valued things, is necessary because resource allocation is itself an ordering problem, not a set-membership problem.
Inferences:
  • Any prioritization exercise that stops at 'what customers value' without forcing a strict order has skipped the hard, decision-relevant part of the exercise.

Narratives

  • Steve Jobs unveiling the MacBook Air from a manila envelope — to be the lightest laptop on the market, it shipped without an internal CD-ROM drive and with a merely 'good enough' battery.
    → Makes concrete, vividly, that even a celebrated, beloved product required an explicit, visible sacrifice to win on the one dimension (weight) that mattered most — grounds proposition 1 and argument 1.
  • Herb Kelleher's letter to a frustrated grandmother who wanted Southwest to transfer her bags between airlines — Kelleher explains the trade-off (fast plane turnaround depends on not transferring bags) and CCs the entire company on his reply.
    → Shows a real, high-stakes 'moment of truth' where a founder personally and transparently defends a strategic trade-off, educates the whole organization by making the reasoning visible, and models the 'contextual no' rather than delegating or caving — grounds propositions 6 and 7 and argument 2.
  • The preschool drop-off line as a visible spectrum of working moms 'killing it' (identifiable by not having time to get their roots done) versus those 'one missed handoff away from a catastrophe' who are still trying to be great at every role (work, daughter, sister, friend, PTA).
    → Illustrates proposition 13 — 'nobility of effort' (exhausted, undifferentiated over-commitment) versus 'nobility of excellence' (deliberate, visible underinvestment in explicitly deprioritized roles).
  • The hosts 'test drove' their own attribute-mapping method on their sons while writing Uncommon Service, discovering their kids didn't value Morriss's school volunteering or elaborate meals, only full presence when actually together — and Morriss separately admits she over-invested in PTA/school-board work (including midnight board meetings) at the cost of being present with her kids at home.
    → Makes the framework self-applicable and self-critical, personalizing proposition 14 — even the framework's own authors under-execute it without doing the explicit ranking work.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How should an organization or individual decide what to deliberately be bad or mediocre at, rather than trying to be good at everything?
    Rank what your customers (or family) value, from most to least important; defend and excel at the top of that ranking; consciously sacrifice or underinvest at the bottom, guided by the ranking rather than by internal habit or preference.
  • ✅ Why do people, especially in service businesses, resist acknowledging trade-offs that seem obvious in physical-product contexts?
    There is no physical law forcing acknowledgment in services the way weight/materials force it in hardware, so people self-deceive ('defy gravity') into believing excellence everywhere is possible.
  • ✅ How does an organization keep a strategic trade-off from eroding under pressure, e.g. from an angry but sympathetic customer or an internal escalation?
    Leaders must personally and visibly absorb and model the emotional cost of the trade-off's 'no,' and hold the same contextual-no posture as the front line, so that escalating past the front line doesn't become a shortcut to a 'yes.'
  • ⭕ Does this framework work when there is no single, clear customer or competitive set to derive the ranking from — e.g. internal platform teams, regulated public services, or organizations serving multiple stakeholder types with conflicting priorities?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe episode never explains how to resolve conflicts between different customer segments' orderings of value (e.g. budget vs. business travelers on an airline), nor how 'the customer' is even defined when an organization serves multiple distinct populations with different priority orders.
    Read next: Multi-sided-market and stakeholder-prioritization literature; product-market segmentation frameworks for reconciling conflicting customer rankings.
  • incompleteNo account of how long a strategic trade-off should hold once made — when does a settled trade-off (no CD-ROM, no baggage transfer) become stale as customer expectations, competitors, or technology shift? Trade-offs are presented as durable and settled, never as something to periodically re-rank.
    Read next: Strategy-renewal / dynamic-capabilities literature on when and how to revisit a prior strategic trade-off.
  • illogicalThe 'defy gravity' claim — that trade-off physics apply identically in services as in physical products — is asserted by analogy ('the same physics applies') rather than demonstrated; the hosts never specify the underlying constrained resource (attention? budget? headcount? culture bandwidth?) the way weight/materials constrain a laptop.
    Read next: Operations-management literature on service-quality trade-off mechanisms (queuing theory, capacity-utilization trade-offs) that would ground the metaphor mechanistically rather than rhetorically.
  • incompleteThe episode gives no method for building the 'courage' itself. Morriss repeatedly names the obstacle as emotional/psychological ('having the stomach for it'), but the conversation offers no concrete practice for developing that tolerance beyond leader role-modeling — nothing for an individual or team that has the process right but still can't sit with visible underperformance.
    Read next: Literature on behavioral/cognitive practices for tolerating sustained social discomfort and visible disapproval; could connect to burnout and psychological-safety material already in this corpus.
