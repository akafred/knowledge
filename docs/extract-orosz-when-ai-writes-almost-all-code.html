When AI Writes Almost All Code, What Happens To Software Engineering?

Gergely Orosz · pdf · 2026-01-06 Reader 🖥️

As AI crosses a real capability threshold and starts writing the large majority of code for many developers and teams, the part of software engineering that was always about judgment, verification, architecture, and accountability — not typing syntax — becomes more valuable, not less, while the raw ability to write code in a given language stops being a differentiator at all.

practical · mixed — Opens narratively (the author's own holiday 'a-ha moment' and a chorus of named practitioners' quotes) to establish a factual premise (AI now writes ~90%+ of code for many devs/teams), then shifts to argumentative/expository mode to work out the professional consequences for individuals, teams, and the industry. The propositions throughout serve a practical aim: what should engineers, teams, and hiring pipelines do about this.

Key terms

  • a-ha moment
    The specific, recognizable experience of an experienced engineer suddenly finding a coding agent capable of producing code as good as, or better than, what they would have written by hand — described as a discrete threshold crossing, not a gradual improvement.
  • coder vs. software engineer
    'Coder' = someone whose value was writing code by hand; 'software engineer' = someone who also breaks down problems, architects for testability/observability, verifies output, and builds for scale/reliability/security. The article's central claim is that AI absorbs the 'coder' part and raises the price of the 'engineer' part.
  • product engineer Product Engineer (PM/Engineering Role Merge)
    A blended role (cited examples: WorkOS at ~1 PM per 80 engineers; early Linear with no PMs at all) where software engineers absorb enough product-management work (talking to customers, specifying, prioritizing) that a separate PM function becomes less necessary, and PMs in turn become hands-on enough to ship prototypes themselves.
  • the middle (thinning middle software work) AI Labor Disruption
    Karri Saarinen's term (quoted) for the translation layer between intent and implementation — writing the actual code — which pure coding-agent workflows are making thinner, shifting the valuable work to forming and shaping the intent itself ('directing and managing agent work becomes the craft').

Arguments

Software engineering as a discipline (judgment, verification, architecture, accountability) becomes more valuable as AI absorbs more code-writing, even as 'coding' itself becomes commoditized. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI increasingly absorbs code-writing tasks: prototyping, well-defined ticket implementation, refactoring, cross-language/cross-stack implementation.
  • More AI-generated code volume means more tech debt, more bugs, more resource usage, and a 30% rise in observed change-failure rate (Cortex 2026 report) — i.e., more raw material for things to go wrong.
  • Good software engineering practice (testing, architecture, verification, observability, tech-debt management) is precisely what catches and manages that increased raw material for failure.
  • Teams lacking those practices will be hurt sooner and more visibly as shipping pace increases.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizations will actually respond to rising failure rates and tech debt by investing more in engineering discipline, rather than by tolerating more outages as an accepted cost of speed, or by simply hiring cheaper, less-disciplined engineers to churn out more AI-assisted code.
  • The market will correctly price 'solid software engineer, not just coder' as a scarcer and more valuable skill, rather than undervaluing it because the volume of superficially-working code makes the difference hard for non-technical decision-makers to perceive.
Inferences:
  • If hiring and compensation don't actually reward verification/architecture skill more than raw output volume, the 'good news' (fundamentals become more valuable) could fail to materialize even though the underlying technical need for it is real — the argument's optimism depends on an unstated market-correction mechanism the article never defends.
Product management and software engineering roles will converge into a blended 'product engineer' role rather than AI letting PMs simply replace engineers.
Explicit premises:
  • At WorkOS, roughly one PM services 80 engineers; at early Linear, there were no PMs at all — both examples predate widespread 90%+ AI code generation but already show the ratio skewing toward engineer-as-product-owner.
  • As AI absorbs 'the middle' (translating intent into implementation), what's left — forming and shaping the intent itself — is product work, which engineers are increasingly well-positioned to do directly for small, well-understood changes (e.g., fixing a reported bug without PM triage).
Implicit premises:
  • The two cited companies (WorkOS, Linear) are representative of where the broader industry is heading, rather than being unusually small, engineering-led, or early-stage organizations whose PM-light structure reflects their size/stage rather than AI's effect on the PM/engineering boundary.
  • Nonfunctional, cross-cutting product decisions (pricing, sequencing across teams, stakeholder alignment at scale) — the parts of product management that are not simply 'write a clear ticket' — will also compress under this convergence, rather than remaining a distinct, scarce skill even in an AI-saturated org.

Narratives

  • The author's own holiday-break experience: using Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.2 to ship a few hundred lines of production code for long-deferred features (subscription self-service, admin panel), including setting up Claude Code for Web so he could review and merge PRs entirely from his phone while traveling.
    → Grounds the abstract 'AI now writes 90%+ of code' claim in a first-person, dateable, falsifiable anecdote, and implicitly supports the article's framing that this was specifically a step change in capability (the 'magical feeling') rather than a gradual improvement he'd already adjusted to.
  • Michael Novati's (formerly Meta's most productive developer, the inspiration for Meta's 'Coding Machine' archetype) GitHub activity roughly doubling in pull-request volume from 2024 (low AI use) to 2025 (heavy AI use), while sustaining 400-600 commits/month and 1,500+ new features plus 800+ bugfixes in a year on a startup platform.
    → Serves as concrete evidence for the 'more code & more resource usage = more problems' proposition in Section 5 — it shows the raw volume increase is real and large, which is the premise the rest of Section 5's risk list depends on.
  • Shaquille O'Neal, in a Replit ad, 'vibe-coding' an app for finding pickup lines with no development experience, producing only prototype-quality output.
    → Illustrates, rather than argues, the limits of non-technical AI-assisted coding (prototypes only) — setting up the contrast the article later draws between low-stakes prototyping (fine for anyone) and production software (still requiring accountable engineers).

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Has AI actually crossed a real capability threshold in code generation, or is this hype?
    Resolved via triangulated testimony from named practitioners across vendors and independents, including a converted skeptic (Karpathy), dated to specific November-December 2025 model releases.
  • ✅ What happens to software engineering as a profession when AI writes almost all the code?
    The 'coder' component commoditizes while the 'engineer' component (judgment, verification, architecture, accountability, product-mindedness) appreciates — argued across Sections 3-6.
  • ⭕ Will this change actually translate into better career outcomes and compensation for engineers who develop these 'engineer, not coder' skills?
  • ⭕ How does a junior engineer acquire the judgment the article says will be more valuable, if the hands-on coding practice that traditionally built that judgment is itself being automated away?
    Partially gestured at (CS education as a filter, new grads may 'mature faster') but not worked through as a pipeline design problem.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article asserts that entry-level engineers will be expected to hit a baseline that used to be senior/staff-level, and separately floats rigorous CS education as a possible answer to 'how does anyone become qualified' — but never designs the actual apprenticeship mechanism: if juniors no longer spend years writing code by hand under senior mentorship, what specifically replaces that as the vehicle for developing judgment about verification, architecture, and knowing when AI output is wrong?
    Read next: Material on deliberately engineered apprenticeship/mentorship models for an AI-saturated pipeline, as distinct from pre-AI, personality-dependent mentorship.
  • incompleteThe accountability argument ('professional software engineers... must be accountable for code deployed to production based on their prompts') asserts who should be accountable but not how that accountability is actually discharged or enforced day to day when the engineer has not read most of the generated code (the article itself quotes Peter Steinberger saying he no longer reads most AI-generated code) — leaving a gap between the accountability claim and the article's own evidence about how little code actually gets read.
    Read next: Material on accountability mechanisms for AI-generated code that a human has not fully read line by line (e.g. architecture-level review, system-behavior tests, debugging competence as a proxy for understanding).
  • illogicalThe article treats the PM/engineering convergence (Section 6) as a smooth, mutually beneficial blending for both roles, but its own earlier evidence (Section 5's work-life-boundary erosion, juniors being pushed to senior-level baselines) suggests convergence could instead mean each role simply absorbing more of the other's workload without a corresponding reduction in total work or headcount pressure — the article doesn't reconcile 'teams become smaller and more efficient' (good news, Section 6) with 'tougher work-life balance' (bad news, Section 5) even though both follow from the same underlying mechanism (fewer people doing more, faster).
    Read next: Organizational-design material on whether role convergence under AI actually reduces total workload or just redistributes and intensifies it.
