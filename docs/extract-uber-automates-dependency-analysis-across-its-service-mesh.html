Uber Automates Dependency Analysis Across Its Service Mesh

Leela Kumili · rss · 2026-10 Reader 🖥️

Cascading Failure & System Resilience PatternsModularity & CouplingSRE & Incident ResponseVendor Self-Reported Metrics
Because sampled distributed tracing is statistically too slow to observe rare failures, Uber instead instruments its RPC framework (YARPC middleware) to emit caller/callee failure metrics and classifies every dependency as fail-close, fail-open or unknown by failure correlation, trading request-level detail for broad, fast coverage of how failures propagate through the service mesh.

practical · expository — InfoQ news report of an Uber engineering blog post. Reports a design decision (infer failure dependencies from RPC-middleware metrics rather than distributed tracing) with a quantitative motivation; the underlying claims are Uber's, relayed secondhand.

Key terms

  • fail-close dependency
    A downstream dependency whose failure consistently causes its caller to fail; operationalized as failure correlation probability >= 0.8.
  • fail-open dependency
    A dependency that can fail without causing its caller to fail (correlation <= 0.2), i.e. the caller degrades gracefully.
  • unknown dependency
    A dependency with insufficient evidence or intermediate correlation (0.2-0.8); an explicit 'don't know' bucket rather than a forced guess.
  • failure correlation probability
    Per caller-dependency estimate of how often a caller failure co-occurs with a callee failure across observed production requests, computed from middleware-emitted metrics.
  • middleware-based dependency inference
    Recording caller/callee endpoint and outcome inside the RPC framework's inbound/outbound middleware, emitting metrics instead of storing per-request traces.

Arguments

Uber should infer dependency failure semantics from RPC middleware metrics rather than from sampled distributed traces. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • More than 99.9% of requests succeed, so errors are rare
  • At 0.01% sampling an erroneous request has a 1-in-10M chance of being sampled; capturing one failure at 500 rps takes ~5.5 hours
  • Middleware metrics cover every production request without storing individual traces
Implicit premises:
  • Tracing at Uber is head-based (uniform) sampling, not error-biased tail sampling
  • Per-request detail is not needed to classify an edge, aggregate outcomes suffice
  • Sampling rate cannot economically be raised for rare-failure capture
Inferences:
  • The same argument applies to any rare-event diagnosis (security anomalies, long-tail latency): uniform sampling is structurally blind to what matters most
  • Metrics-first, trace-on-demand is a general pattern: use aggregate counters to find where to look, then selectively retain traces
A caller-callee failure correlation threshold is sufficient to classify dependencies as fail-close or fail-open.
Explicit premises:
  • Fail-close = failure consistently causes caller failure
  • Correlation >= 0.8 means fail-close; <= 0.2 means fail-open
Implicit premises:
  • Co-occurrence of failure implies causation along that edge
  • Thresholds 0.8/0.2 are appropriately calibrated for the mesh
  • Retry handling removes the main source of correlation inflation
Inferences:
  • Common-cause failures (a shared database failing both caller and callee) would be misclassified as fail-close edges
  • Edges rarely exercised in failure may stay 'unknown' indefinitely, biasing the catalogue toward noisy, frequently failing paths

Narratives

  • Thought experiment of waiting 5 hours 33 minutes to catch one failed request at 500 rps under sampled tracing.
    → Makes the statistical argument against sampled tracing visceral and motivates the metrics-based redesign.
  • Request-flow walkthrough: inbound middleware mints request ID and records caller; ID travels via Go context; outbound middleware records downstream call result; metrics emitted at completion.
    → Shows concretely how correlation is possible without a central trace store, supporting the middleware-inference argument.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ When a request fails in a mesh of thousands of services, how can engineers quickly know which dependency caused it and how the failure propagates?
    Classify each dependency edge as fail-close/fail-open/unknown from middleware-emitted failure correlation and chain classifications along call paths.
  • ✅ How do you observe rare failures when sampling makes tracing too slow?
    Collect aggregate caller/callee outcome metrics on all requests rather than sampled traces.
  • ⭕ How accurate is the classification, and how does it hold up as the mesh changes?
    Not addressed in the report.

Gaps

  • incompleteNo accuracy evidence for the classifier: no precision/recall against known incidents or chaos/fault-injection ground truth, no rationale for the 0.8/0.2 thresholds, and no treatment of common-cause failures that make caller and callee fail together without a true fail-close edge. Correlation is treated as causation.

  • incompleteThe tracing comparison is only against uniform head sampling. Error- and latency-biased tail sampling (which the article acknowledges exists in OpenTelemetry) would capture rare failures with high probability; the claimed overhead advantage of middleware metrics is asserted, not quantified, and the metrics approach cannot distinguish which branch of a request caused a failure.
    Read next: Benchmarks and cost studies of tail-based sampling at scale (OpenTelemetry collector tail sampling, Honeycomb/Lightstep refinery write-ups) versus metrics-based inference.
  • uninformedAll outcome claims (dependencies catalogued for critical APIs, 'most' fail-close anomalies indexed) are Uber's own, relayed by InfoQ without numbers, baselines, or any downstream effect on incidents, MTTR or fixes made. It is unclear what operators do once a fail-close edge is found.
    Read next: Uber's original blog post and any follow-up on incident-response outcomes; comparable service-dependency analysis reports from Netflix, Google (SRE), Meta.
  • incompleteFreshness and drift are not discussed: the dependency graph and fail-close/fail-open properties change with every deploy and retry-policy or circuit-breaker change, yet the article gives no re-classification cadence, coverage limits (async/queue-mediated calls, non-YARPC services), or how 'unknown' edges are resolved.
    Read next: Continuous service-dependency discovery and configuration-drift work; resilience pattern reviews (circuit breakers, bulkheads) that change failure semantics.
