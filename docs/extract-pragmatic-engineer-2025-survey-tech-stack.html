The Pragmatic Engineer 2025 Survey: What's in your tech stack?

Gergely Orosz · article · 2025-07-15 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Coding AgentsEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessVibe Coding
AI coding-tool adoption among software engineers has become near-universal (85% of ~3,000 respondents) by mid-2025, but which tools are used is strongly shaped by company size and policy — larger companies converge on a single vendor-supplied tool (GitHub Copilot) while smaller companies permit broader experimentation (Cursor, Claude, ChatGPT) — and genuine engineer adoption should be distinguished from 'vibe coding' tool usage, which the data shows is mostly non-engineers (founders, directors, engineering leads) prototyping rather than hands-on developers building production software.

theoretical · mixed — Primarily an empirical reader-survey report (2,997 respondents, April-May 2025) with the author's interpretive commentary layered on top. This extraction focuses on the 'AI tools' section and its company-size breakdowns, per the task's scope — the article's later sections (languages, IDEs, version control, cloud) are outside that scope and are not extracted here. The survey is self-selected from this newsletter's own readership, which skews toward senior/engaged engineers already following AI-engineering coverage — a sampling caveat the article does not raise about itself.

Key terms

  • vibe coding tools
    A distinct category from general-purpose AI coding assistants (Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code): tools like Vercel v0, Bolt.new, and Lovable, whose survey mentions come overwhelmingly from founders, director+/engineering-lead roles, and only secondarily from staff+ engineers — framed as prototyping/demo tools rather than tools hands-on engineers use for production work.
  • AI tools by company size
    The survey's cross-tab of tool mentions against company-size bands (50 or fewer up to 10,000+), used to infer that company size proxies for AI-tool policy strictness: bigger companies narrow choice to vendor-supplied tools (and to a single tool), smaller companies allow broader experimentation.

Arguments

An AI coding tool that markets itself as a replacement for software engineers, rather than an assistant to them, undermines its own developer adoption even if the positioning serves a separate sales narrative to non-developer buyers. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Devin markets itself explicitly as replacing developers ('personal AI engineering team', 'crush your backlog').
  • Devin shows markedly lower developer-mention rates in the survey than comparable coding-agent tools (Windsurf, Cursor).
  • Devin was previously shown to have faked parts of its launch demo, which the author separately notes as a credibility problem.
Implicit premises:
  • Low mention counts among this newsletter's self-selected engineer readership reflect genuinely low developer adoption, rather than the tool being newer, less marketed to engineers specifically, or used more by non-respondent populations.
  • A tool's marketing framing (rather than its underlying technical capability) is doing meaningful causal work in developers' tool choice, separable from other differences between Devin and its competitors.
Inferences:
  • A company pursuing both an 'AI replaces engineers' narrative for leadership/investor audiences and grassroots developer adoption for its product faces a structural tension between the two goals, and will likely have to choose one emphasis over time.

Narratives

  • The Devin/Windsurf/Cognition saga: OpenAI's $3B offer for Windsurf collapsed over Microsoft's IP rights in OpenAI; Google then 'bought out' Windsurf's core team and founders for $2.4B (who all moved to work on Gemini), leaving Windsurf a 'zombie' company with $100M cash and remaining staff; Cognition (Devin's maker) then acquired the leftover Windsurf entity.
    → Grounds the proposition that Devin's maker needed developer traction it lacked organically, by showing the lengths (acquiring a competitor's remnant) it went to in pursuit of that traction.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How does AI coding tool choice vary by company size, and why?
    Larger companies converge on fewer, enterprise-vendor-supplied tools (chiefly GitHub Copilot) under stricter policy; smaller companies permit broader multi-tool experimentation.
  • ✅ Are 'vibe coding' tools being adopted by engineers themselves, or by a different population?
    Mostly by non-hands-on roles (founders, directors, engineering leads) for prototyping, not by individual-contributor engineers for production work.
  • ⭕ How representative is this survey's company-size and tool-adoption breakdown of the broader engineering population?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe survey sample is self-selected from The Pragmatic Engineer's own newsletter readership — engineers already engaged enough with tooling discourse to read and respond to this specific newsletter — which plausibly skews toward more senior, more AI-engaged respondents than the general developer population. The article presents company-size breakdowns and adoption percentages without flagging or correcting for this selection effect anywhere.
    Read next: A survey run through a more neutral, broad-reach channel (e.g. Stack Overflow's or JetBrains' annual developer surveys) to check whether the company-size pattern found here replicates outside a tooling-newsletter audience.
  • incompleteThe survey measures only which AI tools respondents mention using, not any team- or process-level outcome (code review practice changes, who owns an AI-authored PR, delivery throughput or stability) — so it cannot speak to whether the company-size-driven adoption patterns it documents translate into better or worse team-level outcomes, which is exactly the question this reading's broader initiative (teams + coding agents in larger orgs) needs answered.
