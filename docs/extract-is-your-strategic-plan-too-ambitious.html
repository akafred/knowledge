Is Your Strategic Plan Too Ambitious? Or Not Ambitious Enough?

Rebecca Knight · article · 2026-09-10 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Decision-Making PracticesFeedback LoopsStrategic Ambition CalibrationStrategic CenteringStrategy & Direction-SettingTalent Density
How ambitious a strategic plan should be is not an intrinsic property of the target number but something you calibrate by systematically examining the underlying problem, your available resources and talent, your tolerance for uncertainty, your measurement time horizon, and your competitive position — a deliberate exercise most organizations skip, defaulting instead to timid, business-as-usual plans.

practical · expository — A prescriptive HBR advice piece: an eight-question diagnostic checklist built from two expert interviews (Sheena Iyengar, Donald Sull) plus short illustrative case narratives (Bezos/Amazon, Burberry/China), closing with a Do/Don't summary. It argues for a method of judging plans, not for a theory of what ambition is.

Key terms

  • Ambition (of a plan)
    A calibrated property of a strategic plan judged relative to the problem being solved, available resources/talent, uncertainty, competitors, and execution capacity — not a fixed or self-evident attribute of the target number itself.
  • Reallocation (vs. allocation)
    Sull's distinction: truly ambitious ('10x') goals require deliberately pulling money and people off existing committed projects and redirecting them to the new one, not simply requesting incremental new funding.

Arguments

Leaders should force themselves to generate multiple, deliberately dissimilar strategic alternatives before committing to a level of ambition. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The pull toward conservative goals runs deep in organizations.
  • A single option evaluated in isolation cannot reveal whether it is too timid or too bold.
  • Building 3-5 distinct alternatives (mirroring competitors, aggressive, and outside-industry-inspired) surfaces trade-offs.
Implicit premises:
  • Judgment about ambition is comparative, not absolute — people can only calibrate boldness against contrast, not against an internally imagined baseline.
  • Left unstructured, planning processes default to imitation of competitors and the lowest-hanging fruit unless a contrasting alternative is deliberately manufactured.
Inferences:
  • Alternative-generation should be a mandated step in planning processes (e.g., a required 'aggressive' and 'outside-industry' option), not left to individual initiative, since the article implies people won't generate the contrast on their own.
A plan's funding mechanism (incremental allocation vs. deliberate reallocation) is a reliable test of whether it is genuinely ambitious. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Incremental improvements can be funded incrementally.
  • 10x-scale goals cannot be achieved incrementally.
  • Reallocation means taking committed money and people from existing projects, which owners resist ('fight tooth and nail').
Implicit premises:
  • Existing budget and staffing allocations reflect organizational inertia and political entrenchment rather than an optimal allocation for whatever the new ambition requires — i.e., there is slack to be reclaimed if leadership is willing to force the fight.
  • A leader who is unwilling or unable to win that internal reallocation fight cannot actually deliver on a stated ambitious goal, regardless of how the goal itself is framed.
Inferences:
  • The real measure of an organization's strategic ambition is not the stated target but the amount of internal political capital its leadership is willing to spend reallocating resources toward it.
Extending the measurement time horizon and inserting interim milestones is itself a lever for raising the achievable level of ambition, independent of the target chosen.
Explicit premises:
  • Annual targets push teams toward incremental innovation.
  • A multi-year target broken into quarterly benchmarks (the Burberry case) let an organization sustain investor confidence while pursuing a bold multi-year goal.
Implicit premises:
  • Stakeholders (investors, boards, employees) will tolerate a longer runway toward a big goal only if they can observe legible interim progress signals — patience is conditional, not open-ended.
  • The cadence of measurement is itself a design choice under the organization's control, separate from the size of the ultimate target.
Inferences:
  • Organizations that cannot or will not change their internal reporting/measurement cadence away from annual cycles have effectively capped their own strategic ambition, regardless of what targets they announce.

Narratives

  • Jeff Bezos launching Amazon narrowly with books — easy to ship, simple to scale — while holding far grander ambitions from the outset.
    → Supports the claim that starting small in scope of execution is compatible with, and even a deliberate technique for, pursuing very high ambition under uncertainty — i.e., 'start small while aiming high' rather than scaling down the goal itself.
  • Burberry's expansion into China under CEO Angela Ahrendts (2006-2014), which roughly tripled company revenue, reported via interim indicators like comparable regional sales growth to keep investors on board during a slow-to-materialize bet.
    → Supports the claim that ambitious multi-year plans survive organizational and investor patience only when paired with measurable interim progress signals, not just a distant final target.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How should a leader judge whether a strategic plan is ambitious enough, or too ambitious?
    Resolved via an eight-question diagnostic checklist (problem definition, alternatives, funding, staffing, uncertainty/experimentation, time horizon, progress measurement, competitive benchmarking) rather than a single formula or threshold.
  • ⭕ How do you actually execute a 'reallocation' of budget and talent when the people who currently hold them will 'fight tooth and nail' to keep them?
    The political difficulty is named explicitly but no method, forcing function, or example of successfully overcoming it is offered.
  • ⭕ When the eight diagnostic signals conflict (e.g., a plan is well-funded but poorly staffed, or faces high uncertainty but is locked into a board-mandated annual reporting cycle), which signal should take precedence?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe eight diagnostic questions are presented as a flat checklist with no guidance on how to weigh or prioritize them when they point in different directions — e.g., adequate funding paired with weak staffing, or high uncertainty paired with an externally mandated annual measurement cycle.
    Read next: Multi-criteria decision frameworks or strategy-scorecard literature that combine qualitative heuristics like these into a single calibration score or decision rule.
  • incompleteThe article names the central political obstacle to ambitious plans — incumbents who 'fight tooth and nail' to keep budget and talent — but offers no method for actually winning that internal reallocation fight (e.g., zero-based budgeting, executive mandate, sunset clauses on existing projects).
    Read next: Resource-allocation and organizational-politics literature on how leaders force reallocation against entrenched internal claimants (e.g., zero-based budgeting practice, 'kill lists' for legacy initiatives).
  • uninformedAll supporting evidence is qualitative and anecdotal (two expert interviews, the Bezos/Amazon anecdote, one Burberry case) with no data on the relative frequency or cost of the two failure modes — plans that were too timid vs. plans that were too ambitious — so a reader cannot tell which error is more common or more costly in practice.
    Read next: Empirical strategy-execution research on goal-setting outcomes (e.g., academic stretch-goal research, or large-sample studies of strategic-plan success/failure rates).
  • illogicalThe piece treats 'start small while aiming high' (the Bezos narrative) and '10x goals can't be achieved incrementally' (Sull's funding argument) as compatible, but doesn't reconcile that starting with books was itself an incremental first step — the article never clearly distinguishes incremental execution in service of a big vision from incremental ambition-setting, even though only the latter is framed as the failure mode to avoid.
    Read next: Real-options theory or staged-investment strategy models that formalize the distinction between a small, reversible first step and a capped level of ambition.
