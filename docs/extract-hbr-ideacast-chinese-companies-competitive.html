What Makes Chinese Companies So Competitive?

HBR IdeaCast (hosts Alison Beard and Adi Ignatius, guest researcher Dan Wang) · podcast · 2026-07-28 Reader 🖥️

Organizational LegibilitySystemic Apprenticeship & Skill Transmission
China's competitiveness comes not primarily from cheap labor, IP theft, or subsidies but from operating as an 'engineering state' that has preserved a dense, tacit 'process knowledge' industrial ecosystem the US allowed to atrophy — a real technocratic strength that coexists with, and does not translate into, either political predictability for entrepreneurs or a better quality of life for ordinary Chinese workers.

theoretical · argumentative — Primarily explanatory (why is China competitive?) built through Wang's engineering-state/lawyerly-society dichotomy, with practical advice to foreign companies appended at the end. The reasoning is carried largely through anecdote and fieldwork impressions rather than formal argument.

Key terms

  • Engineering state
    A political-economic system whose leadership is dominated by people with an engineering/technocratic disposition, oriented toward building — infrastructure, manufacturing, technology — and comfortable with opaque, unappealable administrative decisions, in contrast to a system oriented around legal rights and transparent procedure.
  • Process knowledge
    The third and most underappreciated component of technology — tacit, unwritten industrial experience embodied in workers and organizations ('everything that lives in our hands and between our heads') that cannot be conveyed by tooling or written instructions alone, and is acquired only through practice/apprenticeship.
  • Core competence
    For Chinese companies, Wang says the operative core competence is simply 'making money' — i.e., a willingness to retool production for whatever the market demands, rather than staying within a narrow specialization.

Arguments

China's manufacturing dominance rests substantially on preserved tacit 'process knowledge,' not primarily on labor cost, subsidy, or IP theft. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Technology comprises tooling, direct instruction, and process knowledge.
  • Process knowledge is tacit — transmitted only through hands-on practice and apprenticeship, not written down.
  • China retained a dense ecosystem of experienced industrial labor while the US offshored much of its own manufacturing workforce and, with it, that experience.
Implicit premises:
  • Tacit knowledge, once lost, cannot be quickly rebuilt merely by re-acquiring tooling or written instructions — you cannot buy your way back into manufacturing competence.
  • A large population's accumulated hours of hands-on industrial practice is a durable, hard-to-replicate national asset, functioning almost like an economic moat.
Inferences:
  • Industrial-policy debates that focus only on subsidies, tariffs, or reshoring capital may fail if they don't also address the multi-year, hands-on process of rebuilding a skilled, experienced workforce.
  • The same logic implies that any country attempting to reshore manufacturing (not just the US) faces a structural, time-bound constraint independent of investment levels.
China's economic and technological success does not equate to broad human flourishing for its citizens.
Explicit premises:
  • Youth unemployment exceeds 15%.
  • The property sector has declined roughly 30% over five years.
  • Healthcare remains weak and does not meet people's needs.
  • Households maintain high precautionary savings because they expect little institutional support.
  • The state continues prioritizing infrastructure and manufacturing spending over these welfare gaps.
Implicit premises:
  • A state's investment choices reveal what it actually optimizes for (geopolitical position) as distinct from what it claims or what citizens want (welfare).
  • Infrastructure/manufacturing spending and welfare spending compete for the same finite resources rather than being simply additive.
Inferences:
  • A country can simultaneously be 'engineering-state competitive' by industrial metrics (world's largest EV/drone/telecom maker) and welfare-deficient by human metrics — the two should be evaluated separately, not conflated.

Narratives

  • During COVID, US companies debating whether to make masks, cotton swabs, or air filters largely decided this fell outside their 'core competence' and declined; in China, BYD (an EV maker) and Foxconn (an iPhone assembler) retooled their lines and produced their own branded masks.
    → Illustrates the reinterpreted 'core competence' key term and supports the proposition that Chinese firms treat market responsiveness itself as their defining capability.
  • Jack Ma relocated to Japan and stepped back from running Alibaba after an 'impolitic speech'; ByteDance founder Zhang Yiming substantially relocated to Singapore.
    → Supports the proposition that China's opacity and political unpredictability impose real costs on even the most successful entrepreneurs.
  • The Chinese bestseller 'I Make Deliveries in Beijing' depicts the grinding daily life of gig delivery workers, a growing share of whom are college graduates working DoorDash/Uber-equivalent jobs because the labor market is so soft.
    → Supports the proposition that industrial/infrastructure success has not improved ordinary workers' quality of life.
  • The kitchen analogy: giving a 15-year-old who has never cooked the best-equipped kitchen and the most exquisite recipe still won't produce edible scrambled eggs, because that requires trained, embodied skill.
    → Illustrates the 'process knowledge' key term and grounds the claim that tacit skill, not tools or instructions, is the scarce ingredient.
  • A major US financial-institution executive can, in principle, reach President Trump on his personal cell phone if they try hard enough — something inconceivable for almost anyone at a comparable level trying to reach Beijing's leadership.
    → Illustrates the lawyerly-society/engineering-state contrast: US politics are influenceable but infrastructure execution is uneven; Chinese infrastructure execution is excellent but politics are nearly unreachable.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What actually makes Chinese companies competitive, beyond cheap labor, subsidies, or IP theft?
    A dense ecosystem of skilled labor, hyper-competitive domestic rivalry, and preserved tacit process knowledge, framed within an 'engineering state' model that plans infrastructure well but is politically opaque.
  • ✅ Why do dominant Chinese manufacturers (BYD, DJI, Huawei) lack globally recognized consumer brands?
    Branding lags manufacturing quality but historically catches up, as it did for Japan and South Korea; also compounded by US bans that keep American consumers unfamiliar with the products.
  • ✅ Is it currently a good time/place to be a Chinese tech entrepreneur?
    No — genuinely difficult and risky, evidenced by prominent founders relocating abroad and a broader crackdown on tech/internet firms.
  • ✅ Does China's manufacturing and infrastructure success improve life for ordinary Chinese people?
    No — high youth unemployment, a declining property sector, weak healthcare, and high precautionary savings show industrial success has not translated into broad-based welfare gains.
  • ⭕ Should foreign companies continue investing in China right now?
    Partially — Wang suggests maintaining existing investments given the market's continued size and residual growth, while being more wary of Chinese competitors encroaching on home markets, but he gives no clear answer on new investment.
  • ⭕ Is the tacit 'process-knowledge' explanation for China's manufacturing edge actually falsifiable, or could it just be a redescription of 'China makes more stuff, therefore has more manufacturing experience'?
  • ⭕ If prominent founders keep leaving China (Ma to Japan, Zhang Yiming to Singapore) to escape political risk, does that emigration itself threaten the entrepreneurial dynamism and knowledge ecosystem Wang credits for China's competitiveness?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe 'process knowledge' thesis — the claim that China's tacit industrial experience is what the US lacks and cannot quickly rebuild — rests entirely on Wang's qualitative fieldwork (conversations from 2017-2023) and a handful of named firms (BYD, Foxconn, Boeing, Intel, Detroit automakers). No comparative or quantitative measure of tacit-knowledge stock (e.g., manufacturing workforce tenure distributions, apprenticeship completion rates, factory yield/defect-rate curves) is offered to substantiate that China's advantage is systematic rather than a compelling but unverified narrative fitted to a few salient cases.
    Read next: Industrial-economics literature on learning curves and tacit knowledge in manufacturing (e.g., aerospace and semiconductor yield-curve studies); cross-national productivity/quality benchmarking of factories; Polanyi's 'The Tacit Dimension' for the underlying epistemic claim.
  • incompleteWang treats China's 'hyper-competitive corporate system' (dozens of EV makers, thousands of rival firms) as a straightforwardly positive driver of manufacturing excellence, but does not engage the well-documented downside of that same dynamic — chronic overcapacity and price wars in Chinese EV, solar, and battery manufacturing that erode the firms' own margins and have triggered anti-dumping tariffs abroad. The competitive intensity he credits for excellence may simultaneously be a source of financial fragility.
    Read next: Reporting and analysis on Chinese EV/solar/battery overcapacity and international anti-dumping actions (EU/US tariff responses); industry-level margin data for BYD, CATL, and Chinese solar manufacturers.
  • incompleteWang doesn't reconcile an internal tension: he argues prominent entrepreneurs are relocating abroad (Ma to Japan, Zhang Yiming to Singapore) to escape political risk, while also crediting China's dense, hyper-competitive ecosystem for its continued industrial dominance. He never addresses whether founder emigration is a leading indicator that erodes the same entrepreneurial/knowledge ecosystem he credits for competitiveness, or whether founders and the underlying industrial workforce are cleanly separable such that the departures don't matter.
    Read next: Research on founder/entrepreneur emigration and its effect on domestic startup ecosystems; comparative cases (e.g., Russian tech emigration post-2022) on whether elite flight measurably weakens an economy's innovation base.
  • uninformedThe entire conversation is framed as a US-China binary — 'engineering state' vs. 'lawyerly society' — with no consideration of a third model, such as European/Nordic coordinated-market economies that combine strong worker protections and rule-of-law transparency with selective industrial policy and high-quality infrastructure (e.g., Norway's state ownership stakes, German Mittelstand manufacturing). A listener outside the US is left without guidance on which elements of the 'engineering state' argument — if any — are adoptable inside a transparent, rights-based system, since Wang's framework treats the two poles as effectively exhaustive alternatives rather than a spectrum.
    Read next: Comparative political-economy literature on coordinated market economies (Hall & Soskice's 'Varieties of Capitalism'); Norwegian/Nordic industrial-policy case studies (state ownership in Equinor, Norwegian sovereign wealth fund governance) as a test of whether infrastructure/manufacturing strength requires trading away legal transparency.
