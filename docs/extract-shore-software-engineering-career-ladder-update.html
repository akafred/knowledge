Update on Software Engineering Career Ladder

jamesshore.com · article · 2024-08-20 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Career DevelopmentHands-On Leadership & the PendulumInformal LeadershipPsychological SafetyTeam AutonomyTechnical Leadership Track (IC / Staff-Plus Ladder)
OpenSesame's redesigned career ladder deliberately shifts the organization's definition of engineering seniority away from individual ownership and self-promotion toward teamwork, peer leadership, and manager-verified skill fluency -- trading the old system's clarity and perceived fairness for reduced self-promotion/tenure bias, at the real cost of a heavier, judgment-intensive evaluation burden that falls unevenly on managers depending on how well they already know their people.

practical · expository — A practitioner's progress report: James Shore (VP of Engineering at OpenSesame) reports on rolling out a redesigned career ladder he had previously published, distinguishing what's working from what's still an open question.

Key terms

  • fluency (skill rating: None / Learning / Proficient / Fluent)
    A four-level per-skill rating, where 'Fluent' means the skill is second nature and exercised whenever appropriate; promotion requires fluency (not merely proficiency) in every skill of a title and all prior titles, with individual skills waivable at a manager's discretion.
  • player-coach (Technical Lead, Staff Engineer)
    A technical-track leadership role whose majority of time is still spent coding and working hands-on alongside team members, distinct from a people-management role; Staff Engineer extends this across multiple teams rather than a single one.
  • manage the system (vs. managing the work)
    Shore's definition of an Engineering Manager's actual job: engineer a system that gives the team the context, skills, resources, and peer leadership it needs to excel without the manager's direct involvement -- not telling people what to do.
  • leaderful teams / peer leadership
    Leadership distributed across team members and expected well before any formal leadership title -- named skills starting at the Software Engineer level (basic facilitation, team steward) and formalized further at Senior Software Engineer (peer leadership) and Technical Lead (leaderful teams).

Arguments

Shifting career-ladder evaluation from employee-led to manager-led reduces self-promotion and tenure bias, but this benefit is paid for by a large, unevenly distributed burden on managers. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The old system rewarded 'butt in seat' time and self-promotion ability
  • The new system removes the employee's burden of assembling evidence and shifts the evaluative work onto managers, across a much wider skill set
  • Managers who already know their people find the work tolerable (mostly documentation); managers new to a team find it genuinely hard because they lack the requisite intimate knowledge
Implicit premises:
  • A manager's depth of knowledge about an employee is unevenly distributed across an organization (new managers, reorganized teams), not a fixed baseline competency
  • The theory that the burden 'will only be difficult the first time' is itself untested
Inferences:
  • An organization adopting manager-led evaluation should expect a transition period of uneven evaluation quality correlated with how long a manager has worked with a given team, not uniformly improved fairness from day one
Formal engineering seniority -- even 'Senior,' and certainly Technical Lead and Staff Engineer -- should be defined primarily around peer leadership and systemic contribution, not around solo technical ownership or novel-technology exploration. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The previous ladder, which emphasized individual ownership and investigating new technologies, is being explicitly replaced
  • 'Peer leadership' and 'leaderful teams' appear as named skills well before any formal leadership title
  • Technical Lead and Staff Engineer are defined as player-coach roles whose main currency is working alongside others and cross-pollinating, not managing people or building things solo
Implicit premises:
  • The previous ladder's culture-shaping effect (rewarding solo ownership and tech exploration) was judged undesirable enough to warrant a full redesign
  • Peer leadership and systemic contribution are things an organization can reliably observe and rate via a skills rubric, even though they are less legible than individually-authored deliverables
Inferences:
  • This implies the career ladder itself functions as a deliberate culture-shaping lever, not just a compensation/title mechanism -- the skills an organization chooses to formalize and reward are themselves a behavioral intervention

Narratives

  • Managers report seeing shifts in behavior under the new ladder -- people volunteering to lead meetings and take on work they didn't take on before -- and the new ladder has also been usable as a touchstone for addressing performance problems.
    → Supports the proposition that the culture-change goal of the redesign is showing early, if anecdotal, success.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Is the culture-change goal of the new ladder (teamwork and peer leadership over individual ownership) actually working?
    So far, yes -- per manager-reported behavior change (more volunteering, more initiative), though the author treats this as early, anecdotal evidence rather than settled proof.
  • ⭕ Does manager-led evaluation reduce the old system's bias without creating an unacceptable new burden?
    Explicitly left open ('jury's still out'): it is tolerable for managers who already know their people, hard for managers who don't, and whether it gets easier over time is an untested theory.
  • ⭕ Do employees perceive the new, manager-led system as fairer or better than the old, employee-led one?
  • ✅ What should an Engineering Manager's job actually consist of, as distinct from an engineer's?
    Engineer the system (context, skills, resources, peer leadership) that lets the team excel without the manager's direct involvement -- explicitly not directing day-to-day work.
  • ⭕ Is a 100+-skill, highly granular evaluation rubric itself usable and bias-resistant at scale, or could its own complexity reproduce a new kind of evaluator bias (e.g., toward managers with more rubric-literacy or more 1:1 time)?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece names the manager-led evaluation burden as a known cost but gives no account of how much manager time it actually consumes, and no fallback plan if the stated theory -- that the burden 'is only going to be difficult the first time, and then will get easier' -- turns out not to hold.
    Read next: A later Shore update or comparable published career-ladder retrospectives reporting measured manager time burden over multiple cycles.
  • uninformedPublished August 2024, the ladder has no skill addressing AI-assisted development, coding-agent supervision, or AI fluency at any level -- even though the same period's industry discourse was already reframing exactly the skillset the ladder specifies for Staff Engineer and Technical Lead (defining outcomes, reducing ambiguity, mentoring, architectural review, cross-pollination) as increasingly valuable, and increasingly time-starved, specifically because of AI coding agents.
    Read next: extract-mokhtar-staff-engineer-leading-agents and extract-the-future-of-software-engineering, which argue this exact Staff Engineer skillset becomes more load-bearing (and more oversubscribed) as teams adopt coding agents.
  • incompleteThere is no discussion of how skill ratings are verified beyond a single manager's own judgment -- no peer input, no cross-manager calibration process, no appeals mechanism -- despite the explicit goal of reducing bias; if two managers rate the same demonstrated behavior differently, the piece gives no mechanism for catching or correcting that.
    Read next: Calibration/peer-review mechanisms in other practitioner career-ladder writeups, or a later Shore follow-up addressing cross-manager consistency.
