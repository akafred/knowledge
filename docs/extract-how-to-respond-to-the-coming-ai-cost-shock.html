How to Respond to the Coming AI Cost Shock

As enterprise software vendors end subsidized AI pricing in favor of unpredictable usage-based billing, leaders must stop treating AI adoption as a software procurement decision and instead manage it as an organizational-design decision — modeling true cost elasticity, protecting core institutional capabilities against AI dependency, and integrating token budgets into workforce planning.

Key terms

  • AI elasticity
    The sensitivity of an organization's AI costs to usage variables — interactions per agent, units consumed per request, and price per unit — that are largely outside the buyer's control, making abstract budgeting for AI ineffective.
  • shadow costs
    The AI costs beyond visible token/API billing: infrastructure to run the tools, power consumption, in-house tech teams, and training/change management time.
  • token budgets (as workforce-planning input)
    AI token allocation treated as a line item analogous to headcount — a resource to be planned, capped, and traded off against human staffing decisions — rather than an IT expense.

Arguments

AI investment should be managed as an organizational-design decision, not a software procurement decision. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Vendor subsidies are ending and usage-based pricing is replacing them.
  • The pricing variables that determine cost are outside the buyer's control, so costs are structurally unpredictable.
  • Deploying AI agents re-architects entire workflows and processes around the technology.
  • AI deployment shifts resources from owned labor capacity to rented, variable token consumption.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizational-design decisions warrant deliberate, cross-functional, strategic planning in a way routine software procurement does not.
  • The financial and structural stakes of getting AI cost wrong are comparable to the stakes of workforce restructuring, not to the stakes of choosing a software vendor.
Inferences:
  • If AI investment is truly an organizational-design decision, procurement or IT alone should not own it — HR, finance, and line-of-business leadership should co-own it. The article gestures at cross-department involvement but never states this ownership implication directly.
Organizations should retain expert talent or contractors for core functions even where AI now handles daily execution. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Institutional knowledge and expertise leave the organization when the employees who held them are replaced by AI.
  • Without in-house expertise, an organization has no option but to keep paying an AI vendor even if pricing becomes prohibitive.
Implicit premises:
  • Retained human expertise functions as effective negotiating leverage, or a viable operational fallback, if AI pricing becomes prohibitive.
  • Keeping expert contractors 'at the ready' is affordable enough, relative to the AI savings it hedges, to be a practical recommendation rather than merely a theoretical one.
Inferences:
  • This implies talent-retention costs should be explicitly budgeted as an AI-dependency insurance premium — a framing the article never makes explicit or quantifies.

Narratives

  • Uber, unnamed 'other companies', and an AI consultant's client who spent half a billion dollars in a single month all exhausted their 2026 AI budgets far faster than planned.
    → Establishes that AI costs are already large and unpredictable even before vendor subsidies fully disappear — grounding proposition 3 and the urgency behind the whole framework.
  • Oracle, SAP, and Workday are each independently moving toward usage-based or tiered premium-AI pricing, with Workday explicitly delaying its shift to January 2027 via a stated grace period.
    → Grounds the abstract 'subsidy era is ending' claim in concrete, checkable vendor behavior across three major enterprise software vendors.
  • A worked illustrative example: a 10,000-employee company piloting one premium AI system sees costs balloon from $3,600/year (10% adoption) to $27,600/year (50% adoption) under Oracle's per-unit model, and would double again if the per-unit price rose from one cent to two.
    → Makes the 'AI elasticity' concept concrete by showing how quickly a seemingly modest linear cost structure compounds with scale — supporting the unpredictability proposition (proposition 4).
  • SAP's workforce-planning tool already pairs headcount performance dashboards with AI token budgets, letting managers compare a 'cost-optimized... automation of roles' option against 'structured reskilling by job families'.
    → Illustrates proposition 9 (integrate token budgets into workforce planning) with a real, already-shipping vendor tool, making the recommendation feel current rather than speculative.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How should organizations respond to enterprise AI vendors ending subsidized pricing?
    A three-step operational framework: model true AI elasticity, protect core functions/talent, and integrate token budgets into workforce planning.
  • ⭕ How much should an organization actually budget for AI token consumption?
    The author explicitly says setting an overall AI budget 'in the abstract' is useless, and offers a method (calculate ROI at current usage/rates, derive a maximum justifiable per-unit cost) but never produces an actual budgeting formula or number.
  • ⭕ Who within the organization should own AI-cost-to-workforce-planning integration decisions?
    The article implies broad, cross-departmental involvement ('decisions about these investments should be made by all departments') but never specifies decision rights or a single accountable owner.
