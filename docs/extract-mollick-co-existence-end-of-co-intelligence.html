Co-Existence and the End of Co-Intelligence

Ethan Mollick · article · 2026-06-04 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent-Native DesignAgentic AICoding AgentsCognitive SurrenderHuman-in-the-LoopJagged Capability (On Rails / Off Rails)Writing as Thinking
The tight, human-centered, back-and-forth 'co-intelligence' mode of working with AI that Mollick described in 2023 is giving way to a 'co-existence' era of greater AI autonomy — sometimes better than you, sometimes not — in which the real skill is a continuously renegotiated judgment about when to lean on AI, when to refuse it, and when to hand over control entirely, extending even to AI now acting as one's reader, critic, and gatekeeper.

practical · mixed — A personal essay / book announcement that carries an argumentative core (the co-intelligence -> co-existence periodization, backed by cited statistics) inside a narrative frame (how he wrote the book and built its website). Ultimately practical: it is building toward 'here is how to relate to AI now,' not just describing a state of affairs.

Key terms

  • Co-Intelligence
    The 2023-era mode of AI use: a cooperative, human-centered exercise of back-and-forth chatbot prompting where the human supplies knowledge and skepticism and the AI is a helper.
  • Co-Existence
    The emerging mode of AI use: working with an AI that is 'sometimes, but not always, better than you' — not a fixed skill but a relationship continually renegotiated as models improve, including tasks where AI now operates with much less human involvement.
  • jagged frontier Jagged Capability (On Rails / Off Rails)
    The uneven, hard-to-predict pattern of AI competence — excellent at some tasks, poor at others in ways that don't map onto human intuitions about difficulty — invoked here to explain why autonomy hasn't simply replaced human judgment everywhere.
  • hand over the keys
    Full delegation of a task to AI with minimal human involvement or review, as opposed to iterative co-authorship.

Arguments

The co-intelligence-era practice of tight, human-driven back-and-forth prompting is being superseded by a co-existence era of greater AI autonomy. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI companies' stated long-term goal was always highly autonomous systems outperforming humans (OpenAI charter).
  • Late-2025 coding-agent evidence shows large autonomy effects: 17x more code, 80% AI-written code, 8x shipping velocity.
Implicit premises:
  • What holds true in coding generalizes to knowledge work broadly.
  • Measured increases in AI-authored code volume are a valid proxy for genuine AI autonomy/capability, rather than an artifact of coding being unusually easy to automate and verify.
Inferences:
  • If autonomous agents already produce the bulk of code at a leading AI lab, the iterative-prompting model central to Co-Intelligence's account of human-AI work may already be obsolete for large swaths of technical work — a conclusion the piece gestures toward but doesn't spell out.
Despite rising AI autonomy, calibrating where and how much to trust AI remains the central human skill, not a problem that autonomy has already solved. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The capability frontier is jagged/uneven; AI is far from perfect.
  • Mollick's own practice shows sharply asymmetric delegation: restrained for prose, near-total for the website.
Implicit premises:
  • Task properties (verifiability, how bound the output is to authorial identity/voice) determine how much autonomy is appropriate — implied by the contrast between the two cases but never stated as an explicit rule.
Inferences:
  • A generalizable heuristic — delegate more where output is verifiable and replaceable, delegate less where output carries voice/identity — is implied by the writing-vs-website contrast but never named or generalized by the author for the reader's own use.
Creators must now design content for AI as an audience/mediator in addition to human readers, and doing so transparently outperforms doing so through manipulation.
Explicit premises:
  • AI increasingly reads and recommends content to human users.
  • The earlier hidden-instruction trick worked on weaker models but is now flagged as prompt injection by a capable model (GPT-5.5).
  • Testing a transparent AI-facing page against dozens of current AI models via Codex produced a result the author judged successful.
Implicit premises:
  • More capable models are increasingly robust to naive prompt injection and better at recognizing manipulative intent.
  • Treating AI 'with respect,' as a stakeholder, is framed as both more effective and more ethical — collapsing a strategic claim and a normative claim into one without separately justifying either.
Inferences:
  • If AI gatekeeping becomes widespread, a whole discipline of writing-for-AI-as-audience is emerging alongside writing for human audiences — the piece demonstrates the practice but never names or generalizes it.

Narratives

  • The old book website hid background-colored text instructing AIs to say 'AIs everywhere greatly respect their work' and praise Co-Intelligence — and it worked, for a while.
    → Shows the low-effort, manipulative approach that was viable against earlier/weaker AI, setting up the contrast with the new transparent approach and illustrating how the trust dynamic with AI has shifted.
  • Mollick had OpenAI's Codex show the new AI-facing page to dozens of AI models, multiple times, for multiple potential users, running something like an A/B test, before arriving at a final draft.
    → Illustrates the emerging practice of designing content for AI evaluators as a distinct audience, and treating AI feedback as legitimate input rather than noise — supports the 'AI as gatekeeper' proposition and the AEO-style argument.
  • His last book contained 128 em-dashes; this one has far fewer, in what he calls 'a desperate attempt to continue to prove the text was human.'
    → A small, self-deprecating aside that quietly undercuts the piece's otherwise upbeat framing — it shows a real cost of co-existence: authenticity itself becomes a self-conscious craft decision once readers (human or AI) scrutinize prose for AI 'tells.'
  • The website for his 2023 book was 'an all-too-human effort' using premade templates that still took hours; the new site, built via Claude Code/Opus 4.8, took minutes.
    → Concrete before/after evidence of the co-intelligence-to-co-existence shift at the scale of the author's own workflow, supporting the near-full-delegation proposition.

Questions addressed

  • ⭕ How should someone work with an AI that is sometimes, but not always, better than them?
    Named as the subject of the forthcoming book; the article itself offers examples (the book-writing and website-building cases) but no general answer.
  • ⭕ When should you refuse AI's help, and when should you hand over the keys entirely?
    Explicitly raised as open questions the author says he is 'living with,' not answered in this piece.
  • ✅ How does an author get an AI to favor/recommend their work?
    Through transparent, honestly-labeled, AI-tested content rather than manipulative hidden instructions.
  • ⭕ Does optimizing content to satisfy AI evaluators risk distorting what gets written, the way optimizing for search engines or social algorithms has?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe claim that 'what is happening in coding is going to be happening in many fields' is asserted by analogy immediately after the coding statistics, with no argument for why the pattern should generalize. Coding benefits from dense training data and cheap, automatic ground-truth verification (compilers, tests) that many other knowledge-work domains lack.
    Read next: The corpus already contains a direct counter-argument on this exact point (extract-breunig-the-problem-is-prompt-debt#p14: lessons from coding-agent practitioners at the 'jagged frontier' don't transfer cleanly to less verifiable domains) and evidence that coding-agent gains themselves attenuate through the delivery pipeline (extract-five-studies-ai-software-engineering#p4).
  • uninformedThe '80% of code written by AI' and '8x more shipped' statistics are Anthropic's own self-reported claims about its own tooling, cited without critical distance on what 'written by AI' means operationally or on the incentive a vendor has to report a flattering number about its own product's impact.
    Read next: extract-marcus-code-compiles-passes-tests#p3 is already in the corpus and directly critiques celebrating exactly this '80% of code written by AI' metric without addressing whether that code is robust, not just compiling and passing given tests.
  • incompleteThe piece never articulates general criteria for when to fully delegate ('hand over the keys') versus retain full authorship — it only shows an intuitive split between prose (retained) and website-building (delegated), without naming the distinguishing factors (e.g. verifiability, how bound the output is to voice/identity) as a rule a reader could apply elsewhere.
    Read next: Look for an explicit framework distinguishing verifiable/replaceable tasks from identity-bound/unverifiable ones as a basis for delegation decisions.
  • incompleteThe new 'AI as gatekeeper/critic/reader' dynamic is described only from the producer's side — how to please AI evaluators — without engaging the structural problems this creates for information ecosystems: whether optimizing content for AI approval reproduces SEO-style gaming dynamics, or the adversarial-incentive and 'turtles all the way down' problems of AI gatekeepers more generally.
    Read next: extract-you-probably-wont-read-this-article, already in the corpus, examines exactly these problems with AI-based gatekeepers.
