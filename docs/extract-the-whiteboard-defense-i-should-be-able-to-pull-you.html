The "whiteboard defense:" I should be able to pull you...

Mitchell Hashimoto · tweet · 2026-09-16 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Capacity to Verify AI OutputAnswerabilityCognitive DebtDisposable vs. Durable CodeVibe Coding
Responsible AI use in software is not defined by who or what wrote the code, but by whether the shipper can, on demand, explain and defend the design of any customer-facing system at a high level (not line level) -- while throwaway PoCs and demos are exempt and may be fully generated and not understood at all.

practical · argumentative — A short normative post that sets a standard of conduct ("you should be able to...") for engineers who use AI. Argued by stipulation and example questions rather than by evidence; the propositions all serve a single 'you should' rule with an explicit carve-out.

Key terms

  • Whiteboard defense
    An on-demand, informal oral examination in which the shipper must explain how a customer-facing system works and defend its design decisions, as the benchmark for responsible AI usage.
  • Customer-facing
    Anything shipped to real users/customers -- the line that triggers the understanding obligation (in contrast to PoCs, demos, experiments).
  • Understand at a high level
    Being able to answer design-rationale, adversarial, data-structure and failure questions confidently, without remembering function names or implementation details.
  • PoC / demo / experiment exemption
    A class of work where speed beats quality and total non-understanding of generated output is acceptable.

Arguments

The whiteboard defense is the right benchmark for responsible AI use in shipped software.
Explicit premises:
  • Customer-facing systems affect real customers.
  • The shipper should be able to explain how such a system works and defend its decisions.
  • Line-level code familiarity is not needed for that; design-level answers are.
Implicit premises:
  • Being able to answer design-level questions is a good proxy for actually having the understanding needed to ship responsibly.
  • Design-level understanding is sufficient to catch or handle the failures that matter, so line-level knowledge is dispensable.
  • An informal questioner can judge whether the answers are genuinely understood rather than fluently recited.
  • Understanding at ship time persists (or can be refreshed) for as long as the system is in use.
  • Whether the code was written by a human or an AI does not change what the shipper owes.
Inferences:
  • Authorship by AI becomes morally and practically neutral -- the standard is the same as for a human-written system, which makes the rule tool-agnostic.
  • Because the test is oral and on-demand, it penalises 'prompt and ship' but not heavy AI use paired with genuine design engagement.
  • As agents multiply the number of systems one person ships, the number of systems any one person can credibly defend is capped by human attention (a scaling limit the post does not discuss).
Understanding can be dropped entirely for PoCs, demos and experiments. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • These artifacts are not customer-facing.
  • In these cases speed matters more than quality.
Implicit premises:
  • The cost of a defect in a PoC is bounded and contained.
  • PoCs are discarded or rewritten rather than promoted to production.
  • The author can reliably tell, at creation time, which category a piece of work belongs to.
Inferences:
  • The rule creates a promotion event: when a PoC becomes customer-facing, the understanding obligation arrives retroactively, and it may be unpayable if nothing was understood.
  • The cheapest compliant path may be to build PoCs fast with AI and then rewrite (or deeply study) them before shipping.

Narratives

  • The imagined ad-hoc interrogation: the lead pulls an engineer aside at any moment and asks pointed questions about a system they shipped.
    → Makes the standard concrete and gives it a social enforcement mechanism (accountability to a person, not a process); implicitly supports the claim that understanding is verified by oral questioning.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What is a workable, non-bureaucratic standard for responsible AI use in software engineering?
    Propose the whiteboard defense: be able to explain and defend design decisions of shipped customer-facing systems; exempt PoCs.
  • ✅ How much of AI-generated code must the shipper understand?
    High-level design understanding only; no line-level familiarity required.
  • ⭕ How is that understanding checked, by whom, and how often -- and how does the check scale with the volume of AI-generated output?
    Implied: a lead asks on demand; scaling and verifier competence not addressed.
  • ⭕ How does a PoC graduate into customer-facing work without importing zero understanding?
    Not addressed.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe test is oral and on-demand, but the post never says how genuine understanding is distinguished from borrowed or rehearsed understanding (a fluent briefing from an LLM five minutes before the pull-aside would pass), nor who is competent to judge the answers or what happens when the verifier cannot tell. The mechanism of verification -- who verifies and with what evidence -- is the whole enforcement story and it is left implicit.
    Read next: Read alongside the cognitive-surrender and borrowed-confidence material (extract-cognitive-surrender), the comprehension-vs-fluency discussion in extract-fowler-fragments-february-4, and extract-ai-coding-two-kinds-debt for team-level diagnostic questions.
  • incompleteScaling and persistence are not addressed. Defending one system at ship time is feasible; defending every system a person ships across many parallel agent sessions, or remaining able to defend it months later, after turnover or drift, is not. Understanding is treated as an individual, point-in-time property although cognitive debt is described elsewhere as a team- and time-dependent property.
    Read next: extract-osmani-human-judgment-relocates (human bandwidth does not scale with parallel agents), extract-triple-debt-storey (cognitive debt as team-level and partially repairable), extract-ai-coding-two-kinds-debt.
  • incompleteThe PoC/production boundary is asserted as a clean category with no mechanism for noticing when an exempt, fully-not-understood artifact is being promoted into a customer-facing system. This is the same unresolved gap flagged for disposable software elsewhere in the corpus; the post's strict rule is only as strong as the boundary's enforcement.
    Read next: extract-fowler-bliki-vibe-coding (g1: no detection of silent graduation), extract-honeycomb-disposable-vs-durable-code (g1: misclassification failure mode), extract-boonstra-spec-driven-production-grade-development ('vibe-in-production').
  • incomplete'Customer-facing' is a binary proxy for risk. It omits internal systems with high blast radius (infrastructure, data pipelines, security tooling, systems handling employee data) and gives no gradation: how deep must 'high level' go for a low-risk marketing page versus a payments path? No risk tiering links the depth of required understanding to the stakes.
    Read next: extract-fowler-bliki-vibe-coding#p12 (risk scales with lifetime, audience, data sensitivity), extract-fowler-fragments-february-18 (risk tiering as the new core discipline).
  • incompleteNo evidence that design-level defensibility predicts fewer customer-facing failures. Many consequential AI-code failures (injection, race conditions, subtle edge-case bugs, silent corruption) live at line level and pass design-level scrutiny; waiving line-level familiarity leaves the question of what other verification (tests, evals, review) carries that load unanswered. The standard is a complement to verification, not a substitute, but the post does not say so.
    Read next: extract-marcus-code-compiles-passes-tests (robustness gap), extract-new-sdlc-with-vibe-coding (tests plus evals), and the verifiability material in the corpus.
