Strategi som regi i møte med teknologi: En samtale med Walter Qvam

Verdistrategipodden (Tale Skjølsvik interviewing Walter Qvam) · podcast · 2026-08-01 Reader 🖥️

Adaptive Advantage / Adaptive CapacityChange Management & AdoptionDecision-Making PracticesIntrinsic Motivation & MakingLeadership as Directing (Regi)Ownership Type Shapes Strategic HorizonPublic-Sector DigitalizationStrategy & Direction-SettingTechnology Leadership (Heis)
Strategy is best understood not as a detailed analytical plan but as an ambition or aspiration compressed into a believed, narratable direction that a leader — acting as a director/orchestra-conductor rather than a soloist — uses to make an organization's culture willing and able to execute; and because plans now have a shorter shelf life than ever, strategic success increasingly depends on combining proactive bet-making with the honesty and speed to detect weak signals and respond to the unforeseen (robustness), rather than treating proactive and reactive strategy as opposites.

practical · mixed — A dialogic, practical text: host Tale Skjølsvik and guest Walter Qvam (former Kongsberg Gruppen CEO, chair of Sintef/PGS/DNV/mnemonic/DigitalNorway) jointly work toward a shared position on what strategy is and how to execute it, argued through Qvam's career anecdotes (narrative) interleaved with direct claims (argumentative) and a short expository lightning round.

Key terms

  • Regi / regissør (directing / director)
    The leader's core job: engaging the organization, testing ideas against the board, boss or customers, before the organization itself 'plays' — a facilitating, narrative-authoring role rather than a performing one.
  • Robusthet (robustness)
    A strategic goal in its own right, distinct from reaching a target: the organization's built-in capacity to absorb and respond to the unforeseen (Ukraine, AI, deglobalization, energy shocks).
  • Vage signaler (weak/vague signals)
    Early, ambiguous indications that a strategy in motion is heading toward failure; the discipline is detecting them and acting quickly rather than waiting.
  • Ambisjon vs. aspirasjon (ambition vs. aspiration)
    Skjølsvik's distinction, which Qvam adopts: ambition as status-relative comparison to others; aspiration as wanting to achieve something of broader meaning, societal responsibility, or impact.
  • Å bli ferdig / gjennomføring (finishing / execution)
    A near-compulsive personal and organizational value — completing what has been started — that Qvam traces to his farm upbringing and treats as the scarce ingredient in Norwegian strategy generally.
  • Killing your babies Ownership Type Shapes Strategic Horizon
    The (English-language, borrowed) discipline of terminating a strategic bet once its founding assumptions have failed, rather than sustaining it out of attachment or sunk cost — a discipline he says private-equity ownership imposes better than most Norwegian ownership structures.

Propositions

  • explicitOver time, strategy has shifted from being about detailed, prescriptive plans toward being about broad direction, giving organizations more room to maneuver.conf: high
  • explicitStrategy is ultimately about culture, because the organization — not the strategy document — is what implements it, and a culture oriented toward finishing what it starts is what makes a strategy real.conf: high
  • explicitCulture and strategic direction are not opposed forces; framed as 'Siamese twins,' and in some organizations the realized strategy effectively lies in the culture itself.conf: high
  • explicitAmbition, understood as status-relative comparison to others, should be distinguished from aspiration, understood as wanting to achieve something of broader societal meaning or impact.conf: high
  • explicitA company's stated ambition must give genuine meaning to the people who work there, not merely serve shareholder value creation.conf: high
  • explicitStrategic turning points across his career (fall of the Berlin Wall, the 2000 dot-com crash, the oil era, Ukraine, AI, deglobalization, China) have almost always been triggered by unforeseen external events rather than internal foresight.conf: high
  • explicitRegulation and government intervention now shape corporate strategy far more heavily than before.conf: medium
  • explicitStrategy today is about two things simultaneously: proactively developing the business, and building the capacity to respond to and absorb the unforeseen — robustness is now a strategic goal in itself, not just reaching a target.conf: high

  • explicitExternal shocks and regulatory shifts don't eliminate room for proactive strategy — they open an opportunity space ('mulighetsrom') inside which many proactive strategic choices become possible.conf: medium
  • explicitThe advanced organizational capability is not choosing between reactive and proactive strategy but doing both simultaneously — an ambidextrous, 'two-handed' capacity analogous to running operations and development at once.conf: medium

  • explicitBecause strategic plans now have a shorter 'shelf life' than ever, the critical leadership skill is recognizing weak/vague signals that a strategy in motion will not work and acting on them quickly — organizations chronically wait too long before stopping a failing initiative.conf: high

  • explicitOwnership type materially shapes an organization's strategic capacity: short-horizon exit-focused owners effectively have no real strategy; most Norwegian owners outside the state are dispersed and passive and add little industrial competence, unlike hands-on Swedish industrial owner families.conf: high

  • explicitDurable, industry-wide organizational change happens through one of two mechanisms: a single actor becoming dominant enough to unilaterally set the standard (e.g., the auto industry over its suppliers), or a coalition of actors voluntarily agreeing to act together (a 'dugnad') — the latter is harder but more rewarding.conf: high
  • explicitHis most successful strategy process, Kongsberg's Remote Weapon Station, began as an unplanned reaction to an unrelated product need and an unforeseen external opportunity, and was converted into a strategy by carving out the small team, giving it a broad mandate and dedicated staff.conf: high
  • explicitCompanies must choose fairly early and explicitly among product leadership, cost leadership, and customer-intimacy strategies, because pursuing all three at once does not work and each implies a genuinely different earnings/delivery model.conf: high
  • explicitThe Nordic/Scandinavian model's comparative strength in strategy execution is an unusually high degree of organizational anchoring and involvement — an informal, flat, trust-based way of working that other countries envy.conf: high
  • explicitThe Nordics/Norway have been strong at adopting and applying digital technology (mobile telephony, the post-dot-com wave, companies like Spotify) but lack the investor base and risk appetite to make AI/data-center-scale investment bets comparable to the US and China.conf: high
  • explicitIt is premature to conclude Norway/Europe has already 'lost' the AI race, given the history of repeated AI winters and the size of the current AI investment bubble, though he remains more worried about Norway's decision-making capacity than about the technology race itself.conf: high
  • explicitNorway's realistic AI opportunity is not building unique foundation models but applying and adopting AI distinctively where it holds genuine data or domain advantages (e.g., Arctic-conditions data for the Armed Forces) and, most ambitiously, leading in AI adoption inside an already highly digitized public sector.conf: high
  • explicitNorway/'AS Norge' is held back less by a lack of underlying knowledge — many commissions and inquiries have already produced recommendations — than by an inability to follow through on hard, contested decisions (e.g., land-based wind power); execution, not analysis, is the scarce resource.conf: high
  • explicitGiven the chance, he would convene Norway's prime minister and the leaders of the two other largest parties (Jonas, Ine, Sylvi) in a single, non-partisan strategy workshop to address a shared national lack of direction and decisiveness.conf: high
  • explicitPolitical leaders may be structurally prone to the same difficulty other groups face in holding long-term strategic thinking, because the constant work of case-by-case political compromise erodes the capacity to commit to and sustain long lines of direction.conf: medium

Arguments

Culture and strategy should not be treated as opposing forces (rejecting the 'culture eats strategy for breakfast' framing).
Explicit premises:
  • Strategy must be implemented by the organization, and culture determines whether the organization follows through.
  • In some organizations (e.g. Kongsberg) culture itself functions as a strategic driving force, shaped by centuries of operating 'with its back to the wall.'
Implicit premises:
  • If two things can reinforce each other in the best case, treating them as inherently opposed is a category error.
  • Drucker's aphorism is really targeting weak or undifferentiated strategy, not strategy as such.
Inferences:
  • An organization's culture can itself be cultivated and assessed as a strategic asset, not merely as an implementation risk to a separately designed strategy.
Proactive vs. reactive strategy is a largely false dichotomy. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Many of the biggest strategic successes (e.g., the iPhone) originated as reactions to external opportunities or technology shifts.
  • External shocks and regulatory change open an opportunity space ('mulighetsrom') inside which many proactive strategies then become possible.
Implicit premises:
  • A strategy's reactive origin does not determine how much proactive skill was involved in exploiting it.
  • Pure proactivity (inventing a 'Blue Ocean' opportunity nobody else has seen) is possible but rare enough that it shouldn't anchor the theoretical framework.
Inferences:
  • The valuable leadership capability may be an ambidextrous one — sensing external shifts while placing bets ahead of certainty — rather than optimizing for either pole.
Private-equity-style ownership improves an organization's strategic discipline relative to most other Norwegian ownership structures.
Explicit premises:
  • PE investors have a bounded time horizon (often under 10 years) and an explicit mandate to develop a portfolio company within it.
  • Because of that horizon and mandate, PE owners recognize and act on failed strategic assumptions faster.
  • Most Norwegian owners outside the state are dispersed, passive, 'anonymized' investors who add no industrial competence to strategic leadership.
Implicit premises:
  • Urgency imposed by an external time constraint reliably improves strategic decision quality rather than, e.g., inducing short-termism or premature abandonment.
  • The implied comparison set (typical Norwegian dispersed-ownership firms, the Swedish industrial-owner model) is representative of the relevant alternatives.
Inferences:
  • If ownership structure this strongly determines strategic discipline, improving Norwegian strategy execution may be more a governance/ownership-design problem than a leadership-skill problem.

Narratives

  • Growing up on a farm in Trøndelag, driving a tractor at age six, and learning that once you start turning the turnip rows you cannot stop halfway — you have to finish.
    → Origin story for his execution-orientation ('å bli ferdig'), grounding proposition p3 (culture-as-execution) in personal disposition rather than abstract theory; he explicitly says this trait has 'become more and more important, maybe past its peak.'
  • His German mother — one of the first German women to receive Norwegian citizenship after WWII — married his father, a decorated colonel, in Steinkjer, a town flattened by German bombing; as a child he sensed adult-level tension around this without fully understanding it.
    → Frames his early, formative exposure to disruptive social change and tension, which he links to his comfort 'working with change' throughout his career.
  • Building a roughly half-meter scale model of the ship Fram — deliberately trying to prolong the process rather than finish it quickly, a late-career reversal of his lifelong drive to complete things fast.
    → Complicates propositions p3/p17: shows he is deliberately unlearning part of his own execution instinct, treating being 'in the process' as a reward in itself rather than only a means to a finished result.
  • The founding of Digital Norway (2014–2017): starting as an informal 'rådslag' (council) Qvam convened as Kongsberg CEO with LO, NHO, business leaders and researchers at a time when 'digitalization' wasn't on any Norwegian boardroom agenda, growing via a small-coalition 'dugnad' into a lasting national network hub.
    → Supports p26 (coalition-building as a mechanism for durable industry-wide change) and is offered as his most enjoyable strategy process, precisely because it started 'from nothing.'
  • Kongsberg's Remote Weapon Station: an unrelated mine-clearing product pivoted, after the US Army needed to protect exposed vehicle-turret gunners, into a remotely operated weapon station, spun out as an empowered, resourced team, and grown into a large ongoing Kongsberg business.
    → Supports p27 and Argument 2 (reactive origin, proactively exploited via mandate and dedicated resources); offered as his most successful strategy process.
  • Nokia's decision to become a mobile-phone company, described (secondhand) as involving internal 'blodbad' (bloodbath) over what legacy business to shut down to free capacity for the new area.
    → Illustrates that healthy, accepted friction — not harmony — is required for hard strategic decisions, supporting p20/p21 and Qvam's claim that Nordic harmony-preference is a strategic liability.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Is strategy today more about direction and culture, or about detailed planning?
    About direction, culture, and execution capacity rather than detailed plans.
  • ⭕ Can strategy be genuinely proactive, or is it always fundamentally reactive?
    Qvam argues the dichotomy is largely false and gestures at an ambidextrous alternative, but the conversation explicitly leaves the replacement framework unfinished ('kanskje det er noe som kommer ut av dette til slutt').
  • ✅ What role can or should a consultant play in a strategy or execution process?
    Orchestra member, not soloist or leader — though which 'instrument' remains unspecified.
  • ⭕ Has Norway/Europe already lost the AI race to the US and China?
    Explicitly left open as premature to judge, given past AI winters and the current bubble.
  • ✅ What differentiates organizations that execute strategy well from those that don't?
    A convergent answer across the conversation: people genuinely wanting to do it (motivation/culture), timely honesty about failing bets (weak-signal response), and ownership pressure — though their relative weight is never ranked.

Gaps

  • illogicalQvam argues culture and strategy are 'Siamese twins,' not opposed — yet his own land-based-wind-power example, where legitimate local/cultural opposition has blocked an outcome he himself calls rationally necessary, is a case where culture (in the broad sense of stakeholder values) directly defeats strategic execution. He never reconciles the tension between 'culture and strategy align when done right' and his own clearest example of when they don't.
    Read next: Cases of legitimate, non-pathological stakeholder resistance defeating a leadership-endorsed strategy (e.g., siting/NIMBY literature, or corpus material on change management's limits).
  • incompleteThe Digital Norway and Kongsberg Remote Weapon Station stories are told entirely from Qvam's own vantage point as the architect/CEO who initiated them, with no counterfactual (a comparable Norwegian coalition or product pivot that failed) and no adversarial pressure from the host — the same hagiography-risk pattern already flagged elsewhere in this corpus for founder/leader-narrated case studies.
    Read next: extract-heis-01-torbjorn-larsen-naermeste-vi-kommer-steve-jobs#g9 (same pattern, same corpus) and independent accounts of Digital Norway's or Kongsberg's development.
  • incompleteQvam repeatedly asserts that ownership structure (state, dispersed retail investors, private equity, Swedish industrial families) strongly shapes strategic discipline and calls PE 'an inspiration for everyone,' but this rests entirely on his own anecdotal board experience — no comparative data across ownership types, and no engagement with private equity's well-documented downside risks (leverage, extraction, short-termism).
    Read next: extract-tim-oreilly-tidy-house#p13 (PE capturing AI efficiency gains as profit rather than reinvestment) and broader private-equity outcomes literature.
  • incompleteQvam names 'gruppetenkning' (groupthink) and Norwegian/Nordic conflict-avoidance as a specific failure mode in strategic decision-making, echoing this corpus's open question about whether the 'boss states the answer, team goes silent' dynamic is Norwegian-culture-specific — but neither conversation actually tests the claim against a non-Nordic comparison case; it remains an assumed cultural stereotype corroborated only by another Norwegian speaker's similar assumption.
    Read next: extract-heis-12-greger-teigre-wedel-naar-sjefen-har-svaret#g1 (parallel open gap); Hofstede/Trompenaars on power distance in Nordic vs. other workplaces.
  • incompleteThe conversation never resolves who or what is actually doing the strategic work: is organizational success driven by the individual leader's skill as 'regissør' (director), or by structural conditions (ownership type, regulation, external shocks) that Qvam names as equally decisive elsewhere in the same conversation? Both framings are asserted side by side with no account of how they interact or which dominates when they conflict — arguably the conversation's central unexamined premise.
    Read next: Structure-vs-agency literature in leadership/strategy studies; corpus material on ownership and governance as strategic determinants.
