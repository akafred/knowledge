Leaders Must Speak Honestly About Aging at Work

Christina Wing · article · 2026-10-20 Reader 🖥️

Career DevelopmentGenerativity (Erikson's Life-Stage Sense)Niceness Culture (Comfort Over Candor)Professional Identity
Organizations are harmed when leaders avoid candid conversation about aging, succession and retirement, so leaders should hold ongoing, performance- and need-based (not birthday-based) conversations that acknowledge identity and purpose and frame stepping aside as a responsibility to the institution and its next generation.

practical · mixed — Adapted excerpt from the author's book Unspeakable. Argues for a way of talking about age, retirement and succession using anecdotes (a dentist, Biden, two family-business cases) to motivate four principles.

Structure

PartRole

Key terms

  • succession planning (for every role)
    A plan for who might replace anyone, at any level and age, kept alive through regular conversations about growth, readiness and how long someone will be happy in a job, not a CEO-only event.
  • separating succession from age
    Discussing role requirements, performance, development and organizational needs, not birthdays, tenure or retirement ages, while still using a forward-looking question ('where do you see yourself in five years?') that works for any age.
  • identity and purpose
    What a leader fears losing by stepping aside (meaning, relevance, socialization); the author says you cannot discuss when to retire without discussing how they will spend their days and what will engage them.
  • a life like a Broadway play with several acts
    A career model in which people plan for multiple acts instead of one linear arc ending in retirement.
  • leaders-in-waiting
    Ready, capable successors (e.g. the cofounder's adult children) held in a prolonged apprentice role, with diminished standing, by an incumbent who will not leave, like Prince Charles for decades.

Arguments

Leaders and organizations should talk openly and early about age, succession and retirement. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Silence about aging produced costly outcomes (dentist, Biden, the cofounder).
  • Norms and manners suppress the conversation.
  • People are living and working longer, raising the stakes.
  • Self-aware leaders who initiate transitions (Rohit Gera) have thriving organizations.
Implicit premises:
  • The cases chosen are representative, and openness would have changed the outcomes.
  • The incumbent can be moved by conversation, not just by governance or incentives.
  • Candid talk about age does not itself create legal exposure.
Inferences:
  • Governance mechanisms such as term limits, mandatory ages and independent boards could achieve what conversation does not.
  • Organizations without periodic role-fit reviews will rely on informal courage to raise decline.
Succession conversations should be performance- and need-based, not age-based. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Some professions and organizations have mandatory ages, but most do not.
  • A forward-looking question works for any age and any role.
  • The right time depends on the leader, successors and organization.
Implicit premises:
  • Age-based talk is less effective, or legally riskier, than role-based talk.
  • Performance decline linked to aging can be addressed through role requirements alone.
Inferences:
  • The principle is in tension with her own opening cases, in which age-related decline was the issue and was unspoken.
  • Where decline is medical (tremor), role-based talk may need explicit health or competence assessment.

Narratives

  • The author's dentist of 15 years, trembling and shuffling at about 75, whose son left the practice, patients fled, staff lost jobs and the practice collapsed before his death.
    → Opens with the human and organizational cost of failing to have the conversation.
  • Democratic Party reluctance to discuss Joe Biden's decline in 2024.
    → Public-scale example that silence about age is costly for organizations.
  • A European IT cofounder in his 90s, CEO until 65, chair thereafter with a supporter-filled board that keeps extending his term by five years; he rides a motorcycle to work and says he has no idea what he'd do otherwise.
    → Embodies identity-driven refusal to step aside and its costs to management and family.
  • Gera Developments: Pritamdas Gera to son Kumar, Kumar's power-share with his son Rohit, and Rohit's 2020 decision to hand off to a non-family professional while his child is groomed.
    → Counter-example of leaders who initiate transition, supporting principles 3 and 4.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do organizations fail to have timely, honest conversations about age, succession and retirement?
    Manners and employment-law caution, identity and purpose attached to the role, and a CEO-only view of succession.
  • ✅ How should leaders talk about it without making it about birthdays?
    Four principles: separate from age, ongoing dialogue, address identity and purpose, frame as responsibility to the next generation.
  • ⭕ How should an organization act when a leader's decline is age-related and the leader refuses the conversation?
    The opening dentist case poses it but the principles only advise earlier, gentler conversation.
  • ⭕ What legal limits apply to raising age-linked performance concerns?
    Employment law is named as a reason for avoidance but never examined.

Gaps

  • illogicalPrinciple 1 says to keep succession talk about role and performance, not age, yet the two motivating cases (the dentist's tremor, Biden) are age-related decline that outsiders could see. The piece never says how to raise a decline that is age-linked without making it about age, nor how to do so within the employment law it cites as the cause of silence.

  • incompleteEvidence is two family-firm case studies, a dentist, Biden, and the author's advisory practice. There is no data on whether earlier succession or candor conversations improve outcomes, nor comparison with firms where long-tenured leaders stayed and thrived.
    Read next: CEO-tenure and performance research; succession-timing studies in family and founder-led firms.
  • incompleteThe obstacle is framed as the incumbent's identity, but the cofounder case also shows structural power: a supportive board, extension votes and control rights. The remedy offered is a conversation, with no governance fix (term limits, independent directors, mandatory ages) examined, even though the author cites mandatory ages for pilots and the NYT editor as working models.
    Read next: Board governance and term-limit research for founders; mandatory retirement debates.
  • incomplete'Treat the readiness of the next generation as a factor' and 'move on so the next generation can develop' can be used as a rationale to push out older workers, but the piece does not address the dual-use risk or the interests of employees who want and need to keep working longer, given the longevity trend it cites.
