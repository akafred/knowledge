When Restrictive Cybersecurity Policies Backfire

Aaron Kay, Russell Spitler, Peter Jin · article · 2026-10-21 Reader 🖥️

Change Management & AdoptionEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessFraming Effects (Opportunity vs. Sacrifice)Proxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)Psychological Reactance in Policy DesignShadow AI & AI Use Policy
Security policies that strip employees of autonomy, or that are later reversed without explanation, erode employees' belief that cyber threats are serious, so leaders must design and communicate policy (choice inside the guardrails, threat explained first, the exit planned, beliefs measured) to protect that belief and not only to reduce risk.

practical · argumentative — Reports a six-experiment research program (>2,800 participants) to argue for how leaders should design, roll out and retire security policies. The empirical claims are in service of five prescriptive principles.

Structure

PartRole
Opening: generative-AI tools are so useful that bans push use underground (KPMG/Melbourne: 57% hid AI use, 48% uploaded company data; Verizon DBIR: shadow-AI detections quadrupled; Spitler's deployments find several times more AI tools than security teams believed). Leaders read this as carelessness and reach for more restriction.context / problem statement
Mechanism claim: employees interpret policy, and compliance follows perceived threat severity; restrictive policy can lower that perception via (1) reactance and solution aversion when imposed, (2) inference that the risk was exaggerated when lifted.thesis
Studies 1-3 (imposition): firewall vs education policy; mandatory vs chosen training; mandate with vs without choice of how to comply. Same threat, same content, different autonomy, different reactance and threat denial.evidence
Studies 4-6 (reversal): recalled lifted vs kept policies; imagined lifted generative-AI ban (Samsung as real-world frame); lifting the ban while reaffirming residual risk largely neutralizes the effect.evidence
Five principles: choice inside guardrails; make the secure path less aversive; explain the threat before the control; plan the exit before the ban; measure beliefs as well as compliance.conclusion / prescription
Closing: walls cannot keep pace with AI tool discovery, so the goal is a security culture where employees authentically recognize risk and want to cooperate.conclusion

Key terms

  • psychological reactance Psychological Reactance in Policy Design
    A measurable emotional response (feeling bothered, threatened, anxious) triggered when employees feel the organization is overstepping control and taking a valued freedom away.
  • solution aversion Psychological Reactance in Policy Design
    When people dislike the proposed solution to a problem, they become more likely to deny that the problem is serious; applied here so that disliked security controls lead employees to judge the risk exaggerated.
  • choice inside the guardrails Psychological Reactance in Policy Design
    The organization fixes the safety perimeter (what must be done) while employees keep agency over how, when and with which approved option they comply.
  • psychological residue of reversal Psychological Reactance in Policy Design
    The lasting inference employees draw when a restriction is lifted: 'they said it was dangerous, now they allow it, so it never was.'
  • shadow AI Shadow AI & AI Use Policy
    Employees' less visible use of AI tools that the organization has banned or has not approved, including uploading company data to public tools.
  • security beliefs (vs. compliance)
    Whether employees believe threats are serious, understand why tools are restricted, know the alternatives, and see security as a partner; proposed as a metric alongside incidents, completion rates and violations.

Propositions

  • explicitEmployees are not passive recipients of security policy: they interpret it and decide whether to comply or quietly work around it, largely according to how real and serious they believe the threat is.conf: high
  • explicitCommon restrictive security policies can erode employees' belief that cyber threats are as grave as claimed, and so worsen the very disregard of severity that undermines security.conf: high
  • explicitWhen a policy is imposed in a way that feels like organizational overreach, employees feel psychological reactance and then downplay how serious the threat is; the same mandatory policy delivered with autonomy-preserving language reduces both.conf: high
  • explicitSolution aversion explains the effect: disliking the proposed solution makes people more likely to deny that the problem is real or serious.conf: high
  • explicitStudy 1: given an identical threat description, participants shown an automatic-block firewall policy reported more reactance and more denial of the threat and its evidence than those shown an employee-education policy.conf: high
  • explicitStudy 2: mandatory cybersecurity training produced more reactance and threat denial than the identical training chosen from a menu of quarterly programs.conf: high
  • explicitStudy 3: holding the mandate constant, giving employees choice over how (format, timing, in-person/video/written) they complete required training reduced reactance and, through it, threat denial, so autonomy and non-negotiable requirements are not a forced tradeoff.conf: high
  • explicitWhen an organization lifts or reverses a security policy, employees infer the underlying risk was never as serious as claimed and lower their concern about cyber threats generally (studies 4 and 5, including a generative-AI ban scenario).conf: high
  • explicitExplicitly reaffirming that the risk persists, and giving the real reason for lifting (e.g. efficiency gains, controlled access), largely prevents the reversal inference (study 6).conf: high
  • explicitBans on useful AI tools push employee use out of sight rather than stopping it; employees' hidden use is a symptom of tool usefulness and policy friction, not mere carelessness.conf: medium
    supportsCo-Intelligence, by Ethan Mollick: a Summary · p4 — Quantifies covert, unsanctioned AI use (KPMG 57% hid use) and identifies policy friction as a driver.
  • explicitEmployees use unapproved tools mainly because approved ones are slower, weaker, harder to access or poorly matched to the work, so leaders should treat frustration as security-relevant data and build genuinely useful approved alternatives.conf: medium
    supportsGoverning AI in the Cloud: A Practical Guide for Architects · p10 — Both argue that if the secure path is harder or weaker than the insecure one, people route around it into shadow tools; Jin et al. add the belief-erosion mechanism.
    refinesBuilding AI Agents For The Enterprise · p12 — Admin-controlled approved marketplaces cure shadow AI only if the approved tools are genuinely as useful; restricting people to weaker safe tools pushes shadow use further out of view.
  • explicitLeaders should explain the threat, show how it applies to employees' own work, acknowledge the value of the restricted behavior and offer safe alternatives before introducing the control, rather than announcing the restriction first.conf: medium
    supportsAchieving Compliance as a Platform Engineering Team by Helping Developers · p7 — Both hold that how a policy is introduced (communicating the why, transparency) determines whether it works, not just its technical content.
  • explicitOrganizations should plan the exit before imposing a ban: say a ban is temporary, state what would change it, and when lifting it be clear why and where risk remains.conf: high
  • explicitSecurity programs should measure employees' beliefs about threat seriousness and policy legitimacy in addition to incidents, completion rates, access logs and violations, because those standard metrics miss a crucial psychological variable.conf: high
  • implicitEmployees' beliefs about threat severity are causally upstream of their compliance behavior, so shifts in stated belief in the experiments translate into riskier real-world conduct.conf: medium
  • implicitEmployees who downgrade a threat after a policy is lifted or imposed clumsily are making a mistake, and never a calibrated update; the true threat level is taken as fixed and known to leadership.conf: medium

Arguments

Restrictive or imposed security policies can backfire by lowering employees' perception of threat severity. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Compliance decisions are driven largely by how serious employees believe the threat is.
  • Imposed controls that feel like overreach produce reactance (studies 1-3).
  • Reactance leads people to deny the problem's seriousness through solution aversion.
  • Giving choice within the mandate lowers reactance and the denial (study 3).
Implicit premises:
  • Stated belief and reactance in imagined or recalled scenarios predict real workplace behavior.
  • Lower threat belief produces less careful security behavior.
  • The threat is in fact as serious as leaders say, so denial is error rather than accurate skepticism.
Inferences:
  • A blocking control that is perfectly effective technically could still be net-negative if it lowers vigilance on every control it does not cover.
  • The same logic should apply to AI agents or tools governed by blanket prohibition.
Lifting a restriction without explanation weakens belief in the original threat, but reaffirming residual risk mitigates the effect. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Employees who recalled lifted policies denied threats more (study 4).
  • Participants in a lifted-ban scenario judged the original risk overstated vs kept-ban or no-ban conditions (study 5).
  • Adding an explanation that lifts the ban for efficiency while stressing continuing risk reduced the inference (study 6).
Implicit premises:
  • Employees treat policy as a signal of the organization's own assessment of risk.
  • A stated reaffirmation is credible to employees and does not itself read as boilerplate.
Inferences:
  • Every policy should ship with a documented sunset or revision condition, so reversal is anticipated.
  • Frequent policy churn (reverse, restrict again) could compound the credibility loss.
Leaders should preserve choice, make secure options useful, explain before imposing, plan exits and measure beliefs. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Reactance is greatest when a valued freedom is removed.
  • Employees use unapproved tools because approved ones are inferior.
  • Standard security metrics omit beliefs.
Implicit premises:
  • Perimeter-based control cannot work because AI tool discovery outpaces approval.
  • Choice offered inside the guardrails is substantive rather than cosmetic and employees will perceive it so.
Inferences:
  • Security teams need product and UX capability to make the secure path attractive.
  • A 'belief' dashboard for security would need an instrument (survey) with its own validity problems.

Narratives

  • Samsung banned ChatGPT-style tools in 2023 after employees uploaded sensitive code, then reportedly moved in 2026 to allow outside models with training and controlled access.
    → Real-world frame for the reversal problem: a sensible security evolution that employees may read as 'the risk was exaggerated'.
  • Russell Spitler's company finds, in nearly every deployment, several times more distinct AI tools in use than the security team believed.
    → Practitioner evidence that restriction does not stop usage and that ban-first posture produces blindness.
  • Study scenarios: employees reading about a firewall vs an education policy; a boss demanding vs offering a choice of training; a company banning, lifting, or never having a generative-AI ban.
    → Make the experimental manipulations concrete and show the effect arises with identical threats and content.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do employees disregard cyber threats and work around security policy even as AI tools proliferate?
    Policy style and reversal themselves reduce perceived threat severity (reactance/solution aversion; reversal inference).
  • ✅ How can leaders impose non-negotiable requirements without triggering reactance?
    Fix the requirement, vary the means: give choice in how, when and format of compliance (study 3).
  • ✅ How should organizations move from blanket AI bans to nuanced governance without signaling that risk was overstated?
    Plan the exit in advance and explicitly reaffirm residual risk when lifting.
  • ⭕ Do attitudes shifted by these manipulations change actual security behavior or incident rates?
    Not addressed; outcomes are self-reported belief and reactance.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe evidence is attitudinal and mostly vignette-based (participants read, imagined or recalled policies). No study reports actual compliance, tool use, or incident outcomes, so the central chain policy to reactance to threat denial to riskier behavior is only demonstrated up to its second link.
    Read next: Field or behavioral research on security-policy compliance (protection motivation theory; telemetry-backed studies of workaround behavior such as Verizon DBIR shadow-AI data); the SSRN working paper's own limitations section.
  • incompleteThree of the five principles (make the secure path less aversive, explain the threat before the control, measure beliefs) are presented alongside the experiments but are not tested by them. The explain-first sequencing claim in particular has no described condition comparing orders.
    Read next: Experimental work on message sequencing and autonomy-supportive communication in compliance settings; security-awareness training effectiveness meta-analyses.
  • incompleteNo account of when the reactance cost outweighs the risk-reduction benefit. The authors insist some systems must be locked down, yet give no criterion for which controls should stay hard (e.g. pasting source code into public models, as in Samsung's actual leak) versus which should be offered with choice.
    Read next: Risk-tiering of controls (cf. risk-tiering and guardrail design); cost-benefit work on usable security.
  • illogicalEmployee updating on a lifted ban is treated as an error to be short-circuited, but the authors themselves note the reversal is often legitimate because the risk profile changed (enterprise tools replaced public ones). Whether the inference is a bias or a rational response to costly signaling by the organization is not examined.
    Read next: Costly-signaling and credibility literature on organizational communication; trust repair after policy reversals.
