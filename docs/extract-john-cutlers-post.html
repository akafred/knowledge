Key terms

  • Learning faster than the hype
    Building a team/organizational learning velocity — real signal from customers, systems, and colleagues — that outpaces the churn of AI narratives, vendor claims, and 'advice firehose', so that adoption is grounded rather than reactive.
  • Social contract
    The trust relationship between the people running an AI experiment and the organization around them — psychological safety plus real stakes — without which 'experimentation' collapses into performing compliance rather than genuinely testing anything.
  • Just... / But... / Can
    Shorthand for the two unproductive default postures in AI debates — advocates who say 'just use AI / just adopt it' and skeptics who counter 'but what about quality/context/people' — reframed into the productive question 'what CAN we do?'
  • Three Doors
    The three legitimate entry points into an AI initiative — machine-first ('AI can do X'), problem-first ('we have a problem'), practice-first ('teams are doing Z') — each legitimate but each carrying its own blind-spot questions the initiative must still answer.
  • Strategic clarity
    Not pretending to have all the answers; a grounded thesis about what matters, what remains uncertain, and how you will learn — distinct from confident-sounding certainty.

Arguments

Sustainable competitive advantage requires investing in a socially-trusted, hypothesis-driven learning practice, not simply adopting AI tools faster than competitors.
Explicit premises:
  • Everyone has access to the same AI technology (p2's premise).
  • The remaining differentiators are judgement, execution, customer understanding, trust, and the ability to learn and adapt (p2).
  • Strategic clarity — not certainty, but a grounded thesis plus a plan to learn — beats FOMO-driven high-pressure activity (p4, p5).
  • Most current AI practices are still emergent and unstable, not established best practice (p6).
Implicit premises:
  • Technology alone does not determine the outcome — the same tool produces very different results depending on the wielder and context (p3, the sledgehammer sketch).
  • An organization's practice and trust level mediate whether AI helps or hurts, as shown across all three case stories (p8, p14).
Inferences:
  • Organizations should therefore budget at least as much attention to developing shared practice, judgement, and trust as to acquiring AI capability — a conclusion the deck implies via its case stories and success formula but never states as directly as this.
Honest experimentation with AI requires a genuine social contract; without it, results devolve into compliance theater.
Explicit premises:
  • 'The social contract is not a nice-to-have. It is the precondition for honest experimentation. Without it, you get compliance theater.' (p10)
  • The success formula places Social Contract in the denominator, so a weak or broken social contract degrades the whole calculation regardless of how strong the other three factors are (p9).
Implicit premises:
  • People will not surface true negative results, dissent, or practice failures to a system that punishes honesty about them.
  • Absent trust, teams will over-report success and under-report the friction and rework the case stories describe (echoed in the third story: 'real alignment is expensive').
Inferences:
  • Leaders should treat trust-building as a prerequisite investment made before scaling an AI practice change, not an afterthought bolted on after tools are already deployed.

Narratives

  • 'Escaping the AI build trap': a team used AI to produce more polished PRDs, better handoffs, and 2x+ PR throughput, and celebrated the speedup — but noticed less user interaction, conflated feedback, 'too much change,' and less learning signal. They pivoted to throwaway prototypes, involving engineers earlier, using AI to boost signal (not just speed), and having engineers rebuild the validated prototype. Outcome: fewer items shipped, but higher signal, more leverage, and more impact; engineers trusted to ship fewer, better bets.
    → Supports p8 and p14 — shows AI amplifying a broken build-first practice until the underlying practice itself changed toward customer contact and validated learning.
  • 'Green but not green': a team used AI for rapid code migration — a sophisticated pipeline, loop-until-green, file-by-file translation, done in a fraction of the time — but discovered low institutional knowledge transfer, more production bugs, and harder debugging, because 'code comprehension is not system comprehension.' They pivoted to focusing on intent, domain slicing, a portfolio approach, and a human-in-the-loop validation layer. Outcome: slower at first, no major regressions, engineers felt ownership, and the documentation produced became a lasting asset.
    → Supports p11 (AI pattern-matches surface syntax well but not semantic/system intent) and p6 (current AI practice around migration is still immature).
  • 'From 7/10 to 10/10': a team used AI as an OKR writer and 'cascader' — output looked coherent and leadership felt aligned, but the OKRs actually scored 7/10 despite the polish, conflicted with each other, and had 'no edge, all polish,' because real alignment is expensive. They pivoted to using AI-generated drafts only as the start of a conversation, requiring people to articulate priorities in their own words, and adding a back-briefing step. Outcome: the offsite took longer, but produced fewer and sharper OKRs, less auto-cascading, and less priority churn.
    → Supports p9 and p10 — shows that speeding up an artifact (the OKR document) with AI does not substitute for the social, effortful work (the social contract) that produces real alignment.
