'AI' is Not a Tool

Rich Hickey · article · 2026-10-22 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI Employee vs Tool FramingCentaur vs. Reverse CentaurCognitive SurrenderDesirable Difficulty & Productive StruggleAgencyIronies of AutomationNon-Deterministic SoftwareWielded Tool vs Automation
LLMs are not tools, because a tool is something you wield with continuous control, feedback and intention, whereas an LLM is automation that acts on its own after you make a request, so using one means abdicating the work and the learning that wielding a tool would give you.

theoretical · argumentative — A short LinkedIn essay (the author calls it a quip made longer) that argues by definition and contrast: it fixes a discriminating meaning of 'tool' (something you wield), then sorts LLMs into the other category (automation). Its practical sting (do not abdicate reading and coding; do not take 'just a tool' as reassurance) is a consequence of the classification, not the main claim. The captured content is complete: it runs from the opening aside to the closing 'Rich' signature, with no truncation. No published date is present.

Key terms

  • tool AI Employee vs Tool Framing
    An implement you wield: something held and directed continuously with power, control, intention and sensory feedback; you do something with it, it does not do things for you. Deliberately narrower than 'useful'.
  • wield
    To use as a tool 'with full command or power': handle, manipulate, control. Unpacked into three properties: hand-held (control plus feedback plus intimacy), power (force and direction), and control (intention and continuous directing of events).
  • automation
    A device or process 'working by itself with little or no direct human control'. LLMs belong here: you issue requests and it acts on its own, without your energy or continuous direction.
  • useful
    A broader property than tool: shirt pockets and drive-thru windows are useful but not tools. Utility does not make something a tool.
  • abdicate
    To hand over the doing (reading, writing code) to automation, forfeiting the learning that the doing would have produced.
  • voodoo.md / incantations
    Instruction files and prompt rituals aimed at placating an opaque automaton; knowledge about an automaton's current quirks rather than about the world.
  • tool (the titans' sense) AI Employee vs Tool Framing
    'Anyone/thing that can be exploited': the way AI titans regard VCs, politicians, CEOs, software developers and rural towns. Reserved by Hickey for them.

Arguments

LLMs are not tools; they are automation.
Explicit premises:
  • A tool is something you wield: hand-held, powered, under continuous intentional control with sensory feedback.
  • LLM use lacks control, feedback, intention, intimacy and continuous direction.
  • You make a request and the LLM acts on its own; this request/automation cycle is not wielding.
  • Automation is a device or process working by itself with little or no direct human control.
Implicit premises:
  • The wield-based definition is the right one to adopt, not merely one stipulation among several, and it tracks a real and relevant distinction.
  • All LLM use has the request-then-autonomous-action shape, with no meaningful continuous steering available.
  • Feedback delivered after the fact (inspecting the result of a request) is not the sensory, continuous feedback that wielding requires.
  • A category word should be reserved for the cases that share the property that explains its value.
Inferences:
  • Agentic and long-running loops are even further from wielding than chat-style requests.
  • Interfaces that restore tight, continuous steering (autocomplete, small-step edits with immediate review) would sit closer to tool use by this definition.
  • Deterministic automation (a compiler, a build system) is also not a tool by this definition, which would make the category claim broader than the anti-LLM thrust suggests.
Using an LLM in place of reading or coding is costly because it forfeits learning and leaves you with perishable knowledge. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Expertise comes from continuous application of technique and sensory feedback.
  • Delegating reading or writing to automation removes that application.
  • What you learn from automation is the details of the automaton, which a software update can erase.
Implicit premises:
  • Learning to do the task is a primary purpose of doing it, not an incidental by-product.
  • Reviewing or steering generated output does not teach the same things as producing it.
  • The abdicated tasks are ones where the practitioner's own growth is still needed, not routine work properly handed off.
Inferences:
  • Heavy delegation hollows out the pipeline that produces experts able to judge the automation's output.
  • Evaluating whether a given delegation is acceptable should ask what the person stops learning, not only whether the output is good.
  • The risk compounds across a career: each abdication removes the practice that would make the next judgment possible.
'AI is just a tool' is a false reassurance. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • 'Just tools' means 'they are what you make of them'.
  • Automation has intrinsic properties and differences from tools.
  • Tools do not get misaligned, go rogue, escape or conspire.
Implicit premises:
  • Outcomes of a wielded implement are determined mainly by the wielder, whereas automation contributes its own dynamics.
  • Neutrality (instrumentalism) is only plausible for things under continuous human control.
  • Responsibility language and risk language attach differently to tools and to automation.
Inferences:
  • Safety and alignment concerns are category-appropriate for LLMs precisely because they are automation.
  • Invoking 'it is just a tool' can deflect attention from properties the user has no power over.
  • The same logic cuts against 'AI as colleague': an automaton is neither a wielded implement nor a peer.

Narratives

  • The drive-thru: a drive-thru window is automation for avoiding cooking, not a tool for cooking.
    → Anchors the tool-versus-automation distinction in an everyday case and supports the claim that LLM coding is abdication.
  • Ordering a custom burger with no onions in order to get a freshly made one, and receiving a soggy cold burger with the onions removed.
    → Illustrates gaming a capricious automaton and how what you learned about its quirks goes stale or backfires.
  • Filling voodoo.md with just the right incantations.
    → Shows that agent-instruction files are ritual appeasement of an opaque system, knowledge of the automaton rather than of the world.
  • Shirt pockets, kept for reading glasses: useful but not tools.
    → A minimal counterexample to 'tool means anything useful'.
  • The author who says 'the LLM didn't write my paper, I just used it as a tool to read and summarize research papers', contrasted with wielding a Kindle to read them.
    → Rebuts the 'partial use is still tool use' defence by showing that the reading was done by the machine, not the person.
  • The author as lifelong tool builder and user.
    → Grounds the claim to know what tools are in craft experience (an appeal to authority from practice).

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Does 'tool' name anything distinct from 'something useful', and if so what?
    Yes: a tool is something you wield, with hand-held control, power and continuous directed intention, and it does not do things for you.
  • ✅ Are LLMs tools?
    No; they are automation that acts on its own after a request, so use is abdication, not wielding.
  • ✅ What does a person lose by delegating reading or coding to an LLM?
    Learning: how to write, argue, notice things, build trust in authors, and think; and the knowledge gained is about a perishable automaton rather than the world.
  • ✅ Is 'it is just a tool' a safe way to think about AI?
    No; automation has intrinsic properties that the 'what you make of it' reading hides.
  • ⭕ When, if ever, is it right to abdicate a task to automation?
    Not addressed; the drive-thru implies abdication is at least sometimes legitimate, but no criterion is offered.
  • ⭕ Can LLM interaction be designed to restore continuous direction and feedback, making it closer to wielding?
    Not addressed; the essay treats the request/automation cycle as the nature of LLM use.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe argument runs on a stipulated definition of 'tool' (wield, with continuous control and feedback). It shows the definition is more discriminating than 'useful', but never shows that it matches how the 'just a tool' camp uses the word, or that the distinction it draws is the one that matters for risk and responsibility. The conclusion is close to true by definition; the substantive claim is that the stipulation is the illuminating one.
    Read next: Ivan Illich, Tools for Conviviality (convivial versus manipulative tools); Heidegger on equipment (ready-to-hand); Albert Borgmann on the device paradigm; philosophy-of-technology debates on instrumentalism versus technological determinism.
  • incompleteNo criterion is given for when handing work to automation is fine. Compilers, garbage collectors, CI, dishwashers and the drive-thru itself are automation, yet he does not condemn them. Where does the line fall between healthy abstraction and abdication of learning, and does it depend on the person's goals or career stage?
    Read next: Bainbridge, 'Ironies of Automation'; Whitehead on civilisation extending the operations we can perform without thinking; Hickey's own work on simplicity versus ease; corpus material on abstraction shifts in programming and on which tasks should stay with the human.
  • incompleteThe request/automation cycle is described as having no continuous direction or feedback, but interactive and agentic workflows (small-step edits, plan review, immediate diff inspection, test-driven loops) do offer tight, repeated feedback and steering. The essay does not consider whether granularity or interface design can move LLM use along a spectrum from automation toward wielding.
    Read next: Corpus material on human-in-the-loop, closed-loop coding agents and the agentic loop; practitioner accounts of tight-loop pairing with a model; Cory Doctorow's centaur versus reverse-centaur test.
  • uninformedThe claim that delegating forfeits learning and that nothing equivalent is gained from reviewing or specifying is asserted without evidence on LLM use. Counter-hypotheses (expertise shifts toward specification and judgment, or delegation frees time for deeper learning elsewhere) are not engaged.
    Read next: Empirical work on skill formation under AI assistance and on cognitive offloading; corpus material on cognitive debt and desirable difficulty.
  • incompleteThe closing line that tools do not get misaligned or go rogue implies that those words are apt for LLMs, but the essay does not connect this to the AI-alignment debate. It leaves open whether the alignment vocabulary is a legitimate description of automation or itself a category confusion borrowed from anthropomorphic framing.
    Read next: Corpus pieces on AI alignment and denialism, and on anthropomorphisation of LLMs.
  • incompleteThe 'AI titans treat everyone as tools' aside is a one-sentence political-economy claim that is never argued: who is exploited, how, and how it relates to the user's loss of agency when abdicating to automation.
    Read next: Cory Doctorow on enshittification and reverse centaurs; Zuboff on surveillance capitalism; corpus material on VC hype incentives and vendor lock-in.
