Leder du fra sidelinjen – eller ute på banen?

Mats Kristensen · article · 2026-08-01 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Dual LeadershipHands-On Leadership & the PendulumOrganizational Abstraction LevelsTeam AutonomyTechnology Leadership (Heis)Trust
Good leadership is range, not a fixed position: leaders should deliberately move between an outside, strategic vantage point ('sidelinjen') and direct, hands-on involvement ('banen'), because each position sees something real the other cannot, and staying too long in either one — never stepping back to reflect, or never staying close enough to feel what is actually happening — degrades leadership.

practical · mixed — Argumentative/expository LinkedIn essay built on a single extended sports narrative (the World Cup final, Solbakken vs. Ødegaard). It is practical throughout: every observation is in service of a prescription — leaders should deliberately vary their operating altitude rather than settling at one fixed position. The football material is not incidental color; it does most of the argumentative work the piece never states abstractly.

Key terms

  • Å lede fra sidelinjen
    Leading from an outside, non-executing vantage point: seeing the whole picture, setting direction and plan, but never doing the work yourself — structurally, in football; by choice or discipline, in a workplace.
  • Å lede ute på banen
    Leading from inside the work itself: performing the task while also leading the people around you, gaining a felt, proximate situational awareness (pressure, morale, fatigue) unavailable from outside.
  • Delt lederskap (shared leadership)
    Two leaders occupying complementary positions (outside/inside), each contributing information, trust and direction the other cannot generate alone, with performance depending on bidirectional flow between them.
  • Å sette seg selv på benken
    A deliberate, temporary act by an operationally embedded leader of stepping back from execution — not to disengage or hand off accountability, but to let others take ownership and to regain the capacity for reflection and forward thinking.
  • Spennvidde (range)
    The capacity to move between the two positions as circumstances demand, rather than occupying a fixed midpoint between them — explicitly contrasted with finding 'a fixed place midway between Solbakken and Ødegaard'.

Arguments

Leaders should deliberately alternate between operational involvement and strategic distance rather than settling permanently in either position (or at a fixed midpoint). strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Leading from the sideline (strategic distance) yields whole-picture awareness, planning capacity, and forward thinking, but loses feel for lived reality if sustained too long.
  • Leading from the pitch (operational involvement) yields felt, proximate situational awareness unavailable from outside, but loses reflective capacity and the ability to ask the big leadership questions if sustained too long.
  • The football case shows the two positions functioning well precisely because information and trust flow both ways between them.
Implicit premises:
  • The costs of staying too long in either position are symmetric and comparably serious (this is asserted by structure/juxtaposition, not argued).
  • A single leader (not just a leadership pair) can meaningfully occupy both positions at different times, and switching between them is a skill rather than a fixed trait or role.
  • The workplace, unlike football, gives leaders the freedom to choose their position at any moment — which is precisely what makes the discipline of choosing well necessary.
Inferences:
  • If range is the goal, organizations should probably design roles, calendars, or rituals that force leaders off the pitch periodically (analogous to the football coach's structural constraint), rather than relying on individual willpower alone — the author gestures at this with 'sette seg selv på benken' but never proposes a mechanism.
A leader who steps in and takes over delegated work is making a mistake, even though it can look effective in the moment. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Taking back a delegated task can look effective on a short time horizon.
  • Over a longer horizon, it increases employee dependency and pulls the leader further into operational work.
Implicit premises:
  • Employee dependency and leader over-operationalization are themselves bad outcomes worth avoiding — the value judgment is never defended, only asserted.
  • The short-term/long-term trade-off generalizes across situations; the piece doesn't distinguish cases where stepping in is actually warranted (e.g. genuine crises, safety issues, a team member who is out of their depth) from cases where it isn't.
Inferences:
  • This creates unresolved tension with situations the article doesn't address: sometimes stepping in IS the correct call (compare the corpus's Pincus interview, which argues the best leaders stay maximally close to the work and treat 'micromanagement' as often correct) — the piece offers no criterion for telling these cases apart.

Narratives

  • The extended, real-world contrast between Ståle Solbakken (national coach, leading from the sideline) and Martin Ødegaard (captain, leading on the pitch) during the just-concluded World Cup — including the specific episode of Solbakken resting the first-choice eleven against France and Ødegaard publicly endorsing the decision.
    → Carries the entire argument. The author never independently argues that 'range beats a fixed position' from workplace evidence; the football pair *shows* it — the story does the persuasive work that an abstract argument would otherwise have to do, and the reader is left to trust the analogy's transfer to work rather than being shown it directly.
  • Two named 'lederfeller' (leader traps) drawn from the author's own consulting/coaching observations ('I arbeidslivet møter jeg begge ledertypene') — the leader permanently on the sideline losing touch, and the leader permanently on the pitch never gaining altitude.
    → Functions as the bridge narrative that explicitly transfers the football contrast into a workplace claim; it is the only place in the piece where the author claims first-hand (rather than purely analogical) evidence.
  • Reference to Ronald Heifetz's balcony/dance-floor metaphor ('dansegulvet og balkongen') as an authority citation for the 'move between positions' prescription.
    → Borrows external, established credibility for the prescription without engaging Heifetz's actual framework (adaptive vs. technical leadership) — used purely as a restatement of the sideline/pitch dichotomy in different words, not as an independent line of support.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Where should a leader position themselves relative to the work — close and hands-on, or distant and strategic?
    Neither permanently; the answer is deliberate movement between the two ('spennvidde'), not a fixed position at either pole or at a midpoint.
  • ⭕ How does a leader know when to move from one position to the other — what is the actual trigger or decision rule?
  • ⭕ What structural or organizational mechanisms (analogous to the football coach's rule against playing) could force or support a leader's discipline of periodically stepping back, rather than relying on individual willpower?
  • ⭕ Why does authority sometimes come from competence and credibility rather than volume or position, and how is that authority actually built (as opposed to just observed in Ødegaard's case)?
    Only illustrated (Ødegaard is described as such), not explained mechanistically.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece never converts 'spennvidde' into an actionable, falsifiable decision rule — it does not say how a reader should decide, in a given week or situation, whether to be on the sideline or on the pitch. It stays at the level of a compelling metaphor and a values statement ('leadership is about range') rather than cashing that out into a specific, testable claim or a diagnostic the reader could apply to themselves. This matches the reader's known skepticism toward pieces that don't get past the metaphor to a concrete, falsifiable claim.
    Read next: Charity Majors' original 'Engineer-Manager Pendulum' essay, or the corpus's own extract-heis-14-oystein-ulseth-pendler-lede-levere, for a more concrete treatment of when and how to switch operating modes; Heifetz's actual adaptive-leadership work (not just the borrowed metaphor) for a real decision framework for when technical/operational engagement is warranted versus when the problem is adaptive and requires distance.
  • incompleteThe entire generalization from football to the workplace rests on a single anecdotal pair (Solbakken/Ødegaard) plus the author's own unsupported claim to have observed 'begge ledertypene og begge lederfellene' in professional life. No data, case study, or even a second concrete workplace example is offered. The football analogy is evocative but N=1, and football coaching (a role with a hard, external rule against playing) may transfer only weakly to workplace leadership, where the boundary the author is trying to recreate voluntarily does not exist by default.
    Read next: Empirical work on leader involvement/distance and team outcomes — e.g. the Journal of Organizational Behavior finding cited in extract-hbr-superteam (leaders who work alongside their teams report more energy and effectiveness than those managing from a distance) — to see whether 'range' or 'proximity' is actually the better-supported prescription.
  • incompleteThe article asserts that taking back delegated work 'can look effective short-term but creates dependency long-term' as a near-universal rule, without ever addressing the cases where a leader stepping back in genuinely is correct — a team member in over their head, a safety-critical error in progress, a crisis requiring the leader's specific expertise. It offers no way to distinguish harmful takeover from warranted intervention, leaving a real tension with the corpus's other position (extract-pincus-hidden-pattern-successful-products#p22-p23) that the best leaders stay maximally close to execution and treat routine 'micromanagement' as the correct default until it becomes physically impossible.
    Read next: extract-hbr-superteam#p6 for a sharper version of the same distinction (involvement builds capacity vs. micromanagement creates dependency — the difference being intent and impact); extract-pincus-hidden-pattern-successful-products for the opposing view that proximity/'micromanagement' is usually the right default for founders and product leaders.
