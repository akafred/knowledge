Technology investments — generative AI adoption and internal developer platforms alike — reliably raise individual, team, and organizational performance only when paired with the underlying sociotechnical fundamentals (small-batch delivery discipline, developer independence, transformational leadership, a genuine user-centric and documentation culture, and stable priorities); absent those fundamentals, the same investments degrade software delivery throughput and stability and can increase burnout, so AI and platforms amplify whatever organizational system already exists rather than fixing it.

practical · mixed — A decade-ten, 39,000-respondent empirical survey report (expository, statistical) that is explicitly in service of practical organizational action: it closes with a recommended continuous-improvement method and named interventions (stabilize priorities, be user-centric, become data-informed, commit to cloud or stay out), so the findings are presented as inputs to 'what should a team or org do.'

Key terms

  • Software delivery throughput
    A statistically distinct factor composed of change lead time, deployment frequency, and failed deployment recovery time — the speed component, newly separated from stability this year because the data showed independence between the two.
  • Software delivery stability
    A factor composed of change failure rate plus a newly added 'rework rate' metric (unplanned deployments to fix a user-facing bug) — DORA's hypothesis that change failure rate functions as a proxy for rework.
  • Developer independence
    Existing users of an internal platform being able to perform their tasks for the entire application lifecycle without relying on an enabling team.
  • Golden paths
    Highly automated, self-service workflows a platform's users follow when provisioning or operating application infrastructure, designed to abstract away delivery complexity so the developer only worries about code.
  • Transformational leadership
    A leadership model scored across five specific dimensions — vision, inspirational communication, intellectual stimulation, supportive leadership, personal recognition — used as a validated survey construct, not a vague honorific.

Arguments

AI's harm to software delivery stability stems from an erosion of small-batch delivery discipline, not from AI-generated code being inherently worse. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI improves upstream process measures historically correlated with better delivery performance (doc quality, code quality, review speed, approval speed, reduced complexity).
  • Despite those improvements, AI adoption is associated with worse delivery throughput and especially worse delivery stability.
  • DORA's prior decade of research shows larger changesets are slower and more unstable.
Implicit premises:
  • AI's productivity gains manifest partly as developers producing and committing more code per unit time.
  • Teams are not compensating for this increased code volume by deliberately shrinking batch size.
  • No other confound (e.g., riskier work being attempted because AI makes it feel feasible) fully explains the gap.
Inferences:
  • Without explicit discipline to keep changesets small, raw AI-driven throughput gains convert into integration and stability costs rather than net delivery improvement.
  • The fix is organizational/process (batch-size and testing discipline), not a wait for better models.
Platform engineering's net value depends on preserving user-centeredness and developer independence rather than mandating platform use, because the same mechanism that creates value (self-service) also explains why forced, non-independent use erodes it. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Developer independence correlates with a 5% productivity gain at both individual and team level.
  • Exclusive, mandatory platform use correlates with a 6% throughput decrease.
  • A platform without a user-centered approach is 'more a hindrance than an aid.'
Implicit premises:
  • Platforms generalize best when treated as products with real user feedback loops, not as infrastructure mandates.
  • The added 'handoffs' a platform introduces (automated testing, security checks, deployment, monitoring) are a legitimate throughput cost that independence and good UX can offset but not eliminate.
Inferences:
  • A platform strategy that optimizes purely for adoption percentage (coverage) rather than for developer independence and escape hatches will show the productivity/team gains but amplify the throughput and stability losses.
  • Platform engineering's 'unexpected downside' (lower throughput/stability, burnout linkage) is best read as a trade-off to actively manage, not evidence against the discipline.

Narratives

  • An interview participant (P2) compares trusting AI-generated code to the early days of Stack Overflow — assuming answers from experienced people and copy-pasting them, 'and then things explode' — while another participant (P3) says their company isn't worried about Copilot/ChatGPT copy-pasting because they already have 'so many layers to check it.'
    → Supports the proposition that developers don't need (or have) absolute trust in AI-generated code; a 'mostly correct, tweak it' relationship to AI output is compatible with existing quality-assurance layers and is not a new problem class, just a faster version of an old one.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Does AI adoption improve software delivery performance (throughput and stability)?
    No — it has a small negative effect on throughput and a larger negative effect on stability, even though it improves the upstream process measures that historically predicted better delivery.
  • ✅ Does AI adoption improve organizational, team, and product performance?
    Yes for organizational and team performance (small positive effects); no clear effect found for product performance.
  • ⭕ Why do larger organizations report less reliance on AI in daily work than smaller ones?
  • ✅ Can strong leadership, good documentation, and a user-centered culture protect employees from the burnout caused by unstable organizational priorities?
    No — the burnout effect of unstable priorities is resistant to all three; only stabilizing priorities themselves helps.
  • ⭕ Does platform engineering's throughput/stability cost mean the discipline isn't worth it, or is it a manageable trade-off?
    DORA offers three competing hypotheses (confident experimentation producing deliberate rework; platforms enabling bad changes through; unstable/burned-out teams building platforms as a response) without adjudicating between them.

Gaps

  • incompleteDORA infers that AI erodes delivery stability via growing changelist size, but never directly measures changelist/PR size — the mechanism is a plausible, decade-consistent hypothesis, not a demonstrated causal chain from AI adoption to larger diffs to instability.
    Read next: Look for studies that directly instrument diff size, PR size, or batch size alongside AI-adoption level and delivery-stability metrics, to test the batch-size mechanism DORA only infers.
    closed byTechnology Radar: An opinionated guide to today's technology landscape (Volume 30) — Thoughtworks's TAB reports, from direct observation across many client engagements in a document published six months before the DORA 2024 report, the same mechanism DORA could only infer (AI-assisted coding raises throughput, which tends to produce larger PRs, straining review). This is practitioner testimony, not a controlled measurement, but it is independent, dated-earlier corroboration of the hypothesized causal chain rather than a mere plausibility argument.
  • incompleteThe report's 'team performance' and 'organizational performance' factor scores are coarse composites; it does not examine whether specific team structures (team size, dedicated platform teams vs. embedded, degree of autonomy) moderate how AI adoption affects delivery stability — a gap directly relevant to a team/org durability lens.
    Read next: Team-topology- or team-structure-specific empirical work on how AI adoption's delivery-stability cost varies by team design, rather than by adoption percentage alone.
  • incompleteDORA offers three mutually exclusive hypotheses for why platform engineering correlates with higher change instability and burnout (confident rework, platform-enabled bad changes slipping through, or reverse causation from pre-existing instability/burnout driving platform adoption) but does not design a follow-up to distinguish them.
    Read next: Longitudinal or quasi-experimental platform-adoption studies that could separate reverse causation from a genuine platform-induced instability effect.

Closes gaps elsewhere
