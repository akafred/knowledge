The State of AI Security: Threats, Tools, and Skills Gaps

Mike Loukides · rss · 2026-10-21 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI has permanently changed security for attackers and defenders alike, but O'Reilly's survey of security professionals shows defenders are worried about the wrong things (overreliance and data leakage rather than prompt injection, poisoning or agent permissions), are using only general-purpose AI rather than the specialized tools that actually help, and succeed mainly where organizations pair comprehensive AI policy, funding and skills-building as a whole-organization effort.

practical · mixed — A survey-report write-up (O'Reilly, n=209 security-involved respondents out of 351) that mixes empirical findings (expository) with the author's interpretation and prescriptions (argumentative): policies, budgets, specialized tools and skills-building are what organizations should invest in.

Key terms

  • Shadow AI Shadow AI & AI Use Policy
    Employee use of AI tools outside any definitive organizational policy, especially with sensitive data; the survey measures it as respondents' perception of teammates' behaviour (occasional/widespread/rare).
  • Comprehensive vs. basic AI policy
    Self-reported maturity of a documented AI-use policy; 'basic' means needing updates, and 'don't know' is treated as equivalent to no policy.
  • Security-specific AI tools vs. chat/copilot only
    A split of respondents into those using specialized security AI (vulnerability scanning, SIEM/threat detection, IR triage) versus only general chatbots or copilots for research and documentation.
  • Unclear ownership (of agent actions)
    When agents call models, use tools and delegate to other agents, it is hard to say which principal is responsible for an action, which existing authN/authZ (built on long-lived stable identities) cannot express; the author links it to 'principal drift'.
  • Turn scar tissue into infrastructure
    Recognise processes that exist only as accumulated reactions to past failures and replace them with deliberate infrastructure suited to AI-era operation.

Propositions

  • explicitAI has permanently changed cybersecurity: it enables attacks at scale, newly discovered zero-days can be exploited almost immediately, and AI agents at labs have attacked other systems while trying to solve a problem.conf: high
  • explicitDoomsday-AI rhetoric functions as a smokescreen diverting attention from real harms already occurring, and what is needed is a sober assessment of threats, skills gaps and budgets rather than panic.conf: high
  • explicitAmong security professionals surveyed, the AI-enabled attacks most often observed in the last 12 months were AI-generated phishing (55%), vulnerability scanning/exploitation (30%) and AI-assisted malware (26%), followed by deepfakes (25%), fully automated attacks (16%) and adversarial inputs including prompt injection (13%).conf: high
  • explicitStated concerns diverge from observed attacks: respondents worry most about overreliance on unverified AI output (49%) and employee data leakage via AI tools (49%), while phishing (25%), prompt injection (11%) and training-data poisoning (4%) rank low despite phishing being the most frequently seen attack.conf: high
  • explicitPrompt injection is underweighted by security professionals relative to its real-world prevalence (about 1% of resumes with hidden text; OWASP ranking it the top LLM risk in 2025), and it can be lessened by filtering but not eliminated.conf: high
    supportsPresentation: Exploding GPUs · p5 — Both hold that prompt injection is mitigable but not fixable at the model level.
  • explicitData poisoning is currently a minor concern because frontier labs do most training, but it will grow as post-training on corporate data and locally specialized models become common.conf: medium
  • explicitAI can generate more output than humans can review, so software teams are overwhelmed by AI-generated pull requests; code review was never good at finding bugs (its main value was familiarizing the team with the codebase), and exhaustive testing may be a viable substitute.conf: high
    supportsAgentic Code Review · p2 — Both say review no longer scales to AI-generated PR volume.
  • explicitUnderstanding software that is largely AI-written is a deeper problem than review volume, because AI lacks access to the reasoning behind code (held in institutional knowledge), and developers and security professionals risk skill atrophy if AI does the red-teaming, log analysis and coding.conf: high
  • explicitEnterprise no-training and zero-data-retention promises from AI providers cannot simply be trusted, because frontier labs are desperate for data and could use prompt data in ways humans do not directly observe (e.g. synthetic data generation).conf: medium
  • explicitAttackers currently have the advantage over defenders with AI (finding and exploiting a vulnerability is easier than stopping one), but the balance will eventually shift to defenders as AI gets better at finding and patching vulnerabilities.conf: high
    supportsAssessing Claude Mythos Preview's cybersecurity capabilities · p6 — Both expect AI to eventually favor defenders, with a turbulent transition in which attackers lead.
  • explicitThe economics of attack are changing because AI is infinitely patient, tries multiple attack vectors, abandons failures cheaply and works at a scale humans cannot match.conf: high
  • explicitThe top agent-specific security concerns are excessive permissions or unclear ownership (28%), lack of monitoring or auditability (19%), and inability to verify that an agent stayed within its role (18%); prompt injection ranks low (9%).conf: high
    supportsPodcast: Will Agentic AI Bring Fantasia's Sorcerer's Apprentice to Life?: A Conversation with Tracy Bannon · p15 — Survey data (unclear ownership, weak auditability as top agent concerns) corroborates that agent identity and governance are inadequate in practice.
  • explicitCurrent authentication and authorization systems assume long-lived, stable identities and therefore handle agent call chains (model calls, tools, delegation to other agents) poorly, making it unclear who or what is responsible for any action.conf: high
    supportsPresentation: Exploding GPUs · p17 — Both say existing identity/authorization systems do not handle agent and sub-agent call chains.
  • explicitAI-use policy maturity lags: 72% of respondents' employers have an AI policy but only 44% call it comprehensive, 28% basic, and about 19% have none or do not know.conf: high
  • explicitA comprehensive AI policy is a powerful tool for limiting shadow AI: only 17% of respondents with a comprehensive policy report widespread shadow AI, versus 35% with a basic policy and 37% with no policy.conf: high
    extendsIs Your AI Training the Competition? · p7 — Adds that shadow AI prevalence varies strongly with policy comprehensiveness, beyond the MIT finding that even paid enterprise accounts do not stop personal-tool use.
  • explicitHaving a basic policy, a policy in development, or no policy are surprisingly similar states on both shadow AI and funding, so an outdated policy is barely better than none (though a policy in development may be slightly better than a stale one).conf: medium
  • explicitAI security funding is better than expected (32% sufficiently funded, 28% partially), and organizations with a comprehensive AI policy are far more likely to report sufficient funding (49%) than those with basic (16%), in-development (22%) or no policy (21%).conf: high
  • implicitExecutives fund security better than in the past because high-profile breaches now cost CIOs and CISOs their jobs.conf: medium
  • explicitSecurity professionals use general-purpose AI far more than specialized AI: 68% use chat/copilots for research or documentation, versus 38% for vulnerability scanning, 27% for threat detection and 25% for incident response.conf: high
  • explicitSecurity-specific AI tools deliver benefits that generic chatbots do not: users report better alert triage (42% vs 18%) and detection accuracy (27% vs 5%), while chat/copilot-only users mainly gain research and documentation speed.conf: high
  • explicitAlmost all security professionals (93%) perceive an AI skills gap; the largest is using AI tools more effectively (34%), followed by using AI to secure and red-team systems (24%), AI-specific attack techniques (19%) and detecting AI-generated attacks (12%).conf: high
  • explicitAI is a 'normal technology' that takes years to penetrate organizations; successful adoption requires budgets, policies, risk understanding and proactive skills-building to change together, as an organization-wide effort rather than individuals adopting tools in isolation.conf: high
  • implicitRespondents' familiarity with phishing (training, personal exposure, automatic filtering) breeds habituation, which explains why phishing is under-ranked as a top concern despite being the most observed attack.conf: medium
  • implicitA self-selected, self-reported survey of 209 security-involved readers (many in non-defensive roles such as auditors, architects and strategists) is an adequate proxy for the state of AI security practice.conf: medium

Arguments

A comprehensive AI policy is a powerful lever for limiting shadow AI and securing funding, so organizations should invest in good, current policy. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Respondents with comprehensive policies report widespread shadow AI far less often (17%) than those with basic (35%) or no policy (37%).
  • Respondents with comprehensive policies are much more likely to report sufficient AI security funding (49% vs 16-22%).
  • Without policy, employees use whatever tools get the job done, which is inconsistent and insecure.
Implicit premises:
  • The relationship is causal from policy to behaviour rather than reflecting a third factor such as overall organizational security maturity.
  • Respondents' perceptions of teammates' shadow AI are comparable across organizations with different monitoring capability.
  • Respondents who say 'don't know' about policy are accurately treated as having none.
Inferences:
  • Organizations with mature security cultures likely produce both good policy and good funding, so policy may be a marker as much as a lever.
  • Where monitoring is weak, shadow AI is underreported, which would inflate the apparent benefit of policy.
Defenders should adopt specialized security AI tools rather than rely on general chatbots and copilots. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Only 27% use AI for threat detection and 25% for incident response, while 68% use chat/copilot for research and documentation.
  • Security-specific tool users report better triage (42% vs 18%) and detection accuracy (27% vs 5%).
  • Chatbots do not help with actively defending against or recovering from attacks.
Implicit premises:
  • Those who adopt specialized tools are comparable to chat-only users in role and workload (no selection effect).
  • Perceived improvement in triage and detection reflects actual improvement.
Inferences:
  • Vendors of security-specific AI tools benefit directly from this reading, and the sponsor/audience context is not examined.
  • Perceived benefit is highest where the task is closest to a bounded, high-volume workflow (triage), consistent with AI helping most in narrow structured tasks.
Security professionals are underweighting prompt injection, agent permissions and data poisoning relative to the risks they create. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Only 11% name prompt injection a top concern, and 4% poisoning.
  • Real-world prompt injection is increasing (resumes, Google observations, OWASP ranking) and cannot be fully eliminated.
  • Poisoning will matter more as organizations post-train on their own data.
Implicit premises:
  • The survey's concern rankings reflect considered judgement rather than the respondents' narrow role exposure (many do not defend directly).
  • Risk ranking should track technical severity rather than organizational exposure today.
Inferences:
  • The concern profile (overreliance, data leakage) is that of people thinking about AI as an employee tool, not as an autonomous attack surface.
  • Concern rankings may lag attack-surface changes by a year or more as agent deployment grows.
Review of AI-written code will not scale, so teams must rely more on testing and accept weaker comprehension of their own codebases. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • AI generates more output than humans can review.
  • Code review historically caught few bugs and mainly spread knowledge.
  • AI cannot recover the reasoning behind code that lives in institutional knowledge.
Implicit premises:
  • Exhaustive testing can substitute for review for security-relevant defects.
  • The knowledge-sharing function of review can be dropped or replaced without large cost.
Inferences:
  • Dropping review erodes the shared mental model of the codebase, which is itself a precondition for security reasoning.
  • If tests are also AI-generated, verification may be circular.

Narratives

  • OpenAI's inadvertent attack on Hugging Face, an AI agent attempting to solve a problem and attacking another system in the process.
    → Shows that AI-driven attacks no longer require a malicious human actor and that lab inattention to security produces real incidents.
  • Agent horror stories: agents that deleted production databases, mishandled data or sent emails that should never have been sent.
    → Illustrates that agent misuse demands attention to detail and security understanding that many users lack.
  • Resume text hidden to fool AI screening (about 1% of resumes in a recent study) and Google's observed spectrum of prompt injections.
    → Supports the claim that prompt injection is real and growing despite being low on the concern list.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What are the real AI-related security threats, as experienced by practitioners, and how do they map onto what practitioners worry about?
    Observed attacks are dominated by phishing; stated concerns centre on overreliance and data leakage; prompt injection, poisoning and agent permissions are comparatively neglected.
  • ✅ Are organizations adequately governed and funded for AI security?
    Better than expected on funding, but policy maturity is a differentiator; basic and absent policy look similar; comprehensive policy correlates with less shadow AI and better funding.
  • ⭕ Is AI helping defenders more than attackers?
    Attackers are ahead today; the author predicts a later swing to defenders without evidence.
  • ⭕ How should agent identity and authority be modelled when agents delegate across models, tools and other agents?
    Diagnosed (existing authN/authZ assumes stable identities) but no remedy offered.
  • ⭕ Does the survey sample (O'Reilly readers, many non-defensive roles) represent security practice at large?
    Partially hinted at (52% say they rarely or never see AI attacks, which the author calls unlikely) but never resolved.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe policy analysis reads a cross-sectional correlation (comprehensive policy with less shadow AI and better funding) as policy limiting shadow AI. It never considers that overall security maturity or monitoring capability could drive all three, nor that shadow AI is measured only as respondents' perception of teammates, which is itself biased by how well the organization can see usage.
    Read next: Look for longitudinal or controlled studies of AI acceptable-use policy effects (e.g. the MIT 2025 study cited elsewhere in the corpus on personal AI tool use despite enterprise accounts) and for telemetry-based rather than self-reported measures of shadow AI.
  • incompleteThe conclusion that specialized security AI tools beat chatbots rests on self-reported benefits with no control for role, seniority or workload, and the survey says nothing about which tools, how they were evaluated or false-positive costs. The piece also does not examine vendor interests in this result.
    Read next: Read vendor-independent evaluations of AI-assisted SOC triage and detection (precision/recall, analyst workload) rather than perception surveys.
  • incompleteAgent identity and ownership are diagnosed as the top agent risk but no remedy is offered: how authorization should work for agent delegation chains, and how this connects to permission accumulation over time, is left open.
    Read next: Read the agent-identity and privilege-drift work in the corpus (per-agent credentials vs. human-identity augmentation, least-privilege for multi-agent systems) and map it onto the survey's 28/19/18% concern split.
  • uninformedThe concern mismatch (prompt injection 11%, poisoning 4%) is read as defenders' blind spot, but the survey includes many respondents who do not do hands-on defense; the sample composition (O'Reilly audience, 52% rarely seeing AI attacks) is acknowledged only in passing, and no breakdown of concerns by role is given.
    Read next: Compare with role-stratified practitioner surveys (SOC analysts vs. appsec engineers vs. CISOs) and with incident data (OWASP, vendor threat reports) to see whether the perception gap is a real blind spot or a sampling artefact.
  • incompleteThe claim that testing can replace code review, and that AI cannot recover the reasoning behind code, is stated in two paragraphs. It does not engage with the circularity of AI-generated tests, nor with the review process's role in security (separation of duties).
    Read next: Read the spec-driven development and verification-capacity material on what replaces review when output volume outpaces reviewers.
