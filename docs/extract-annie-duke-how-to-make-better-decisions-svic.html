Because knowing about our biases does not stop us from acting on them, better decisions come from structure rather than awareness: gathering opinions independently before any discussion, deciding in advance (via premortems and kill criteria) what signals will make us stop, and judging decisions apart from their outcomes.

practical · mixed — Conversational podcast (two hosts and Annie Duke) that argues for decision practices, interleaved with explanations of biases and many anecdotes; a practical text in service of 'do X to decide better', with theoretical background.

Key terms

  • resulting (outcome bias) Resulting (Outcome vs. Decision Quality)
    Judging the quality of a decision by its outcome; making the decision fit the result.
  • hindsight bias Resulting (Outcome vs. Decision Quality)
    Believing you should have known, or did know, what you could only learn after the fact.
  • premortem Premortem
    Imagining that a bad outcome has already occurred and working backward to its early signals, as input to checklists, kill criteria and pre-commitments (not as generic insurance brainstorming).
  • kill criteria Strategic Quitting & Kill Criteria
    Signals decided in advance that, if observed, commit you to stop (sell the investment, kill the deal).
  • pre-commitment contract Strategic Quitting & Kill Criteria
    Binding your future self (and telling someone) to a specific action when specified conditions occur, made in the abstract before being in the moment.
  • sunk cost fallacy Strategic Quitting & Kill Criteria
    Taking past investment into account when deciding whether to continue, which makes stopping very hard.
  • base rate Outside View & Base Rates
    How often something occurs in situations similar to the one considered; an outside-view check on fear and optimism.
  • outside perspective Outside View & Base Rates
    Other people's independently formed view of the same information, or looked-up base rates, used to discipline one's own biased interpretation.
  • opinion contagion Independent-First Group Elicitation
    Beliefs and biases spread in a room like infection, driven by confidence, seniority and leadership, so a group amplifies bias.
  • dispersion
    Spread of independently stated opinions on an item; the places where people disagree are where discussion should be spent.
  • alignment (redefined) AI Alignment & Safety
    Everyone has been heard and the decision-maker decides; explicitly not consensus or all agreeing.
  • guardrails Guardrails
    Structures that make good behaviour happen regardless of whether people know the underlying bias.
  • medic (vs quitting coach)
    A third party who pulls you out when pre-written conditions are met, so the stop is not your personal failure.
  • sandbagging / de-risking
    Padding estimates or choosing decisions that neither win nor lose much, to avoid postmortem exposure.
  • out of how many
    The denominator question: counts mean nothing without the group size, control group and sample size.

Propositions

  • explicitHumans are not terrible decision-makers (the species is alive and has done well), but our shortcuts produce systematic, predictable errors; roughly 20% of the time we go wrong, and that 20% is large relative to the quality of our life outcomes.conf: high
  • explicitHeuristics are good shortcuts in the environment they evolved for and fail when the environment changes: availability bias is sensible in a social group of ~300 in a small territory but misleads on a global scale (we fear terrorism over car accidents because TV makes it vivid).conf: high
  • explicitcorroboratedKnowing about a bias does not remove it: like the Muller-Lyer illusion, you still see it after it is explained and measured, so 'now I know about it, so I won't do it' is the central mistake of the popular decision-science literature.conf: high
    supports ←This will make you a better decision maker | Annie Duke (Thinking In Bets, former pro poker player) · p3 — Both hold that knowing about biases does not remove them; structure is what works.
  • explicitThere are two families of practical remedies that work without relying on awareness: (1) outside perspectives (other people's independent views, base rates) and (2) pre-commitment (deciding in advance, as a future self, what you will do).conf: high
  • explicitConfirmation bias has two parts, biased search and biased interpretation; you cannot stop it by introspection, but you can expose where your interpretation diverges by showing others the same information without your opinion attached.conf: high
  • explicitLooking up base rates (how often something occurs in comparable situations) disciplines you toward reality in both directions (fear of terrorism too high, restaurant success too optimistic: ~40% survive vs the founder's 90%) and lets you act proactively (capitalize, build runway, time the Series A raise).conf: high
  • explicitWe are bad at certain decisions in the moment but good at deciding on behalf of a future version of ourselves, so decisions should be moved out of the moment through pre-commitment contracts (throw out the ultra-processed food; write down kill criteria and tell someone).conf: high
    supportsIntroducing The Founder Mindset: Tim Ferriss on Experiments, Risk, and Freedom · p6 — Both set pass/fail judgment points in advance so an experiment or commitment can actually fail.
  • explicitThe sunk cost fallacy keeps people in a position because of what they already invested (holding a stock they would not buy at that price to 'get back to even'); the remedy is to imagine a week from now, write down the signals that would make you sell, and commit to them (ideally to someone else).conf: high
    supportsPresentation: Platform Engineering for Everyone - Success Can’t Be Coded · p15 — Both argue that explicit criteria defined ahead of time are needed because sunk-cost reasoning otherwise keeps failing things alive.
  • explicitA premortem imagines that the bad outcome has already happened and works backward to the early signals that would have warned you; it counters overconfidence, sharpens estimates of failure probability, and yields good kill criteria, checklists and pre-commitment contracts.conf: high
  • explicitWe have a false intuition that when things start going wrong we will notice and act; in fact we systematically ignore warning signals (people climb Everest in blizzards although they would say beforehand they would not), so the signals and the response must be fixed in advance.conf: high
    supportsTBM 445: The Feature Factory: A 10-Year Retrospective · p16 — Duke's evidence that people discount warning signals supports the point that contrary results do not dislodge conviction; her pre-committed kill criteria answer it.
  • explicitcorroboratedA premortem is only valuable when paired with a commitment to a specific action when a signal appears; it is a decision tree built in advance and locked in against the moment of weakness, not just 'insurance' brainstorming of what might go wrong.conf: high
    extendsThe hidden pattern behind successful products | Mark Pincus (Founder of Zynga) · p11 — Pincus says teams fail by continuing on hope; Duke supplies a mechanism (premortem-derived signals plus pre-committed kill criteria) for stopping before hope kills you.
    supports ←This will make you a better decision maker | Annie Duke (Thinking In Bets, former pro poker player) · p24 — Both hold that a pre-mortem is worth doing only when paired with pre-committed actions on the signals.
  • explicitResulting: when we know the result we bend our judgment of decision quality to fit it (green light plus a crash caused by a red-light runner does not make going through green lights bad; getting home safely drunk does not make drunk driving good), and this is hard to see where the environment is not transparent.conf: high
  • explicitHindsight bias makes us feel we should have known, or did know, beforehand; often the relevant information arrived only after the decision, so a postmortem tries to fit decisions to the outcome like a square peg into a round hole.conf: high
  • explicitBad outcomes can follow good decisions (and the reverse) through luck, so a postmortem that always searches for a bad decision behind a bad outcome will invent errors that were not there.conf: high
  • explicitPostmortems are asymmetric: teams analyze going over budget or late but never shipping way early and under budget, although both are the same prediction error and both offer learning (why did we under-promise? can it be replicated?).conf: high
  • explicitAsymmetric, blame-flavored postmortems make sandbagging and de-risking rational for the individual (tell the manager 10 weeks when you think 6; avoid being in a postmortem) while irrational for the leader who allocates resources across those individuals.conf: high
    extendsSoftware Engineering at Google · p9 — Henderson says blame suppresses honest reporting; Duke adds that downside-only postmortems also create rational sandbagging, de-risking and aversion to innovation.
  • explicitBecause failure is punished harder after unconventional success paths, individuals rationally prefer failing conventionally to succeeding unconventionally: they build consensus, accumulate data beyond the point of decision, and avoid anything new, which is why innovation stalls as organizations grow.conf: high
  • explicitExcessive layers of bureaucracy and demands for more evidence made Google sit on the Transformer and LaMDA while OpenAI, with less evidence but conviction, shipped; Duke agrees this is exactly the pattern.conf: medium
  • explicitcorroboratedCognitive biases are contagious like COVID: in a room (or on a synchronous Zoom) the most confident person, leadership or seniority infects everyone's beliefs, so a group amplifies one person's bias rather than disciplining it; 'more heads are better' only holds if you collect each head's opinion independently.conf: high

    supports ←This will make you a better decision maker | Annie Duke (Thinking In Bets, former pro poker player) · p5 — Same claim that in-room elicitation lets the most confident or senior person infect the group, so independence is a precondition for diverse views to count.
  • explicitWhen a CEO declares that the organization is aligned (or that he is leaning one way), even disagreeing reports soft-pedal; asking each person independently and in writing exposed that 'remote first' meant different things to different leaders and that there was no consensus for it.conf: high
    supportsWhy Great Leaders Need More Than Good Judgment · p5 — Both note that asking across a power differential does not reliably produce candid answers; Duke's remedy is independent written elicitation.
    supportsTBM 438: Am I the Only Weirdo in This Company? · p2 — The CEO's perceived alignment was visible adaptation to power-weighted norms; independent elicitation reveals the underlying range of belief.
  • explicitdevelopedDuke's group decision procedure: (1) leaders independently write open-ended opinions on the question (what each option means, opportunities and challenges, short- and long-term); (2) from those answers build a form of ~40 statements rated 1-7 (agree to disagree), completed independently and asynchronously, each with a 3-5 sentence rationale; (3) pour results into a colour-coded spreadsheet (shades of green for agreement, red for disagreement); (4) a facilitator, not the leader, drives discussion only on the areas of dispersion; (5) the decision-maker decides afterwards.conf: high

  • explicitDiscussion time should be spent only where smart people disagree (dispersion), with the facilitator 'excavating' the different points of view and the leader not speaking; that is where the gains in decision quality are.conf: high
    supportsScaling the Practice of Architecture, Conversationally · p4 — Both hold that seeking out those who disagree, rather than rubber-stampers, yields deeper input.
  • implicitcorroboratedThe decision should be separated from the discussion and the independent opinion-gathering: the discussion exists to surface viewpoints, and the decision-maker may then adopt all, part or none of anyone's view.conf: medium
    supports ←This will make you a better decision maker | Annie Duke (Thinking In Bets, former pro poker player) · p9 — Both separate the decision from discussion and independent opinion-gathering.
  • explicitDiverse teams only deliver the value of diverse viewpoints if the way the team operates lets those viewpoints be expressed; synchronous meetings and leader signalling turn a diverse hire into 'one mind'.conf: high
  • explicitcorroborated'Alignment' is wrongly taken to mean that everyone agrees and a consensus is reached; the right meaning is that everyone's view was heard and a decision-maker decides, and the pretense of consensus mostly serves blame diffusion in the postmortem ('no one gets fired for hiring McKinsey').conf: high
    supports ←This will make you a better decision maker | Annie Duke (Thinking In Bets, former pro poker player) · p10 — Both reject 'alignment' as agreement and redefine it as everyone being heard before a decision-maker decides.
  • explicitOur culture treats not quitting as a virtue and quitting as a character failure, even when persistence is harming the person's real goals: on the show Alone, experts who equate grit with virtue keep going until medical staff pull them out (frostbite, starvation), and Everest climbers continuing in a blizzard are not showing virtue.conf: high
  • explicitcorroboratedPeople are so biased against quitting that they won't quit until it is no longer a choice (Richard Thaler's point), so startups run out of runway instead of returning capital while there were many earlier signals to stop.conf: high
    supports ←This will make you a better decision maker | Annie Duke (Thinking In Bets, former pro poker player) · p26 — Both cite Thaler: people quit only when it is no longer a decision.
  • explicitContinuing something no longer worthwhile costs twice: you do not reach your goals, and the time, effort and attention are unavailable for something worthwhile (opportunity cost).conf: high
  • explicitcorroboratedOpportunity cost has a second face: while focused on the current project you often fail to see alternatives at all; Butterfield only saw Slack as a business after he quit Glitch.conf: high
    supports ←This will make you a better decision maker | Annie Duke (Thinking In Bets, former pro poker player) · p28 — Both use Butterfield quitting Glitch to see Slack as the opportunity cost of not stopping.
  • explicitQuitting done well speeds you up rather than slows you down.conf: high
  • explicit'I owe it to my employees to keep going' is a common rationalization for not quitting; Butterfield said he owed it to his employees to shut Glitch down because their equity was not going to be worth it (the host adds that the motive may be ego and fear of being called a quitter).conf: medium
  • explicitStructures that create guardrails matter more than whether people know the bias: the sales team did not need to understand sunk cost because kill criteria became part of deal review, with a carrot (credit for following them) and a stick (a ding for ignoring them).conf: high
  • explicitBecause we are only bad when we are in the middle of the decision, criteria set asynchronously in the abstract, before any particular deal is on the table, bind better than in-the-moment resolve.conf: high
  • explicitA third-party 'medic' who pulls you out under pre-written rules works better than a 'quitting coach', and also removes the blame and ego cost ('the medic pulled me out, I had no choice').conf: medium
  • explicitTo interpret data you need the right comparison: 'out of how many' (denominators), control groups, base rates and sample size; counts cannot be compared to counts (6,000 vaccinated vs 2,000 unvaccinated deaths means nothing without group sizes), and the knee anecdote shows the right comparison is your other knee, not all knees.conf: high
  • explicitDecisions are built out of forecasts, and decision skills (agency, pre-commitment, forecasting, resisting bandwagoning) can be taught from kindergarten up, because biases such as sunk cost are pervasive across ages.conf: medium
  • explicitLLMs can serve as non-judgmental third-party checks (fact-check an argument, base-rate sanity check a plan) and as statistical aggregators, which helps people too insecure to ask others for feedback.conf: medium
  • implicitThe core problem is structural rather than individual: poor decisions come from the moment-of-decision state, from social influence and from career-risk incentives, so the fix is to change where, when and from whom judgments are collected rather than to find wiser people.conf: medium

Arguments

Debiasing requires external structures (outside perspectives, base rates, pre-commitments), not awareness or willpower. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Illusions persist even after they are explained and measured.
  • We cannot stop confirmation bias by knowing about it.
  • We are bad at in-the-moment decisions but good at deciding for a future self.
Implicit premises:
  • Reasoning errors behave like perceptual illusions (the analogy carries over from perception to judgment).
  • Structures stay effective for people who do not understand why they exist.
  • The cost of structures (forms, rules, third parties) is lower than the cost of the errors they prevent.
Inferences:
  • Education about biases alone should have little effect on behavior, which sits uneasily with her own decision-education nonprofit unless that teaching is about structures and practice.
  • Organisational design (deal reviews, forms, facilitators) is a higher-leverage intervention than training.
A group decision is better when opinions are collected independently and in writing before any discussion, and discussion is then focused on where opinions diverge. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Biases and opinions are contagious in a room or synchronous call; the most confident, senior or the leader dominate.
  • Leaders' declarations make disagreeing people soft-pedal.
  • Asked independently, leaders held very different definitions and no consensus on remote first.
  • Gains in decision quality come from smart people who disagree.
Implicit premises:
  • Independently stated opinions are honest and not strategically shaded.
  • Dispersion marks genuinely informative disagreement rather than noise or differing definitions.
  • Unweighted written opinions (no expertise or stake weighting) are a good basis for discussion.
  • A decision-maker will actually use the surfaced disagreement.
  • Independence survives the later phase: once names and rationales are in the spreadsheet and discussed, the same career-risk dynamics Duke criticizes in postmortems could return.
Inferences:
  • The room is where opinions are corrupted, so meetings should come after, not before, opinion capture.
  • Diversity hiring without process change wastes the diversity.
The five-step remote-first procedure (independent written opinions -> 1-7 survey with rationales -> colour-coded spreadsheet -> facilitated discussion of dispersion -> decision separate from discussion) is a sound template for group decisions. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Step 1 (open-ended, individual) reveals the real range of definitions and views.
  • Step 2 (numeric scale plus 3-5 sentence rationale, asynchronous) makes opinions comparable and reasoned.
  • Step 3 (colour coding) makes agreement and dispersion visible at a glance.
  • Step 4 (facilitator, not leader, speaks) prevents leader anchoring and focuses time on disagreement.
Implicit premises:
  • The question can be framed as statements people can rate; the procedure collects opinions on a question, not generated ideas or proposals.
  • Dispersion alone is enough to prioritize discussion; no ranking, weighting or aggregation step is needed before the decision.
  • A 40-item survey is affordable relative to the stakes of the decision.
  • Separating decision from discussion is safe because the decision-maker is trusted.
Inferences:
  • The procedure is best for one-off, high-stakes, slow decisions, not frequent reversible ones.
  • Without a ranking or decision rule, the final step collapses back into the leader's judgment.
Pairing a premortem with pre-committed kill criteria makes people stop pursuing hopeless efforts earlier and reallocate time to better ones. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • In the moment we ignore warning signals and fall to sunk-cost reasoning.
  • Imagining the failure having happened makes early signals easy to generate.
  • Commitments made in the abstract, to a manager or in deal review, bind later behaviour.
  • Sellers like to win, so credit and dings align incentives with the criteria.
Implicit premises:
  • The signals generated in a premortem are actually predictive of failure.
  • Kill criteria will not cause premature abandonment of deals or projects that would have succeeded (false-positive cost is acceptable).
  • Criteria will not be gamed (e.g. sellers avoid hard but valuable deals to stay clean).
  • Opportunity cost of the freed time is realized in better use.
Inferences:
  • Kill criteria should themselves be reviewed against realized outcomes.
  • The same pattern applies to investments, projects and technical bets.
Postmortems systematically mislead and distort team behaviour, whereas premortems avoid the same traps because they are conducted before the result is known. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Resulting and hindsight bias bend decision-quality judgments toward outcomes.
  • Postmortems usually focus on downside misses only.
  • People rationally respond by sandbagging and avoiding innovation.
Implicit premises:
  • Decision quality is at least partly separable from outcome quality and can be judged without knowing the outcome.
  • Premortem outputs are not themselves distorted by the narrative of an anticipated failure.
  • Leaders cannot simply run unbiased postmortems by good intentions ('we're not blaming anyone').
Inferences:
  • Review successes and under-budget surprises too, and evaluate decisions against what was knowable then.
  • Organisations should measure the process, not only the outcome.
We should quit earlier and by design, using pre-agreed rules and an external 'medic', rather than persisting until forced. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Not quitting is culturally treated as virtue.
  • People won't quit until it is no longer a choice.
  • Persisting pays twice: lost goals and lost opportunity.
  • While committed, we cannot see alternative opportunities (Slack).
Implicit premises:
  • Most persistence under bad signals is sunk-cost error rather than justified patience.
  • The stop-or-go signals can be specified ahead of time with enough accuracy.
  • The goal being pursued is the one the person actually values (health, runway, expected value).
Inferences:
  • The 'quitting coach' framing leaves the decision in the decision-maker's compromised moment, so the medic metaphor is better.
  • Quitting is a skill with its own process, not a failure of character.

Narratives

  • Moon illusion and Muller-Lyer lines: the brain builds a 3D world from depth cues; the moon looks bigger on the horizon, and two equal lines look different even after you measure them with a ruler.
    → Analogy from perception to cognition: shortcuts mostly work but can be tricked, and explanation does not dispel the illusion (supports the claim that awareness is insufficient).
  • Everest in a blizzard: climbers who would say at the bottom that they would never climb in a blizzard do exactly that at the top.
    → Shows that people ignore warning signals in the moment, motivating pre-committed signals and responses (premortem plus kill criteria); also used against grit-as-virtue.
  • Sales team premortem (CDP SaaS product): Duke asked sellers to imagine a lead pursued for six months had just died and to list the early signals; they named (a) the lead only asks about price and wants no demo, (b) more than three meetings without a decision-maker in the room, (c) an RFP/RFI clearly written for a competitor. Each signal was paired with a commitment: kill the deal ourselves; ask for executive alignment and kill if refused; ask directly whether they work with the competitor and how far along, then go to the sales manager. The criteria became part of deal review, with credit for following them and a ding for ignoring them.
    → Worked example of premortem plus pre-commitment contract: the signals are identified in the abstract, bound into an organisational guardrail, and make sellers reallocate time to winnable deals without needing to understand the bias.
  • Remote-first CEO decision (Duke consulting): the CEO said the organization was aligned on remote first. Duke emailed the leadership individually asking what remote first, hybrid and in-person mean and their opportunities, challenges and risks; answers showed different definitions and no consensus. She then built a ~40-statement form rated 1-7 with 3-5 sentence rationales, completed independently and asynchronously, dumped into a green/red colour-coded spreadsheet, and facilitated discussion only on areas of dispersion with the CEO not speaking. 'That company is now in person.'
    → Shows opinion contagion and leader-declared alignment concealing real disagreement, and demonstrates the independent-first group procedure end to end.
  • Postmortem asymmetry: Joe (a former engineering manager) confirms that his teams held long postmortems when projects shipped late but never when they shipped early and under budget; Duke notes both are the same prediction error and that the asymmetry teaches 'don't screw up', pushing people to sandbag or avoid innovating.
    → Illustrates how incentive-laden postmortems distort behaviour and why decisions should be evaluated ex ante.
  • The show Alone: ten survival experts try to outlast each other in the wilderness with a satellite phone to tap out; almost all insist they are 'not a quitter', and many are pulled by medics (a possible foot amputation from frostbite, two people in organ failure) before they choose to quit.
    → Illustrates grit-as-virtue bias and that people won't quit until forced; motivates the 'medic' (pre-agreed external stop) metaphor.
  • Stewart Butterfield and Glitch: with $6M in the bank, 5,000 users playing 20+ hours a week but 99 others playing seven minutes and never returning, a six-week paid marketing push grew traffic ~6-7% week over week; his math showed break-even about 31 weeks away at rising acquisition cost and no venture-scale business, so he wrote to investors and co-founders that 'Glitch is dead' and offered to return capital. Two days later he realized the internal chat tool the team loved could be the next product; investors rolled their capital into it, and it became Slack.
    → Case of strategic quitting before being forced out (he did not wait for the 'medic'), reframing duty to employees, and showing opportunity blindness while committed to the old project.
  • Knee story: Duke's dog crashed into her knee; an orthopedist found her right knee hypermobile and concluded a torn ACL by comparing it to all other knees, but the right comparison was Duke's own left knee, which was equally hypermobile; it was only a bone bruise.
    → Illustrates the 'in comparison to what?' problem in interpreting data; the opening chapter of her next book on out-of-how-many, control groups and base rates.
  • Resulting examples: going through a green light and being hit by a red-light runner, or driving home drunk and arriving safely; no one concludes from the outcome that the first is bad or the second good, yet we do exactly that in less transparent environments such as engineering postmortems.
    → Makes the outcome-vs-decision-quality error visible before applying it to postmortems.
  • Google vs OpenAI (host's framing): Google had the Transformer and a LaMDA prototype by about 2017 but layers of bureaucracy demanded ever more evidence and it did not release, while OpenAI shipped on conviction.
    → Offered as an instance of the 'fail conventionally rather than succeed unconventionally' dynamic in larger organisations.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why are we bad at decisions, and does knowing about the biases help?
    Shortcuts mis-fire predictably; awareness does not help; use outside perspectives and pre-commitment.
  • ✅ How should a leadership team make a contested decision so that opinion contagion does not distort it?
    Independent written opinions, rated survey with rationales, colour-coded spreadsheet, facilitated discussion on dispersion.
  • ⭕ How do you run postmortems/learning without resulting and blame-driven distortion?
    Diagnoses the problem and moves the effort ex ante (premortem), but does not give a fixed postmortem protocol beyond symmetric review of surprises.
  • ✅ When and how should you quit?
    Pre-commit kill criteria in the abstract, use an external 'medic', count opportunity cost.
  • ⭕ How can the procedure choose between options or generate ideas, not only reveal disagreement?
    Not addressed; the decision returns to the leader after discussion.
  • ✅ How to teach decision skills to children?
    Start with agency and simple possible-outcomes thinking, add pre-commitment, then forecasting on topics students care about.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe remote-first procedure collects opinions on a question (agreement ratings with rationales on prepared statements whose option set was handed in by the CEO), not ideas or proposals, and it has no ranking or aggregation step: dispersion tells you where to talk, but nothing turns the spread into a prioritised option or a decision rule, so the last step reverts to the leader's judgment. The procedure is therefore incomplete as a full decision process (no option generation, no weighting by expertise or stake, no tie-break).
    Read next: Delphi method and nominal group technique (independent generation, then ranked voting); Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, Noise (mediating assessments protocol, structured delay of intuition); Tetlock-style team forecast aggregation; Amazon-style silent-reading memo reviews.
  • incompleteIndependence is protected only in the collection phase. Once rationales sit in a named spreadsheet and the facilitator calls on 'Jordan' and 'Joe' to defend positions, the same career-risk and deference pressures she blames for sandbagging and soft-pedalling can return; nothing says whether responses stay anonymous, how psychological safety is established, or how the CEO's eventual choice avoids punishing dissenters.
    Read next: Edmondson on psychological safety; research on anonymous electronic brainstorming and Delphi anonymity; the corpus's pluralistic-ignorance material on power-weighted norms.
  • illogicalThe evidence is anecdotal and judged by outcome, which is the very 'resulting' error she warns against: 'that company is now in person' is offered as vindication of the procedure, the sales team's kill criteria are described without conversion data, and the Glitch/Slack story is a success narrative that makes the quit look wise in hindsight. No control, no failed uses of the procedure, and no measure of decision quality independent of outcome.
    Read next: Tetlock's work on measuring decision/forecast quality independent of outcome; the evidence base on premortems (Mitchell, Russo and Schoemaker) and on group-decision protocols; Kahneman on outcome bias.
  • incompleteKill criteria are presented as costless guardrails with no attention to false positives (killing deals or projects that would have closed), to periodic recalibration of the criteria against outcomes, or to gaming (sellers avoiding hard-but-valuable leads to stay clean). She argues against quitting too late but never states a rule for avoiding quitting too early, which is the opposite risk persisting founders face.
    Read next: Optimal stopping and explore/exploit literature; real-options thinking on the value of continuing; Goodhart's-law material on criteria becoming targets; the corpus's Pincus 'kill hope' discussion.
  • incompleteNo triage of which decisions deserve this heavy process: a 40-statement independent survey plus facilitated session is affordable for a company-wide policy like remote work but not for the many fast, reversible decisions teams make weekly; no guidance on stakes thresholds or on avoiding the consensus-seeking, slow-shipping behaviour she criticises in large organisations.
    Read next: Reversible vs irreversible (type 1 vs type 2) decision frameworks; risk-tiering practice; Harmel's Advice Process for lightweight consultative decisions.
  • incompleteTension left unresolved: she says awareness of biases does not help, yet her Alliance for Decision Education aims to teach decision science to every K-12 classroom. The reconciliation (teach structures and practices, form habits early) is implied but no evidence is given that such teaching transfers to real decisions made under pressure.
    Read next: Debiasing and training research (Larrick; Soll, Milkman and Payne, 'A User's Guide to Debiasing'); evaluations of decision-education curricula.
