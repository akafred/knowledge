AI Is Undermining Leaders’ Judgment. Here’s What to Do About It.

Leonid Sudakov, Nathan Furr · article · 2026-08-19 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Capacity to Verify AI OutputCognitive SurrenderDecision-Making PracticesExplainable AIIronies of AutomationLeader Restraint
As AI-driven decision-support systems make organizations more informationally “intelligent,” they simultaneously train leaders out of original judgment through a three-stage erosion of cognitive offloading, deference to tools, and organizational monoculture — so leaders must deliberately practice structured curiosity and intentional dissent to preserve the independent judgment that produces competitive breakthroughs.

practical · mixed — Opens theoretical/diagnostic (a named 'Original Judgment Paradox' backed by a field experiment and a three-stage erosion mechanism), then turns practical: two named methods (CAST, RED) with historical and client narratives as illustration and evidence of feasibility.

Key terms

  • original judgment
    The uniquely human capacity to see beyond the dominant narrative and make choices that represent one's values under uncertainty — the thing that underlies competitive breakthroughs.
  • breadth of perception
    One of two interlocking capacities behind original judgment: noticing weak signals and adjacent patterns beyond the obvious.
  • independence of interpretation
    The other capacity behind original judgment: forming one's own view rather than deferring to the model's or the group's.
  • cognitive offloading Cognitive Surrender
    The first stage of judgment erosion: leaders increasingly relying on data-driven inputs, visible even at the neural level.
  • deference to tools
    The second erosion stage: trusting outputs from an 'algorithmic authority' the person does not fully understand.
  • organizational monoculture
    The third erosion stage: an organization-wide convergence on the same metrics, models, and narratives because everyone is deferring to the same tools.
  • structured curiosity (the CAST method)
    A disciplined practice — Collect from the edges, Arrange disparate signals, Suspend premature closure, Trigger alternative interpretations — for maintaining breadth of perception deliberately rather than as a personality trait.
  • intentional dissent (the RED method)
    A disciplined practice — Recruit independent perspectives, Engage through structured challenge, Dialogue to sustain over time — for cultivating dissent as an organizational capability rather than hoping for spontaneous bravery.

Arguments

Leaders must deliberately practice structured curiosity (CAST) and intentional dissent (RED) to preserve the original judgment that AI decision-support systems otherwise erode. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • A three-stage erosion process (cognitive offloading → deference to tools → organizational monoculture) is real and demonstrated by the cited field experiment and neural-level research.
  • Historical breakthroughs (Walton, Jobs, Huang, Tu Youyou, Warren and Marshall) depended on leaders/scientists exercising original judgment against prevailing consensus, orthodoxy, or exhausted pipelines.
  • Dissent and curiosity function better as deliberately designed organizational mechanisms than as personality traits left to chance.
Implicit premises:
  • What worked against human/institutional consensus and dogma in the past (the historical examples) generalizes to overcoming AI-generated consensus, even though the mechanism of persuasion (algorithmic authority vs. social/scientific orthodoxy) differs.
  • Two anonymized client engagements (one CAST program, two RED advisory-network programs) are representative enough to establish that these methods are broadly implementable, not idiosyncratic to those organizations.
  • Individual leadership practices can offset structural incentives — including the career risk of dissenting against an AI-vetted, seemingly objective recommendation — without requiring change to the incentive structure itself.
Inferences:
  • If AI recommendations produce the same silencing 'the answer is already known' effect that an authoritative human boss produces, then dissent-cultivation practices built for human hierarchies may need to be extended explicitly to treat AI outputs as a suppressible authority — a step the article gestures at (RED) but doesn't spell out as an AI-specific design requirement.

Narratives

  • MIT/Harvard field experiment: 228 evaluators judging 48 innovation-challenge submissions under three conditions (human-only, AI-with-narrative-explanation, AI-with-no-explanation), compared against expert judges.
    → Supports the core erosion thesis and specifically the counterintuitive finding that explanation worsens rather than improves override behavior.
  • Sam Walton building a discount retailer in towns under 50,000 people against conventional wisdom; Steve Jobs integrating calligraphy into the personal computer; Jensen Huang betting on unproven hardware emulation at Nvidia.
    → Supports the claim that original judgment underlies competitive breakthroughs.
  • Tu Youyou, without a PhD, returning to 2,000 ancient Chinese medical texts after Western pharmaceutical screening pipelines were exhausted, and noticing a fourth-century manuscript detail (extract cold, not boiling) that led to artemisinin.
    → Supports 'structured curiosity' as a disciplined, teachable practice rather than an innate trait — grounds the CAST method.
  • Pathologist Robin Warren and trainee doctor Barry Marshall building a deliberate dissent network (100-patient case series, outside methodological critics, skeptical-venue publication) to overturn the stress-and-acid consensus on peptic ulcers and establish H. pylori as the cause.
    → Supports 'intentional dissent' as requiring deliberate social reinforcement to survive — grounds the RED method.
  • An anonymized global company transforming from scale manufacturing to a health-focused ecosystem ran a nine-month program: 15 high-potential leaders in five mismatched teams of three, given complex out-of-expertise challenges with no predetermined solution.
    → Supports that structured curiosity can be organizationally engineered, not just individually practiced.
  • Two anonymized global companies built advisory networks of external experts outside reporting lines, tasked with pressure-testing logic rather than providing answers, which shifted over time from polite questioning to direct critique.
    → Supports that intentional dissent can be organizationally engineered as a protected practice space.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article reports as 'striking' that narrative AI explanations reduced override of incorrect recommendations, but never investigates or theorizes the mechanism — it doesn't engage with why explanation would increase rather than decrease inappropriate reliance, which runs counter to the common assumption (implicit in much explainable-AI work) that explanation supports appropriate calibration of trust.
    Read next: Explainable-AI and automation-bias research specifically on when and why explanation increases versus decreases over-reliance on an AI recommendation.
  • incompleteCAST and RED are each illustrated by exactly one historical pair plus one or two anonymized client engagements, with no measurement of whether these practices actually preserved judgment quality, no discussion of failure cases, and no account of whether the featured companies were simultaneously reducing AI usage (making it unclear whether the intervention, rather than reduced AI exposure, produced the reported outcomes).
    Read next: Any controlled or longitudinal study of structured-curiosity/structured-dissent interventions specifically in AI-saturated decision environments.
  • uninformedThe piece never engages the tension it stands in with 'broaden your inputs' leadership advice — e.g. work recommending leaders deliberately create AI personas, including contrarian ones, to surface blind spots. If AI-supplied recommendations measurably suppress independent override judgment (this article's own central finding), then leaning on AI personas for a 'different view' risks reproducing the very algorithmic-authority deference this article warns against, rather than curing it.
    Read next: extract-why-great-leaders-need-more-than-good-judgment (Marshall Goldsmith's 'panoramic leadership,' which recommends AI personas as a blind-spot tool)
  • incompleteThe article frames judgment erosion purely at the level of the individual leader (a leader deferring to AI). It does not address the cascading, accountability-laden version of the same problem: subordinates who must explain and defend a leader's AI-endorsed decision to others, and who may mask, embrace, or reinterpret that decision to protect their own credibility — a distinct organizational dynamic from the leader's own judgment eroding.
