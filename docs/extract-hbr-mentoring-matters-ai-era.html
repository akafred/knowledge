Why Mentoring Matters More in the AI Era

Marlo Lyons · article · 2026-07-24 Reader 🖥️

Capacity to Verify AI OutputCoaching Styles (Situational)Enterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessGiving Feedback (Performance & Coaching)Psychological SafetySystemic Apprenticeship & Skill TransmissionTaste & Judgment
Because AI is automating the routine work that once built professional judgment and pattern recognition through repetition, organizations must replace that lost experiential foundation with deliberately engineered, intentional mentoring — not the ad hoc, exposure-dependent mentoring models that assumed hands-on learning was already happening.

practical · mixed — Predominantly argumentative-practical: an HBR 'how to' piece arguing organizations should redesign mentoring practices. Framed with expository problem-setting up front, argued through six prescriptive practices in the middle, and closed with a narrative/quotation appeal to stakes.

Key terms

  • Training ground
    The routine, repetitive, exposure-based work (reviewing memos, interpreting ambiguous data, reading stakeholders) that builds tacit judgment and pattern recognition as a side effect of doing the work — not a formal training activity.
  • Making invisible thinking visible
    A specific mentoring technique: a senior leader narrates their tacit decision logic in real time (before/after high-stakes moments) so it becomes observable and teachable, rather than remaining an unspoken byproduct of experience.
  • Coaching style (vs. directive style)
    A questioning posture ('What would you do differently?') that develops a mentee's own critical thinking, contrasted with telling them the answer ('Here's what you should do').
  • Redesigned mentoring
    Mentoring built as a deliberate system — named competencies, defined cadence, written agreements, incentives, mentor training, and gap-based matching — rather than an informal, personality-dependent relationship.

Arguments

Mentoring must be redesigned — not merely resourced more heavily — for the AI era. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI automates the routine tasks whose repetition built judgment and intuition.
  • On-demand learning platforms and one-off training sessions don't transfer judgment, pattern recognition, professional instinct, or tacit knowledge.
  • Traditional mentoring models assume foundational learning has already occurred through hands-on work.
Implicit premises:
  • Judgment and pattern recognition can only be built through repeated exposure/practice, not through instruction or documentation alone.
  • Mentoring is mechanistically different from — not just a more intensive version of — L&D platforms and training programs.
Inferences:
  • Organizations that scale AI adoption without redesigning mentoring will develop leaders more slowly, or fail to develop a leadership pipeline at all.
  • The shortfall may compound across cohorts, since each generation of leaders eventually mentors the next from an increasingly AI-mediated (thinner) base of firsthand experience.
The six mentoring-redesign practices (clarify expectations, incentivize mentors, offer coaching support, match by skill gap, make thinking visible, build learning into everyday conversations) together close the AI-era development gap. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Each practice addresses a distinct, named failure mode of ad hoc mentoring (drift, lack of incentive/protected time, mentor skill deficit, misallocation by proximity, invisibility of tacit expertise, infrequency/formality).
Implicit premises:
  • The six practices are jointly sufficient and the list is exhaustive — no other structural factor (e.g., manager-to-report ratios, time budget, organizational flattening) limits their effect.
  • Organizations have the senior-leader bandwidth and organizational will to execute all six simultaneously.
Inferences:
  • Partial adoption (e.g., incentivizing mentors without training their coaching skill) would under-deliver, by analogy with the corpus's related finding that talent/team/routine interventions fail when applied in isolation.

Narratives

  • A mentor narrates real-time decision logic before and after a high-stakes meeting: 'Here's how I'm thinking about this room and what I'm watching for,' followed by a debrief on what shifted.
    → Makes concrete the 'make invisible thinking visible' practice — shows tacit judgment being externalized as teachable narration rather than argued for abstractly.
  • A senior leader reviews a draft and explains why they'd reposition the recommendation; a director walks through the tradeoffs in a project decision — framed as 15-to-30-minute 'as you work' exchanges.
    → Shows how ordinary work moments, not formal sessions, can carry the same developmental payload as scheduled mentoring — supports the claim that normalizing informal exchange sustains continuous learning.
  • Alison Levin (President, NBC Universal Advertising & Partnerships) is quoted from her talk at the IAB's annual leadership meeting: 'The best AI strategy in the world won't matter if we stop producing people who can actually lead this industry forward,' warning of a story of 'fragmentation, chaos, and missed opportunity.'
    → Borrows executive authority to dramatize the stakes of the closing claim — that pairing AI efficiency with mentoring is what separates thriving organizations from fragmented ones.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How do organizations accelerate employee development to build a stronger leadership pipeline, now that AI has removed the daily work that used to build judgment?
    Redesign mentoring intentionally around named competencies, incentives, mentor training, gap-based matching, and narrated tacit knowledge.
  • ✅ Is mentoring still the right lever if its traditional form assumed a foundation of hands-on learning that AI has removed?
    Yes, but only if mentoring itself is restructured — not merely scaled up in its old, assumption-laden form.
  • ⭕ How would anyone verify that a redesigned mentoring program is actually transferring judgment and pattern recognition, given these are among the least directly measurable competencies in the article's own list?
  • ⭕ Where will senior leaders find the time and bandwidth to do all six practices, especially in organizations simultaneously flattening management layers and increasing senior individuals' own hands-on workload?

  • uninformedThe prescriptions (real-time narration before/after meetings, 15-to-30-minute as-you-work reviews) implicitly assume synchronous, often co-located or at least highly available working relationships. There is no engagement with how this translates to distributed, asynchronous, or cross-timezone teams, where spontaneous narration is much harder to arrange.
    Read next: Remote-work and asynchronous-collaboration literature; any corpus material on distributed team practices.
  • incompleteThe article assumes an effectively stable reservoir of senior-leader tacit judgment to transmit downward, without acknowledging that today's senior leaders are the last cohort trained the old way — through the very routine-task repetition AI is now removing. If automation continues moving up the org over time, tomorrow's mentors (today's junior mentees) may themselves have thinner firsthand judgment to eventually transmit, making mentoring a one-generation patch rather than a durable structural fix unless the underlying on-ramp problem is also solved earlier in careers.
