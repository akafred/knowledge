DevOps Topologies

Matthew Skelton (devopstopologies.com) · article · 2026-10-08 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Platform EngineeringSociotechnical ArchitectureTeam TopologiesValue Streams & Verticals
There is no single universally correct Dev/Ops team structure for achieving DevOps's actual goal — improved delivery of customer and business value — because the right topology is contingent on an organization's product count (via Conway's Law) and on whether its Operations function has the capability and appetite to move from infrastructure-racking toward genuine value-stream alignment; a reference catalog of named anti-patterns and working patterns lets an organization diagnose and choose among structures rather than assume one conformation fits all.

practical · expository — A reference taxonomy page (predecessor material to Skelton & Pais's Team Topologies book) arguing that Dev/Ops team structure should be chosen deliberately from a catalog of named patterns, against the implicit claim that any one structure is universally correct. The capture is thin and largely visual — the catalog of named anti-types (A-G) and types (1-8) is represented mostly as bare image references without the descriptive text that (per the live site) normally accompanies each; only the intro/summary prose and a handful of named patterns carry textual argument here.

Key terms

  • Anti-Type B (DevOps Team Silo)
    A dedicated 'DevOps team' that itself becomes a new silo positioned between development and operations, defeating the collaborative purpose DevOps is meant to serve. Named explicitly in the text but not elaborated beyond the label in this capture.
  • Type 1 (Dev and Ops Collaboration)
    The baseline 'good' pattern in which development and operations collaborate directly, without an intervening DevOps-team silo.
  • Type 3 (Ops as IaaS)
    A pattern in which Operations' primary contribution is to provide infrastructure as a self-service capability to development teams, rather than acting as a silo that mediates every operational action.
  • Topology
    A named, reusable pattern describing how Dev and Ops responsibilities and team boundaries are arranged relative to each other — used as a diagnostic/heuristic vocabulary rather than a single prescribed design.

Arguments

Organizations should choose their Dev/Ops team structure deliberately from a reference catalog of named patterns, diagnosed against their own product count and Ops's value-stream readiness, rather than assuming a single correct structure exists. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The real goal of DevOps is value delivery, not cost/automation/config-management as ends in themselves.
  • Product count (via Conway's Law) and Ops's capability/appetite for value-stream alignment are the two named variables that determine fit.
  • Prior published work already documents several of these patterns; this page consolidates and extends that work.
Implicit premises:
  • A finite, nameable set of recurring patterns (rather than an open-ended design space) is sufficient to characterize most real organizations' Dev/Ops arrangements.
  • Diagnosing which named pattern an organization currently exhibits is a tractable and useful first step toward improving collaboration — the catalog is treated as actionable, not merely descriptive.
Inferences:
  • If product count and Ops readiness are the only two named contingency variables, the catalog likely under-specifies other drivers of fit (team size, regulatory constraints, legacy architecture) that the author doesn't name here — a question later addressed more systematically in Team Topologies via cognitive load and the reverse Conway maneuver.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What determines which Dev/Ops team structure will work for a given organization?
    Product count (via Conway's Law) and Operations' capability/appetite to align with the value stream.
  • ✅ Is there a single best DevOps team structure applicable across organizations?
    No — the page explicitly rejects a single 'magic conformation', offering a catalog of contingent patterns instead.
