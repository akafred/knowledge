When You Feel Envy, Turn It Into Something Productive

HBR IdeaCast (host Alison Beard, guest Stacy Kim) · podcast · 2026-10-20 Reader 🖥️

Leader Self-AwarenessPerformance Psychology
Envy is a diagnostic emotion rather than a vice: by shifting attention from the envied person to the specific intangible desire it reveals (the SIRN framework) and answering with small primary actions (learn, practice, ask) instead of secondary ones (tear down, gossip, rationalize), individuals and teams can turn envy into growth and collaboration.

practical · mixed — HBR IdeaCast episode 1101 (29 min), an interview with executive coach and author Stacy Kim about her book on envy. Full transcript from the hbr.org episode page: conversational prescription backed by coaching anecdotes, cited philosophy and psychology, and cultural examples.

Key terms

  • benign vs malicious envy
    Two types of envy distinguished by focus: malicious envy centers on the person who has the thing (wishing they didn't, wanting to bring them down); benign envy centers on the good or desire itself and motivates emulation.
  • SIRN
    Kim's four-part decoding of envy: Similar target (it is someone close to you, so the desire is within reach), Intangible desire (the feeling you want, not the tangible object), Relevant comparison (the domain you care about), Negative emotion (alarm bell and gauge of importance).
  • intangible desire
    The feeling or value behind what you envy (security, mentoring others) as opposed to the tangible prize (promotion, money, vacations).
  • primary vs secondary response
    Primary: go after what you want and build the skill (study and practice). Secondary: lower the other person or elevate yourself (genius effect, gossip, 'I could be better if I tried', 'I'm better at X').
  • inert envy
    Philosopher Sara Protasi's term for the hopeless, sulking state in which the envied thing seems unobtainable and the person turns inward and gets stuck.
  • genius effect
    A secondary response that dismisses the envied person's success as innate talent ('freak ability') so no catch-up is possible or expected.

Propositions

  • explicitWestern culture treats envy only as a shameful deadly sin, so people miss benign envy, a motivating form that the Korean phrase bureowoyo ('I envy you, I admire you') normalizes by saying it to the envied person.conf: high
  • explicitThere are two basic types of envy: malicious envy, focused on the person who has the thing, and benign envy, focused on the good that is desired; refocusing from the person to the desire turns envy into emulation.conf: high
  • explicitEnvy cannot be controlled but carries information, like fear signaling danger and anger signaling injustice, it tells us what we really want; suppressing or ignoring it is a mistake.conf: high
  • explicitComparison is natural and useful (it helps us join and stay in groups and learn about ourselves); competitive situations and social media, which exposes everyone's career, multiply opportunities for envy.conf: high
  • explicitWhen guilt about envy hardens into shame ('I'm a bad person') people freeze and cannot act; this produces 'inert envy', a hopeless sulk in which the envied outcome feels unobtainable.conf: high
  • explicitEnvy targets are usually similar to us and in domains we care about, so what we envy is within reach; the tangible object (promotion) usually stands for an intangible desire (security, mentoring) that should be named; the intensity of the negative feeling gauges importance.conf: high
  • explicitTurn envy into action by finding the smallest micro-action doable this week or within 20 minutes: research the person (podcasts, observation), practice one skill, or approach them with a specific compliment and question.conf: high
  • explicitProductive primary responses build the skill the envied person has; secondary responses (genius effect, gossip, claiming you could do better, elevating yourself on another dimension) bring the other person down or prop the self up and do not help.conf: high

  • explicitGroups and nations can harness collective envy: Korea in the 1970s-80s mobilized envy of Japan into competing for the 1988 Olympics and in cars and electronics, later copied and improved Japan's long-term-care insurance, and deliberately invested in a culture industry that overtook J-pop.conf: medium
  • explicitManagers cannot reliably tell when someone is envious (there is no facial expression for envy), so they should not assume it but talk about envy in general terms, using the four components and teamwork framing to shift competition into mutual learning.conf: high
  • explicitTo prevent destructive envy, pair people at different levels or with different skills rather than similar peers (Susan Fiske's practice), because we envy similar targets and dissimilar pairs collaborate; diverse teams reduce envy-driven competition.conf: high
  • explicitTeams can direct collective envy outward at a competitor or better-resourced team, as sports teams do, instead of competing internally.conf: medium

  • implicitEnvy is an accurate and trustworthy indicator of authentic desire, so acting on what we envy leads people toward what they really want rather than toward socially induced or status-driven wants.conf: medium

Arguments

People should treat envy as information and convert it into small, concrete action rather than suppressing it or feeling ashamed. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Envy is a universal negative emotion that tells us what we want.
  • Suppression and shame freeze people (inert envy).
  • Envy targets are similar people, so the desire is within reach.
  • Small micro-actions and specific compliments plus questions move people toward the desire.
Implicit premises:
  • The desire revealed is authentic and worth pursuing.
  • Others will respond generously to admiration and specific questions.
  • Refocusing from person to goal is within a person's voluntary control even though the emotion is not.
Inferences:
  • Envy-awareness practices could be taught as a career-clarification tool in coaching.
  • Organizations that stigmatize envy may deprive people of useful self-knowledge.
Leaders can turn envy on teams into collaboration instead of competition. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Managers cannot see envy reliably, so they should discuss it generally.
  • We envy similar people, so pairing dissimilar people prevents envy.
  • Collective envy can be aimed outward at competitors (sports teams, Korea).
Implicit premises:
  • Dissimilar pairings do not sacrifice other benefits such as peer learning and calibration.
  • An external envy target will not become demonizing or hostile.
Inferences:
  • Advice to prevent envy between similar peers conflicts with advice to approach similar peers and learn from them.
  • Outward envy risks drifting into the unethical behavior documented in rivalry research.

Narratives

  • Kim's adolescence in late-1970s Korea, where people said bureowoyo to their envy target ('I envy you, I admire you') as an opening to learn how they did it.
    → Cultural proof that envy can be expressed as admiration and become emulative.
  • Kim's own suppressed wish to be a writer, revealed when she examined whom she envied (writers, knitters).
    → Shows envy as a pointer to a domain that matters ('Relevant comparison').
  • Basketball players envying other basketball players: the primary response practices and dribbles more; the secondary response says 'freak ability' or 'I could be better if I tried'.
    → Concrete contrast between primary and secondary responses.
  • Korea mobilizing envy of Japan: 1988 Olympics, cars and electronics, copying and improving Japan's long-term-care insurance, culture-ministry investment that let K-pop overtake J-pop.
    → Scales the claim from persons to nations and organizations.
  • Susan Fiske declining to put two people at the same level on a research project to avoid envy, and instead pairing different levels or skills.
    → Offers a structural prevention technique for managers.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why should we revalue envy instead of treating it as a toxic emotion?
    Because it signals what we want; benign envy motivates emulation and malicious envy can be redirected.
  • ✅ How can an individual convert envy into something productive?
    Normalize it, decode it with SIRN, take a micro-action this week, favor primary responses.
  • ✅ How should managers and leaders handle envy on teams and across functions?
    Do not assume it; talk about it openly in general terms; pair dissimilar people; point collective envy outward.
  • ⭕ When is envy a misleading signal and not a reliable guide to what one wants?
    Touched on once ('so easy to chase the next thing') but left unaddressed.

Gaps

  • illogicalThe episode offers three leader levers that pull against each other: Fiske's advice to avoid pairing similar people because we envy similar targets; Kim's core claim that envy of similar others is useful because the desire is within reach and they can be approached; and Beard's idea of aiming team envy at an external competitor. Which to use when, and what is lost by preventing rather than harnessing peer envy, is never reconciled.
    Read next: Research on social comparison and team composition (Fiske, Festinger); work on peer rivalry vs collaboration in teams; Kilduff on rivalry.
  • incompleteThe case rests on coaching anecdotes, a cultural impression of 1970s-80s Korea, and cited philosophy and psychology (Protasi, Lyon and Rubin, Fiske). No evidence is offered that the SIRN framework or the micro-action practice changes outcomes, and the benign/malicious distinction is stated without discussion of how reliably people can shift between them.
    Read next: Empirical envy research (van de Ven on benign vs malicious envy; Smith and Kim on envy), and intervention studies on reframing social comparison.
  • incompleteEnvy is assumed to reveal authentic desire, yet comparison with similar others on visible markers (LinkedIn careers, promotions) may reveal status-driven or socially transmitted wants rather than values. The episode never separates desire that is one's own from mimetic desire, apart from a single caution to 'be honest with yourself'.
    Read next: Mimetic desire (Girard); ambition vs aspiration distinctions in leadership literature; self-determination theory on intrinsic vs extrinsic goals.
  • incompleteThe Korea-Japan story is told as collective envy but described as nationalism and hatred of a former occupier, and as state-led industrial policy. Whether corporate or team settings can reproduce this without the demonizing and unethical behavior documented for rivalries is not addressed.
    Read next: Pink's rivalry material and Kilduff's findings on rivalry and unethical behavior; research on out-group competition and cohesion.
