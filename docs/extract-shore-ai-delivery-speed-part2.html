Measuring AI's Impact on Delivery Speed

James Shore · article · 2026-09-15 Reader 🖥️

Goodhart's LawMeasuring AI ImpactProxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)
Because no unit of software output is standardized and raw on-time-delivery rates are corrupted by chronic estimate over-optimism, an organization can only find out whether AI is really making delivery faster by running a randomized, within-subject, repeated-measures trial that compares each person's or team's own 'with AI' and 'without AI' actual-to-estimate ratios, collected before assignment is known, under an otherwise identical process.

practical · mixed — Practical: the essay argues for a specific action (run a randomized, within-subject, repeated-measures trial to measure AI's effect on delivery speed) rather than for theoretical understanding of AI. Form is mixed — expository redefinition of terms ('delivery speed isn't productivity'), argumentative elimination of bad candidate metrics (feature/bug/story counts, on-time-estimate rate), and a constructive experimental-design walkthrough illustrated with a real dataset (Star Citizen). This is part 2 of James Shore's 4-part series (plus a CFO epilogue) quantifying AI's impact on software development: part 1 ('Are You Getting Your Money's Worth from AI?', already extracted as extract-shore-ai-money-worth-part1.json) established why rigor is needed and what not to measure (LOC, PRs, individual performance); this part supplies the constructive methodology for the delivery-speed leg specifically; part 3 (forthcoming, unintended consequences), part 4 (business outcomes), and a CFO-facing epilogue complete the series.

Key terms

  • delivery speed
    How fast a team finishes assigned work, independent of whether that work was worth doing. Explicitly a subset of productivity, not a synonym for it.
  • productivity
    Value produced divided by money spent — a strict, outcome-anchored ratio Shore contrasts with mere output or speed.
  • actual:estimate ratio
    Actual time spent on a task divided by its pre-assignment estimate; Shore's chosen proxy for relative speed, valid only once compared to itself over time and purged of the four named confounds ('snags').
  • snag
    A specific way the naive before/after comparison of actual:estimate ratios can produce a misleading result — Shore identifies exactly four, each with a corresponding design fix.
  • randomized within-subject repeated-measures trial
    The specific experimental design Shore proposes: each participant (or team) serves as their own control, doing both 'with AI' and 'without AI' tasks in randomized order, so comparisons are made within a person/team rather than across different people/teams — reducing the number of samples needed relative to a between-subjects design.

Arguments

To reliably measure whether AI increases delivery speed, an organization needs a randomized, within-subject, repeated-measures trial rather than any simpler before/after or cross-team comparison. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • No unit of software output (features, bugs, stories, points) is standardized enough to compare across teams or time.
  • On-time-estimate rate is corrupted by chronic estimator over-optimism, or by padding when estimates are consistently hit.
  • The actual:estimate ratio forms a log-normal distribution, not a single number, so only distributional shifts (not individual data points) are meaningful.
  • People and teams vary systematically from each other, introducing noise unless each is compared only to itself.
  • Knowing in advance whether a task will use AI changes the estimate itself, biasing any comparison unless the estimate is collected before assignment.
  • Process changes introduced alongside AI adoption (streamlined workflows, closer business contact) are themselves productivity-improving and will be misattributed to AI unless interleaved rather than time-separated.
Implicit premises:
  • The actual:estimate ratio, once purged of these four confounds, is itself a valid-enough proxy for speed — the essay treats the ratio as basically sound once the snags are handled, without addressing whether the underlying estimation process is stable enough across the length of a multi-week study to serve as a reliable denominator at all.
  • Participants are able and willing to have their individual estimate/rework data collected this closely without the psychological-safety concerns Goodhart's Law implies (per part 1's own warning against tying metrics to individuals or teams).
Inferences:
  • If teams' estimation skill itself drifts over the course of the study — e.g. people get better at estimating simply from practicing the protocol, independent of AI — that would reintroduce exactly the kind of within-team confound the four snags are designed to eliminate; the essay doesn't name this as a fifth snag.
This level of methodological rigor is worth the organizational cost, despite being 'a lot more complicated than just counting PRs.' strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Productivity expectations and AI spending are both at unusually extreme, order-of-magnitude levels compared to a routine process-improvement question.
  • The METR finding that engineers overestimate AI's speed benefit by nearly 50% shows that unmeasured judgment is unreliable at exactly the scale of decision this data would inform.
  • The sample-size burden is manageable given a large-enough effect and participant count, and teams collect the data during normal work rather than a separate exercise.
  • Defining precisely how people should use AI in the 'with AI' condition is valuable in its own right, independent of running the study.
Implicit premises:
  • The value of knowing — even approximately — which approach is faster outweighs the risk that the study itself creates the kind of individual/team performance-measurement dynamic part 1 explicitly warned against ('Measure Approaches, Not People').
Inferences:
  • There is an unresolved tension with part 1's own Goodhart's-Law discipline: this design is fundamentally a repeated, per-person, per-team comparison of actual vs. estimate, which is much closer to 'measuring people' than part 1's prescription admits — the 'approach, not people' framing survives only if results are reported and acted on strictly at the approach level and never surfaced per individual, a safeguard this piece doesn't explicitly state.

Narratives

  • Real actual:estimate-ratio data from Star Citizen's publicly released alpha 3.0 task estimates and actuals (112 data points), plotted on a log scale and matched almost exactly by a best-fit censored log-normal curve, with a visible 'cliff' at the 1.0 ratio mark flagged as a classic sign of work expanding to meet its estimate.
    → Grounds the 'estimates aren't consistent' snag in a real, named, checkable public dataset rather than an anonymized anecdote — a notable methodological improvement over part 1's reliance on unnamed practitioner conversations — and visually demonstrates why a single before/after number would be misleading.
  • The Star Citizen data artificially split in half and manipulated to simulate a 'Without AI' vs. 'With AI (Simulated)' distribution shift, explicitly labeled by the author as not representing real-world AI impact.
    → Illustrates, without overclaiming real evidence, what a genuine AI-driven distributional shift in the actual:estimate ratio would look like on the same kind of chart — a pedagogical device the author is careful to flag as simulated.
  • An AI-use disclaimer footnote: Shore used OpenAI's GPT-5.6 'Sol' model in 'High' thinking mode to critique his own explanation and to generate the Python code that drew the article's graphs, with a public link to the full ChatGPT conversation.
    → Models, in miniature, the same 'named, checkable' rigor the essay argues organizations should apply to AI's impact — transparent process disclosure rather than an unverifiable claim about how the piece (or its figures) were produced.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How can you measure a change in delivery speed when no unit of software output is standardized across teams or time?
    Compare each person's or team's own actual:estimate ratio distribution to itself over time, within a design that controls for the four named confounds.
  • ✅ Is on-time-estimate delivery a valid speed metric?
    No — estimates are systematically over-optimistic, or gamed via padding when consistently hit, so hitting or missing them doesn't reflect team speed.
  • ✅ How do you separate AI's specific effect on speed from simultaneous process changes (workflow streamlining, closer business-developer contact) introduced alongside AI adoption?
    Interleave 'with AI' and 'without AI' tasks under an identical, already-updated process, rather than comparing a pre-AI period to a post-AI period.
  • ✅ Is this level of measurement rigor proportionate, given the effort required?
    Yes — because AI spending and productivity claims involve order-of-magnitude stakes far beyond a routine process-improvement question, and the sample-size burden turns out to be manageable.
  • ⭕ Does collecting per-person, repeated actual/estimate data for this trial create the same individual-performance-measurement risk that part 1's Goodhart's-Law discussion explicitly warned against?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe essay never addresses estimation-skill drift over the course of the study — teams could get better (or worse) at estimating simply from practicing the protocol, independent of any AI effect, which would reintroduce exactly the kind of within-team confound the four 'snags' were designed to eliminate. It is a plausible fifth snag the piece doesn't name.
    Read next: Look for discussion of calibration/learning effects in controlled measurement studies (e.g. how the METR study Shore credits for the design handled repeated-measures learning effects), or a statistician's treatment of practice effects in within-subject trial design.
  • incompleteThis design is a per-person, per-team, repeated comparison of actual to estimated time — functionally much closer to 'measuring people' than part 1's own prescription ('Measure Approaches, Not People,' grounded in Goodhart's Law) admits. The essay never states the safeguard that would preserve part 1's discipline (e.g., that results must be reported and acted on only at the approach level, never surfaced per individual), leaving open exactly the gaming risk part 1 warned about.
    Read next: Watch whether part 3 or part 4 of this series addresses how the trial's per-person data should be governed, reported, or anonymized to avoid becoming an individual performance target.
  • incompleteThe essay doesn't address ethical or practical constraints on random assignment — work that can't be randomly denied AI assistance (or forced to use it) for safety, regulatory, or externally-committed-deadline reasons is left out of the protocol entirely.
    Read next: Look for adaptations of within-subject experimental designs to constrained real-world engineering environments (e.g. regulated industries, on-call/incident work) rather than the idealized 'virtual bag' assignment described here.
  • incompleteThe essay asserts 'it doesn't take many samples' and that 'the exact number will depend on the number of participants, the amount of variance in your results, and how conclusive you want the results to be' (footnote 7), but gives no worked numbers, formula, or example power calculation — a real gap for a reader who wants benchmark rigor over reassurance, since the whole essay otherwise leads with concrete numbers (Star Citizen's 112 points, the METR nearly-50% overestimate, the 2x/1.33x example).
    Read next: Look for the underlying METR study's own sample-size/power methodology (the design's stated inspiration), or a follow-up post from Shore with a worked example.
