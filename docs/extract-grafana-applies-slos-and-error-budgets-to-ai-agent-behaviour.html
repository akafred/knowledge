Grafana Applies SLOs and Error Budgets to AI Agent Behaviour

Craig Risi · rss · 2026-10-16 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent Behavioral Drift (Day-Three Problem)Agent ObservabilityBehavioral SLOs & Error BudgetsEvalsLLM-as-JudgeLLM Reliability FailuresSRE & Incident Response
Because an AI agent can stay technically 'up' while becoming progressively less correct, grounded, safe or affordable, the SRE machinery of SLIs, SLOs and error budgets should be extended from system reliability to behavioural reliability, with evaluations as the measurement layer, as long as teams accept that the budget is only as trustworthy as the evaluators beneath it.

practical · expository — An InfoQ news piece reporting a Grafana Labs vendor blog proposal, but framed as an argument for action: teams putting agents into production should define behavioural SLOs and manage error budgets. The author adds his own analysis (limits of LLM judges, what the pattern means for observability) and closes with a survey of adjacent efforts (Anthropic's agent evals, OpenTelemetry semantic conventions).

Key terms

  • Behavioural reliability Behavioral SLOs & Error Budgets
    Whether an agent's outputs remain within agreed boundaries of correctness, groundedness, safety and cost over time, as distinct from whether the service is available and error-free.
  • Evaluation (as SLI source)
    A check applied to an individual agent interaction that decides whether it met one behavioural criterion (grounded, fulfilled, non-toxic, no PII leak, injection-resistant); implemented by an LLM judge or a deterministic check such as a regex.
  • Error budget (behavioural)
    The share of interactions (e.g. 5% of conversations over 30 days) that may be judged unacceptable; an explicit, pre-agreed tolerance for bad behaviour that governs whether to experiment or remediate.
  • Error-budget burn Behavioral SLOs & Error Budgets
    The rate at which the budget is consumed; used to surface slow drift where no single execution fails dramatically.
  • Offline vs online evaluations
    Offline evals validate changes against known scenarios before release; online evals assess behaviour on real production interactions.
  • pass@k vs pass^k
    Attributed to Anthropic: pass@k asks whether an agent succeeds at least once in k attempts; pass^k asks whether it succeeds on all k attempts, i.e. consistency.

  • explicitAn agent can be technically available while being operationally unreliable, so SLOs extend the reliability model from system reliability to behavioural reliability.conf: high
  • explicitThere is no operational consensus on acceptable behavioural targets (85%, 95% or 99% fulfilment or groundedness), unlike availability targets backed by decades of experience; the practical route is to baseline with evaluations, derive a target from history, and tighten it as the agent matures.conf: high
  • explicitAn SLO is only as valid as the measurement beneath it: a mis-calibrated LLM judge yields false precision in the error budget, and a poorly designed evaluation can reward outputs that satisfy the criterion while missing user intent.conf: high

  • explicitTrustworthy evaluation needs representative datasets, clear definitions of acceptable behaviour, deterministic checks where possible, human review of ambiguous cases, and ongoing validation of the evaluators themselves; the SLO supplies governance, not correctness.conf: high
  • explicitOffline evaluations (validating changes against known scenarios) and online evaluations (judging real production interactions) are distinct and complementary, forming a feedback loop between evaluation, observability and engineering change.conf: medium
  • explicitFor production agents, consistency (pass^k: succeeds on every attempt) matters more than eventual success (pass@k: succeeds at least once), which implies reliability targets should be set against the former.conf: medium
  • explicitThe emerging division of labour in agent reliability is: evaluations determine whether an agent behaved correctly, observability (e.g. OpenTelemetry semantic conventions) provides the evidence, and SLOs provide the operational threshold for acceptability.conf: high

Arguments

Teams running production agents should define behavioural SLOs with error budgets rather than rely on infrastructure metrics or an aggregate quality score. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Infrastructure metrics cannot show whether answers are correct, grounded or useful.
  • Agent quality is multidimensional, so separate measurements are needed.
  • Once measured, SRE concepts (SLO, error budget) apply directly.
  • Error-budget burn exposes gradual drift that a single failure alert would not.
Implicit premises:
  • Each behavioural dimension can be measured reliably enough per interaction to serve as an SLI.
  • An interaction is a meaningful unit of account (rather than a turn, session or task).
  • Behaviour quality is roughly stationary enough over a 30-day window for a rate-based budget to be meaningful.
  • Teams will actually reallocate effort from features to remediation when the budget runs out, as SRE teams are assumed to do.
Inferences:
  • Model or prompt rollouts could be gated on remaining behavioural error budget, mirroring SRE change freezes.
  • Different agent features of one product may deserve different budgets, since dimensions are decoupled.
An SLO over agent behaviour provides governance but not correctness, so evaluator validation is a prerequisite. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • LLM judges can misclassify consistently.
  • Evaluations can reward literal satisfaction of a criterion while missing user intent.
  • Targets for behavioural SLOs have no historical consensus.
Implicit premises:
  • Human review is available at sufficient volume to validate evaluators.
  • Misclassification is systematic enough to bias the budget rather than average out.
Inferences:
  • A behavioural error budget needs its own confidence interval or evaluator-error term, which the SRE analogue (availability probes) does not need.

Narratives

  • Grafana's own Agent Observability work grew out of trying to monitor the Grafana Assistant, where changes to prompts, models and tools made traditional monitoring insufficient.
    → Supports the claim that infrastructure monitoring is insufficient for agents and that behavioural measurement arises from practical need, not theory.
  • Hypothetical drift scenario: an agent keeps responding successfully while a model, prompt or retrieval change erodes answer usefulness by a fraction of a percentage point per day.
    → Illustrates why burn-rate monitoring beats spike alerts for agents.
  • Worked example of a 95%-fulfilled-over-30-days SLO whose remaining 5% becomes the error budget.
    → Makes the SRE-to-agent mapping concrete and anchors the experiment-vs-remediate decision rule.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How do you measure whether an AI agent is behaving well in production, beyond latency, errors and tokens?
    Per-behaviour evaluations as SLIs, targeted by SLOs, with the error budget guiding experiment-versus-remediate decisions.
  • ⭕ What target is acceptable for a behavioural SLO (85%, 95%, 99%)?
    Only a procedure is offered: baseline via evaluations, derive from history, tighten over time. No guidance on how to choose the initial level or relate it to user harm.
  • ⭕ How do you know the evaluators feeding the SLO are themselves trustworthy?
    Named as a requirement (ongoing validation, human review) but no method given.
  • ⭕ Should the unit of measurement be a conversation, a turn or a task, and how should severity be weighed within the budget?
    The article implicitly uses the conversation and treats events as equally weighted.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe error budget counts bad interactions as equal units. Nothing addresses severity weighting: a prompt-injection success or PII leak and a marginally ungrounded answer would burn the same budget, so a 95% SLO can be met while the rare catastrophic failures that matter most are tolerated. SRE practice itself separates low-severity budget burn from hard-stop incidents; agent behaviour needs the same split, which the piece does not offer.
    Read next: Look for work that pairs budgeted soft metrics with hard guardrails or zero-tolerance policies for safety-critical dimensions (agent guardrails, circuit breakers, severity classes in SRE incident practice).
  • incompleteEvaluator error is not propagated into the budget. The piece says LLM-judge misclassification yields 'false precision' and calls for validating evaluators, but gives no method for measuring judge accuracy (agreement with human labels, TPR/TNR), no way to carry judge error as uncertainty into the SLO, and no treatment of the judge itself drifting with model updates. The SLO's credibility rests on an unsolved calibration problem.
    Read next: Pair with calibration-focused LLM-as-judge work (TPR/TNR validation against human labels, judge isolation from the generating model) and statistical treatment of noisy SLIs.
  • incompleteNo evidence that behavioural SLOs work. The article reports a vendor blog post with no deployment data, customer results or case where an error budget changed a ship/no-ship decision, and it presupposes SLO and telemetry infrastructure. It also leaves open whether teams will actually freeze agent changes when the budget is exhausted, the organisational discipline classic SRE depends on.
    Read next: Look for practitioner postmortems or case studies of behavioural SLOs in production, and SRE literature on when error-budget policies are actually enforced.
  • incompleteThe unit of observation is assumed to be the conversation, while other corpus material argues agent observability should be organised around the turn (a user intent fans out into many model and tool calls). It is unclear how a conversation-level pass/fail SLI attributes degradation to a cause (prompt, model, retrieval, tool) in multi-step agents, which limits what burn can tell a team to fix.
    Read next: Read alongside turn-level and trace-tree observability designs; look for attribution methods linking an SLO breach to a specific agent component change.
