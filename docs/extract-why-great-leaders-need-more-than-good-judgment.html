Why Great Leaders Need More Than Good Judgment

Harvard Business Review · article · 2026-07-21 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Decision-Making PracticesLeader RestraintMental Models & Understanding
Because the pace of change now outstrips any individual leader's judgment and experience, leaders should replace reliance on personal judgment alone with an institutionalized discipline of systematically gathering and integrating stakeholder perspectives ('panoramic leadership') before moving to action.

practical · mixed — A conversational interview built around a named practical framework ('panoramic leadership'). Argumentative core (leaders should systematically broaden decision inputs) delivered through expository explanation of the framework plus a string of anecdotal case examples (DBS Bank, Alan Mulally, Steve Jobs, Indra Nooyi, Hubert Joly) that do the persuasive work the abstract claims can't.

Key terms

  • Panoramic leadership
    The practice of making better decisions by broadening one's perspective with insights from surrounding stakeholders to build a more complete and accurate view of reality, then moving to action.
  • Internal map
    A leader's decision-making model, built from personal values and past successes/failures -- an internal GPS that decays faster than the leader notices given the pace of environmental change.
  • Chief investigator (vs. expert)
    A leadership identity shift away from being valued for having the answers, toward being valued for the discipline of asking and investigating.
  • Don't add too much value
    Goldsmith's specific prescription: when someone offers an idea, resist contributing your own on top of it, because doing so converts their idea into 'the leader's idea' and the other person shifts from ownership to compliance ('salute the flag and go do it').
  • Zooming in / zooming out
    The two-part movement of getting close to a specific stakeholder's perspective (zoom in), then pulling back to integrate that insight into one's own broader map (zoom out).

Arguments

Leaders should institutionalize systematic stakeholder-perspective gathering ('panoramic leadership') instead of relying on personal judgment and expertise alone.
Explicit premises:
  • Rapid environmental change outdates any individual leader's internal map faster than in the past.
  • At least half, and likely more, of new executives fail within their first 18 months.
  • Leaders no longer inherently know more than the people they lead.
Implicit premises:
  • Stakeholders, once approached, will provide accurate rather than self-protective or flattering information.
  • A leader can reliably judge which stakeholder perspectives are worth incorporating into their own map.
Inferences:
  • The practice should become a recurring discipline (daily question process, perspective audits) rather than a one-time initiative, since the pace of change means any map update decays.
Leaders should deliberately withhold their own ideas and listen instead of contributing content ('don't add too much value').
Explicit premises:
  • Voicing your own idea on top of someone else's converts the interaction from collaborative discovery into command-and-compliance.
  • Team members disengage from ownership once an idea becomes 'the leader's'.
Implicit premises:
  • A leader's own expertise and instinct to contribute is a bigger threat to genuine stakeholder input than any incentive stakeholders have to flatter or defer to the leader.
Inferences:
  • Restraint has to be treated as a trained skill requiring external accountability infrastructure, since Goldsmith frames it as something he personally cannot sustain unaided.
Steve Jobs's early success does not actually contradict the panoramic-leadership thesis. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Jobs's initial 'success' at Apple was followed by failure and being fired.
  • Apple recovered and has thrived since, under Tim Cook's operational leadership.
Implicit premises:
  • Operational execution (Cook's contribution) is equivalent to, or a substitute for, the outside-perspective-seeking that Adi's original question was actually asking about.

Narratives

  • DBS Bank under Piyush Gupta institutionalizes panoramic leadership at four levels: Two-in-a-Box co-leadership between operations and technology, journey-leader KPIs requiring an outside idea, and biannual half-day sessions exposing the whole senior team to outside trends.
    → Supports the claim that perspective integration can be built into organizational structure at scale, not just practiced by individuals.
  • Alan Mulally at Ford kept a management team of 16 with a named representative for every key stakeholder group (community, customer, suppliers, union), sustained week after week for years.
    → Supports the claim that the practice, though conceptually simple, is a sustained structural and personal discipline rather than a one-time act.
  • Steve Jobs 'succeeded' by ignoring outside opinion, then failed and was fired; Apple only stabilized once Tim Cook (an operational counterpart) took over.
    → Offered as a rebuttal to the 'trust your gut' objection, but the logic doesn't fully close the loop (see argument 3 / gap 1 below).
  • Indra Nooyi got personally close to activist investor Nelson Peltz -- a hostile party many executives would ignore -- and ultimately brought him together with the board.
    → Supports the claim that getting close to stakeholders applies even to adversarial ones, not just friendly ones.
  • Hubert Joly reached out to competitors and people around him as a 'day-to-day-to-day discipline'.
    → Supports the sustained-discipline claim (proposition 7) with a second, independent example.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How does a leader lead in a world that's changing faster than they are?
    By practicing panoramic leadership -- systematically widening decision inputs via stakeholder perspective before moving to action.
  • ✅ How do you balance a bias toward speed and action with the time cost of gathering broader perspective?
    Choe frames it as a matter of leader judgment about which stakeholders matter most; the practice can be compressed to minutes rather than requiring exhaustive canvassing.
  • ⭕ When should a leader trust their own gut over outside input (the Steve Jobs case)?
    Goldsmith's answer (Apple needed both Jobs the artist and Cook the operator) doesn't actually answer when to trust your own judgment over outside perspectives -- it answers a different, adjacent question.
  • ⭕ Will stakeholders actually tell a leader the truth once approached, given the power differential?
    Goldsmith explicitly flags this as unresolved and evidence-free ('zero proof... leaders are more open'), but the framework as a whole proceeds as if candor were obtainable once you 'get up close' -- the two speakers don't reconcile this with the rest of the practice.

Gaps

  • illogicalThe Steve Jobs rebuttal doesn't address the question it's answering. Adi's challenge was about trusting one's own vision over outside input during a strategic/creative call; Goldsmith's answer (Apple needed Cook's operational management to stabilize post-Jobs) is really about needing both 'artist' and 'soldier' leadership types in an organization -- a different claim that leaves the original 'when do you trust your gut' question open.
    Read next: Read against other treatments of Jobs's leadership style (e.g. any extraction touching founder-mode / visionary leadership) for a more direct engagement with when concentrated individual judgment outperforms broad consultation.
  • incompleteThe framework's core mechanism -- getting close to stakeholders surfaces the accurate information a leader's own map is missing -- assumes stakeholders will actually be candid. Goldsmith himself undercuts this ('naive... a little childish... zero proof leaders are more open'), but neither speaker revisits the rest of the framework (perspective audits, the three questions, 'don't add too much value') in light of that admission. The candor problem is raised and then dropped rather than designed around.
    Read next: Read alongside literature on eliciting honest upward feedback across power differentials (360 reviews, psychological safety, anonymous surveys) to see what actually closes this gap, since neither the interview nor the underlying book appears to.
  • incompleteChoe's AI-persona technique (deliberately generate personas, including contrarian ones, to reveal blind spots) offers no method for verifying that a given persona actually represents an outside perspective rather than the leader's own priors reflected back through the model. This is the same self-undermining selection problem already flagged elsewhere in the corpus for a near-identical practice (picking advisor personas whose blind spots differ from your own presupposes you can already identify your own blind spots) -- unaddressed here despite being presented as a solved technique.
