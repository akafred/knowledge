Research: How AI Agents Broaden the Scope of Knowledge Work

Jeremy Yang, Kate Zyskowski, Noah Yonack, Jerry Ma (Perplexity) · article · 2026-07-29 Reader 🖥️

Agentic AIAI Unit EconomicsEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessHuman as Orchestrator of AgentsHuman-in-the-LoopJevons ParadoxMeasuring AI ImpactVendor Research Conflict of Interest
AI agents don't just make existing knowledge work faster — by combining much longer autonomous execution with far broader tool and context integration, they lead people to attempt fundamentally different, more cross-disciplinary, and previously-impractical work, so leaders must redesign roles, review points and team boundaries around this expanded scope rather than just harvesting the time and cost savings.

practical · mixed — Opens as a theoretical/empirical field study (what changed when the same users moved from a conversational assistant to an autonomous agent) but is organized entirely in service of a practical conclusion — five explicit management moves for leaders. The data sections (autonomy, efficiency, scope) are evidentiary scaffolding for the closing prescriptive section.

Key terms

  • assistant / agent
    Two operationally distinct product categories measured along two axes — autonomy (minutes of unsupervised planning/execution) and context integration (rate and count of external tool/connector calls) — not marketing labels. The assistant is the low-autonomy, low-integration conversational baseline; the agent is high on both.
  • scope
    The range and kind of task a user attempts with a tool — operationalized via LLM-based classification of cognitive level ('higher-order', 'create' vs. 'remember/summarize'), count of knowledge domains touched, cross-occupational-boundary crossing, and incidence of tasks 'never even tried' with the assistant. Treated as a construct orthogonal to speed/cost efficiency.
  • matched sessions / matched queries
    Pairs of near-identical opening requests the same user submitted to both the assistant and the agent, used to isolate a same-task, same-user comparison for the autonomy and efficiency numbers.

Propositions

  • explicitOn matched sessions, the agent spent about 26 minutes of autonomous planning/execution versus roughly 33 seconds for the assistant (about 48x more machine work on average; about 40x at the median).conf: high
  • explicitAlthough the agent paused for permissions/clarifying questions far more often than the assistant (about 38% of sessions vs. under 1%), the rate at which users actually stopped it was essentially unchanged (3.7% vs. 3.4%) — people treated the pauses as checkpoints, not red flags.conf: high
  • explicitUsers were, if anything, more satisfied with agent sessions than assistant sessions (any dissatisfaction 10.8% vs. 16.6%) — more autonomy did not produce more frustration.conf: high
  • explicitAdopting the agent increased daily assistant usage by about 1.05 searches per user — the two tools are complements, not substitutes, because offloading longer work to the agent freed capacity for the shorter tasks the assistant handles well.conf: high
  • explicitRelative to an assistant-plus-human workflow, an agent-plus-human workflow reduced average task time by 87% (269 to 36 minutes) and cost by 94%, with a second, LLM-based estimate reported as landing in a similar range (84% time saved, 93% cost saved) and users self-reporting even larger speedups (median ~25x).conf: high
  • explicitThe efficiency advantage follows from a cost-structure shift: agents carry a higher fixed cost (specifying the task, reviewing output) but a low marginal cost per execution step, so they pay off on long, complex, multi-tool tasks and lose to assistants on short, simple ones.conf: high
  • explicitcontestedWhat people asked the agent to do was categorically more cognitively demanding than what they asked the assistant: more higher-order cognition (76% vs. 55%), more "create"-level work (50% vs. 26%), more abstract-vs-routine tasks (71% vs. 53%), and more knowledge domains per query (2.40 vs. 1.74, with over half of agent queries spanning 3+ domains vs. 17% for the assistant).conf: high

  • explicitAgent requests crossed occupational boundaries more often than assistant requests (59% vs. 50%), with the largest increases in cross-occupational task share concentrated in management/entrepreneurship (+19 points), digital technology (+13), and arts/design (+12).conf: high
  • explicitcontestedAbout 23% of what people asked the agent to do (up to 40%+ under a looser definition) were tasks they had never even tried with the assistant — work that only became realistic once an agent could do it, and these unlocked tasks were generally more complex than the rest.conf: high
    supportsYou Don't Get to Create Anything (Still Burning: Beck + Randy Shoup on Jevons paradox and distributed systems under AI) · p1 — Beck/Shoup predict from Jevons paradox that near-free cognition creates more cognitive work by making previously-uneconomic tasks viable; this piece's field data — 23-40% of agent tasks were never attempted with the assistant, plus agent adoption increasing (not cannibalizing) assistant usage — is a concrete empirical instance of that mechanism.
    contradictsHow People Are Really Using AI in 2026 · p8 — That piece, reading public social-media posts, found autonomous agentic operations still 'experimental and small-scale' in 2026; this piece's internal-telemetry field study finds agent use already reshaping 23-40% of task content within one company's product — a sharp contrast that may reflect a real maturity gap between consumer-facing agent features and a dedicated agent product, or the very different (and equally non-independent) methodologies each study relies on.
  • explicitcontestedA 'time and cost saved per task' dashboard is the most likely metric to mislead managers, because it rewards doing old work faster and is blind to the new, more ambitious work agents make possible — scope expansion, not raw efficiency, is where the larger returns and larger risks live.conf: high
    contradictsManagers Are Struggling to Keep Up with the AI Productivity Boom · p4 — That piece frames the fix for AI-accelerated manager overload as a manager-behavior change that can 'fully resolve' the bottleneck; this piece argues the deeper problem is structural — roles, boundaries, and review points must be redesigned around expanded scope, not just adjusted individual behavior.
  • explicitLeaders should adopt five specific moves: design workflows for supervision rather than constant operation, route work to agents by complexity/verifiability rather than reflex, build an explicit human commit boundary for consequential or irreversible actions, redesign roles and team boundaries around scope rather than headcount, and measure scope expansion alongside efficiency.conf: high
    supportsWhy OpenAI and Anthropic Are Becoming Consultants · p1 — Both pieces converge on organizational and role redesign, not raw tool capability, as the actual bottleneck and lever for realizing AI's value — here framed as redesigning roles around scope, there as organizational readiness outpacing model capability.
  • implicitBy presenting the 'second, LLM-based estimate' that lands 'in the same neighborhood' as the primary human-equivalent-cost estimate as corroborating triangulation ('we didn't rely on a single method'), the authors imply independent cross-validation — but both estimates were generated and calibrated by the same research team using models and assumptions of their own choosing, so their agreement is much weaker evidence than the framing suggests, and does not rule out a shared bias running through both.conf: high
  • implicitThe authors' own prescription to route agents only tasks whose output is 'relatively easy to verify' sits in direct tension with their own scope finding that agent use is increasingly crossing outside a user's primary occupational expertise — precisely the condition under which a user is least equipped to verify the output — and the piece never revisits the verifiability recommendation in light of the scope-crossing finding.conf: medium

Arguments

Agents change the content of work, not merely its speed, and therefore require redesigning roles and team boundaries around expanded scope rather than just capturing the time/cost savings. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Agent queries skew toward higher-order, create-level, abstract, multi-domain work more than assistant queries.
  • Agent requests cross occupational boundaries more often than assistant requests.
  • 23-40% of agent tasks were never attempted with the assistant at all, and these are generally more complex.
Implicit premises:
  • The scope-expansion pattern observed within Perplexity's own agent and user base generalizes to agentic AI tools broadly, rather than being an artifact of Perplexity's specific product design, feature set, or early-adopter user population.
  • Current job descriptions, team boundaries and 'who does what' assumptions are drawn along single-domain-expertise lines that autonomous, cross-domain-capable agents actually violate in practice.
Inferences:
  • If scope continues expanding as agents mature, occupational taxonomies (job titles, hiring criteria, licensing) built around single-domain expertise may eventually need structural revision beyond what any single company's internal role redesign can address.
The reported efficiency gains (87% time, 94% cost reduction) are a credible order-of-magnitude estimate of the value agents create when delegated action-taking work.
Explicit premises:
  • A primary costing model converts action-taking steps into human-equivalent time and multiplies by domain wages.
  • A second, LLM-based estimate is reported as landing in a similar range.
  • Direct user-reported speedups (5x to 300x+, median ~25x) are offered as a third, independent-feeling signal.
Implicit premises:
  • The three estimation methods are independent enough from one another that their rough agreement constitutes genuine triangulation rather than shared institutional bias.
  • Matched-query sessions, though excluding agent-only tasks the authors themselves say 'tend to be the more complex ones,' are still representative enough of overall agent value that the reported point estimates are meaningful.
Inferences:
  • If matched queries systematically exclude the more complex, higher-value tasks (as the authors' own limits paragraph suggests), the true efficiency gain from agent delegation could differ substantially — in either direction — from the reported 87%/94% figures.

Narratives

  • The opening vignette contrasts two identical requests for a 'comprehensive competitive landscape for a new product line' — one sent to a conversational tool that returns links and a summary the user must act on, one sent to an agent that returns a built spreadsheet, a drawn chart, and a draft memo.
    → Dramatizes the paper's central distinction — the 'intent-to-result gap' between information handed to you for action versus completed work handed to you for review — before any data is presented, so the reader already feels the difference the rest of the piece then quantifies.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Do AI agents merely speed up the same work people already do with a conversational AI, or do they change what work people attempt in the first place?
    They change what people attempt: scope (cross-occupational reach, higher-order cognition, previously-impractical tasks) expands independently of, and beyond, the measured efficiency gains.
  • ✅ Does giving an agent much more autonomy make users anxious enough to intervene more often?
    No — pause/clarification rates rose sharply, but the rate at which users actually stopped the agent stayed essentially flat, and dissatisfaction fell rather than rose.
  • ⭕ Can users adequately verify agent output when the agent is being used precisely to reach outside the user's own domain of expertise — the scenario the paper's own scope data says is becoming more common?
    Not addressed. The piece recommends routing only 'easy to verify' work to agents but never revisits this advice in light of its own finding that agent use is crossing further into unfamiliar occupational territory.
  • ⭕ Does the fact that Perplexity's own staff are measuring engagement with and the economic value of Perplexity's own commercial products introduce bias into the findings?
    Never raised as a question at all — despite the limits paragraph itemizing several other methodological caveats (sample skew, matched-query exclusion, measurement error, single-ecosystem observation) in explicit detail.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece never addresses that its authors are Perplexity staff studying engagement with, and the economic value of, Perplexity's own commercial assistant and agent products — a direct conflict of interest disclosed only as a parenthetical ('where the author team works') and never discussed as a limitation, even though the itemized limits paragraph addresses several other caveats in detail.
    Read next: An independent, non-vendor field study of assistant-vs-agent usage — academic or from a researcher with no commercial stake in either product — to see whether the scope-expansion effect replicates outside a single company's own telemetry.
  • illogicalThe 'second, LLM-based estimate' is framed as independent triangulation ('we didn't rely on a single method') that corroborates the primary human-equivalent-cost estimate, but both estimates were produced by the same research team using models and assumptions of their own choosing; agreement between two internally-generated estimates is much weaker evidence of accuracy than the framing implies, since it cannot rule out a bias shared by both methods.
    Read next: A genuinely external replication — a third party re-deriving human-equivalent time/cost from comparable session logs using an independently chosen costing methodology.
  • incompleteThe authors recommend routing agent work by 'complexity and verifiability' and building a human commit boundary for consequential actions, but their own scope data shows agent use crossing further outside users' primary occupational expertise over time — the exact condition that makes verification hardest — and the piece never measures whether cross-occupational agent output is actually being verified, or is simply being accepted on trust.
    Read next: Research on whether and how non-expert users actually catch errors in AI output outside their own domain (calibration and overconfidence in cross-domain review), which the piece assumes a verification capacity for but never tests.
  • uninformedThe definitions behind 'higher-order cognition,' 'create-level work,' 'abstract vs. routine,' and 'more complex' unlocked tasks are never operationalized — no coding scheme, no example task placed in each bucket, no inter-rater reliability or human-audit check — so a reader cannot judge how much of the reported scope-expansion is real behavioral shift versus an artifact of how the classifying LLM was prompted.
    Read next: The underlying research methodology or appendix (if published separately from this HBR summary) for the actual classification scheme and any reliability checks performed.
