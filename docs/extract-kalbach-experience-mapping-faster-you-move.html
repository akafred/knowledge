Experience Mapping Matters More the Faster You Move

Jim Kalbach · rss · 2026-09-08 Reader 🖥️

AI as AmplifierCode Was Never the BottleneckCoordination CostMental Models & UnderstandingMultiplayer vs Single-Player AIOrganizational Legibility
Because AI now lets every team execute fast and independently, the real risk facing organizations is uncoordinated speed, not slow speed — so experience maps matter more, not less, because they are one of the few tools that reliably turn scattered, siloed knowledge into a shared, in-the-room conversation that AI cannot produce on its own.

practical · mixed — Argumentative essay (AI speeds execution -> coordination need must grow -> experience maps are the mechanism) built almost entirely on a single extended case-study narrative rather than data or a survey of evidence; ends in an explicit call to action ('Protect it. Expand it.').

Key terms

  • Experience mapping
    A broad field of visualizing human experience (journey maps, service blueprints, etc.) whose real function is not to deliver an answer but to freeze a fast-moving, chaotic situation so a cross-functional group can look at it together and argue about what it means.
  • The map
    The artifact itself, explicitly distinguished from 'the mapping' as an activity — a boundary object people point at, but not the source of value. The author insists 'the value was never in the diagram.'
  • Execution speed
    The rate at which individual teams can independently build and ship work, now accelerated by AI — explicitly separated from alignment/coordination speed, which does not automatically rise with it.
  • Draft (map)
    A deliberately unfinished artifact used as a facilitation device to invite stakeholders to question and add to it, rather than simply approve a finished deliverable.

Arguments

Organizations must expand, not cut, the time and effort spent on cross-functional alignment as AI increases execution speed. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI lets each team move fast and independently.
  • When many teams move fast independently, there is a growing risk of chaotic, uncoordinated divergence.
  • Only human conversation — not data or dashboards — resolves what teams should do together in response to what data shows.
Implicit premises:
  • Coordination effort does not automatically scale up on its own as execution speed increases; it must be deliberately protected and invested in, or it falls behind the pace of independent action by default.
  • The organizational risk created by uncoordinated fast execution (chaos, customer harm, missed problems) outweighs the time cost of investing more in alignment.
Inferences:
  • If coordination effort must scale with execution speed rather than merely persist against it, organizations should treat recurring, protected alignment rituals as a fixed proportional cost of increased AI velocity, not a one-time fix.
  • Teams that are individually AI-accelerated but lack a shared coordination artifact are at elevated risk of customer-harming blind spots relative to the pre-AI baseline, even though each team is executing 'better' on its own metrics.
Experience maps (and similar boundary artifacts) are valuable specifically because they convert individually-held, siloed knowledge into one shared field of view that a cross-functional group can react to together. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Each stakeholder already knows a piece of the picture; none holds the whole picture alone.
  • The map freezes a moving, chaotic situation so people can look at the same thing together.
  • In the case study, no single team (support, billing, product) held all three pieces of the invoice-dispute problem; the map made it visible to all three at once, and the fix followed.
Implicit premises:
  • People are more likely to acknowledge and act on a problem when they discover it collectively, in real time, alongside the people who own the adjacent pieces, than when they are separately informed of the same underlying fact.
  • The information needed to solve the problem already existed in the organization before the workshop (the author states he had 'already uncovered this' in his own research) — so the map's function was composing and socially co-locating already-distributed knowledge, not discovering new information.
Inferences:
  • If the mechanism is composition of already-distributed knowledge rather than information-discovery, it should generalize to any cross-functional problem where the relevant facts already exist somewhere in the organization but are never co-located in front of the right people — a broader class of problems than customer-experience mapping specifically.
Now — before AI forces the issue — is the time to invest in collaboration, not after. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • AI acts as a 'spotlight,' amplifying whatever is already happening: it makes strong collaboration visible fast and exposes silos just as fast.
  • Waiting until AI forces the issue means the exposure and its costs happen before the fix does.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizations currently retain a real choice about whether to invest in collaboration now — this is urgent but not yet foreclosed for most organizations.
  • Being 'exposed' by AI (silos, dysfunction becoming visible fast) carries real costs — reputational, competitive, or customer-trust costs — that the organization would prefer to avoid.
Inferences:
  • Combined with the claim that AI commoditizes execution capability across competitors, this implies collaboration quality itself becomes the actual basis of competitive advantage in an AI-saturated market — a stronger, more specific claim than 'collaboration is good,' which the essay gestures at but never builds out with comparative or competitive evidence.

Narratives

  • The invoice-dispute case study: after customer interviews revealed that people disputing a charge kept receiving late-payment warnings anyway, the author built a deliberately unfinished draft journey map and convened billing, support, and product — teams at the same company for years that had never worked together — in a structured working session. The shared map exposed a gap between two systems that never talked to each other; the room's emotional reaction ('quiet, then loud') led directly to a fix and a new monthly cross-team meeting cadence.
    → Serves as the entire evidentiary basis for the piece's core mechanism claim (propositions p3, p5, p7, p9, p10, and Argument 2) — the author substitutes one vivid anecdote for a general argument or comparative data about why mapping works.
  • A brief counter-narrative/foil: 'Some claim that journey mapping is dead... static maps can't keep up with real-time data and AI-driven personalization.'
    → Set up specifically to be knocked down, installing the 'the map isn't the point, the conversation is' reframe — it functions as an implicit concession that some maps really do fail, while relocating the cause of failure to misuse of the artifact (treated as a one-off deliverable) rather than to the medium itself.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How should organizations sustain cross-team alignment as AI lets individual teams execute independently at much higher speed?
    Protect and expand time for facilitated, artifact-anchored conversation — using shared visual maps as boundary objects and recurring structured sessions as the mechanism.
  • ✅ Is journey/experience mapping obsolete now that real-time data and AI can analyze customer behavior directly?
    No — the 'mapping is dead' critique conflates the artifact with the activity; mapping's value was never in the diagram's information content, so AI-driven personalization doesn't replace its function.
  • ⭕ How should an organization decide which of its many cross-functional gaps deserve a facilitated mapping workshop, given that AI increases the number of independently-moving teams and thus the number of potential misalignments — i.e., how is this now-scarcer coordination time supposed to be prioritized?
  • ⭕ Who actually bears the cost of reinvesting AI's saved execution time into alignment activity — is 'protect it, expand it' a matter of individual/team conviction, or does it require a structural resourcing and incentive change, given that delivery-speed metrics don't reward time spent in workshops?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece never explains how an organization should decide which of its (now more numerous, since AI lets more teams move independently) cross-functional gaps deserve a facilitated mapping workshop. It shows one successful case discovered via customer interviews but offers no general prioritization method for choosing where to spend this scarcer coordination time — the same gap already flagged against extract-tbm-434-how-maps-can-hide-problems, which asks for a way to decide which organizational misalignments are 'consequential' versus tolerable.
    Read next: Leverage-points / prioritization literature (Meadows), or later Kalbach/O'Reilly material on triaging journey-mapping investment across a portfolio of possible workshops.
  • incompleteThe essay's closing advice ('Protect it. Expand it.') treats investing more time in alignment as a matter of individual/team will, without addressing the structural incentive problem: if delivery speed and shipped output remain what an organization measures and rewards, AI-accelerated teams under pressure to ship have no organizational cover to spend more time in workshops, however strongly an individual leader is urged to prioritize it.
    Read next: DORA-style incentive and metrics literature, or Cutler's writing on institutional overload, flow, and where slack actually gets funded from.
  • incompleteThe case study is a single successful anecdote with no counterexample or failure case. The author doesn't address what happens when a facilitated mapping session fails to produce alignment — unresolved disagreement, turf/political defensiveness, a room that doesn't reach shared recognition — leaving open whether the mechanism (shared visual artifact plus facilitated session) is generally reliable, or whether this instance was helped by favorable conditions (the author's own facilitation skill, a factually uncontested gap, cooperative stakeholders).
    Read next: Facilitation-failure case studies, or psychological-safety literature on when shared visibility produces defensiveness rather than collective action.
  • illogicalThe essay argues AI 'won't give you an edge' because competitors have access to the same models trained on much of the same data (implying execution-speed parity across companies), yet elsewhere argues AI lets a team's own internal functions move in many directions 'because they can' (implying execution speed is in fact a live, consequential differentiator inside an organization — the very thing creating the coordination problem the piece is about). These two claims are never reconciled: if AI-driven execution speed doesn't differentiate companies from each other, it's unclear why its intra-organizational effects are framed as such an urgent, novel problem rather than a wash.
    Read next: Compare against extract-the-organization-is-the-bottleneck and Jevons-paradox material on whether AI-driven speed gains net out at the market level even as they reshape work at the organizational level.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • TBM 434: How Maps Can Hide Problems · g1 — Partially: Kalbach's case study offers one concrete instantiation of the missing prioritization method — ground the map in direct customer evidence (interviews) first, then use structured facilitated exercises within the cross-functional session to let the group itself surface which specific gap is consequential, rather than deciding via top-down criteria in advance. It is a worked example, not a general method.
