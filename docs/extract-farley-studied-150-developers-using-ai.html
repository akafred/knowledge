We Studied 150 Developers Using AI (Here's What's Actually Changed...)

Dave Farley (Modern Software Engineering) · video · 2026-01-27 Reader 🖥️

AI as AmplifierBehavioral Code AnalysisChangeability & OptionalityCognitive DebtDORA & Engineering MetricsSoftware Design FundamentalsTechnical Debt
A pre-registered, two-phase controlled experiment with 151 mostly-professional developers found that AI-assisted code is no harder or easier for a different developer to maintain later than human-written code, and slightly easier when written by experienced habitual AI users — because AI acts as an amplifier of a developer's existing skill and engineering practice, not a substitute for it, so maintainability outcomes are determined by practice and discipline, not by whether AI was used.

theoretical · mixed — Primarily expository (reporting a pre-registered controlled experiment's findings) in service of a theoretical claim about AI's actual effect on maintainability, with a practical coda (which engineering practices determine the outcome).

Key terms

  • Maintainability
    Downstream ease of understanding and evolving code, operationalized in the study as: time for an independent, AI-origin-blind second developer to extend the code; objective code-health/quality metrics (CodeScene); test coverage; and perceived productivity (the SPACE framework) — explicitly multi-dimensional, not one number.
  • AI as amplifier AI as Amplifier
    AI magnifies whatever engineering practice and skill a developer already has — good practice compounds into (slightly) better downstream outcomes, and by analogy to DORA's findings, poor practice would compound into worse outcomes — rather than AI having an independent, uniform effect on code quality in either direction.
  • Cognitive debt Cognitive Debt
    The erosion of a developer's own understanding and skill over time from not thinking hard about code an AI generates — a slow, compounding risk distinct from, but related to, code bloat.
  • Code bloat
    Excess code volume generated because producing code is now nearly free — a driver of complexity in its own right, independent of any single defect or quality problem in the generated code.

Arguments

AI coding assistants do not, by themselves, damage (and may marginally help) code maintainability, so the real risk in AI-assisted development is not the AI itself but whatever engineering discipline — or its absence — the AI amplifies.
Explicit premises:
  • A controlled, blind, two-phase experiment found no significant maintainability difference between AI-assisted and non-AI-assisted code.
  • Experienced habitual AI users showed a small, consistent downstream maintainability improvement.
  • AI speeds initial development substantially (30% overall, 55% for habitual users).
  • Developer skill mattered more than AI usage in the study's own data, consistent with DORA research.
Implicit premises:
  • A controlled experiment isolating downstream maintainability from the original author's own familiarity is a more valid test of 'does AI hurt maintainability' than studies that only measure typing speed or self-reported productivity.
  • The amplifier framing (AI magnifies existing practice) is the correct causal model for interpreting the result, rather than AI having an independent, uniform effect on code quality.
Inferences:
  • Teams should expect that improving engineering practice, not whether they adopt AI, will determine whether AI accelerates them toward better or worse systems.
  • Organizations worried about AI's effect on code quality should audit and invest in underlying engineering discipline rather than restricting AI tool usage.

Narratives

  • The two-phase, blind experimental design — phase-one developers build with or without AI; phase-two developers, unaware of AI involvement and without AI access, evolve the code — is itself engineered specifically to separate 'did AI help write this' from 'is this actually easier for someone else to maintain.'
    → Supports p2/p3 by establishing the methodological validity behind the headline null result.
  • Dave Farley discloses that study participants were recruited largely from his own YouTube channel's audience, and volunteers this himself as a likely source of pre-selection bias toward already-good engineering practice.
    → Supports p7 and motivates the gap about how much this confound could be driving the headline findings.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Does AI-assisted code impose a hidden maintainability cost in exchange for development speed?
    No measurable cost was found in this controlled study; AI sped up initial development substantially with no significant downstream maintainability penalty, and a small benefit among habitual expert users.
  • ⭕ Under what conditions does AI amplify good versus bad engineering outcomes?
    Developer skill and specific practices (small batches, modular design, blast-radius localization, high autonomy) are asserted as the determining factors, citing DORA and Jason Gorman, but the study itself doesn't appear to test a cohort with poor baseline practice using AI — the 'amplifies bad practice into worse outcomes' half of the thesis is inferred by analogy, not directly measured here.
  • ⭕ How much does the self-selected, channel-recruited participant pool specifically bias the maintainability finding (as opposed to the speed finding)?

Gaps

  • incompleteFarley flags that participants were recruited from his own channel's audience and were 'presumably... predisposed to doing a better job,' but doesn't follow through on what this means for the study's two distinct findings: self-selection for discipline could fully explain the small maintainability improvement among habitual AI users, independent of any amplifying effect from AI itself, while leaving the null maintainability-difference finding unaffected — he raises, then drops, a confound that could reinterpret his own most interesting result.
    Read next: Look for a replication attempt with a less self-selected, randomly recruited professional sample to see whether the habitual-user maintainability edge survives.
  • incompleteThe 'amplifier' thesis (AI amplifies whatever practice already exists, for better or worse) is only tested on its positive half in this study (skilled/disciplined developers using AI) — there's no reported cohort with poor baseline engineering practice using AI, so the 'amplifies bad practice into worse outcomes' half of the claim is asserted by analogy to DORA's correlational survey findings rather than demonstrated by this study's own controlled design.
    Read next: Look for a controlled (not just correlational) study that specifically measures downstream maintainability for developers assessed in advance as having weak baseline engineering practice.
  • incompleteLarge-scale observational evidence elsewhere (623 million real-world commits) finds AI-assisted commits correlate with rising technical-debt signals across eight maintainability metrics — directly in tension with this study's controlled null/slightly-positive result. Farley doesn't address this kind of large-N observational counter-evidence, and neither study fully reconciles the gap between controlled small-N causal evidence and large-N real-world correlational evidence.
    Read next: Compare study designs directly: what differs in codebase type, task duration, and AI-usage pattern between a two-phase controlled Java-webapp experiment and a 623-million-commit real-world corpus, to see whether they're actually measuring the same underlying phenomenon.
