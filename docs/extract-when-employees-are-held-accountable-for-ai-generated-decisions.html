When Employees Are Held Accountable for AI-Generated Decisions

Anne-Sophie Mayer, Elmira van den Broek, Tomislav Karačić · article · 2026-07-22 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent Blame Attribution & AccountabilityExplainable AIHuman-in-the-LoopProfessional IdentityPsychological DebtTrust
When organizations automate decisions but leave employees accountable for explaining and defending those decisions to others, employees do not simply relay AI outputs — they mask, embrace, or develop new interpretive expertise around them to protect their professional credibility — and which response emerges, and whether it helps or harms the organization, is shaped by relational and organizational structures (access to data, opportunities for dialogue, recognition of interpretive labor) rather than by the AI technology itself.

practical · mixed — Grounded in a multi-year qualitative field study (banking, recruitment, biotechnology) but written for a managerial audience: expository framing, three narrative case studies, and an argumentative closing section deriving prescriptive lessons for leaders.

Key terms

  • masking (AI's role)
    Substituting the AI's actual (often opaque or counter-intuitive) rationale with familiar, plausible-sounding professional-expert explanations that do not reflect how the decision was actually made, in order to preserve one's perceived expertise.
  • embracing (AI)
    Actively leveraging AI outputs (scores, graphs, traits) to bolster one's own credibility with a skeptical audience, potentially to the point of automating away one's own explanatory role.
  • complementing (AI)
    Building durable, visible interpretive expertise that translates an AI's granular outputs into operationally meaningful explanations, without competing with the AI for the underlying decision.
  • explainable AI / accountability
    Reframed as a social and organizational problem — whether an employee positioned between the AI and a stakeholder can produce an explanation the stakeholder finds credible — rather than a purely technical property of the model.
  • interpretive expertise
    A new form of professional value built around interpreting, contextualizing, and connecting AI outputs to business outcomes, distinct from the expertise of making the decision oneself.
  • critical engagement
    The organizationally sanctioned ability of an employee to question, investigate, and push back on an AI decision rather than merely being expected to defend it.

Arguments

Leaders should treat AI explainability as an organizational-design problem rather than a purely technical one — building recurring learning loops, redefining and making visible interpretive expertise, and enabling employees to question AI — rather than assuming better technical explanations alone will resolve accountability. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Three companies deploying functionally similar 'difficult to interpret' AI systems produced three different employee responses (mask, embrace, complement).
  • What differed across the cases was not the AI technology but organizational and relational structures: access to underlying data, opportunities for dialogue with stakeholders, and recognition of interpretive labor.
  • Only the case with the richest organizational structures (biotech) produced a durable, functional response; the other two produced costly outcomes (customer attrition; invisible expertise).
Implicit premises:
  • Organizational-design factors are more tractable and leader-controllable levers than an AI system's inherent technical opacity.
  • A comparison across three single-company case studies in different industries (banking, recruitment, biotechnology) generalizes to AI-accountability situations broadly.
  • Employees' inferred motivations (protecting credibility, seeking visible value) are a reliable causal explanation for their differing behavior, rather than merely correlated with other unobserved differences between the companies (e.g., resources, risk stakes, regulatory pressure).
Inferences:
  • Structured cross-case comparison attributing outcome variance to organizational rather than technical factors.
  • Prescriptive inference from 'what worked in the best-resourced case' to a general leadership recommendation.
Masking an AI decision behind familiar expert language is a rational short-term move for an individual employee but self-defeating for the organization because it erodes customer trust and can drive attrition. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Loan officers substituted plausible expert-sounding reasons for the AI's actual (sometimes counter-intuitive) rationale.
  • Some customers detected the mismatch between the stated reason and their real situation through follow-up questions.
  • Some of those customers subsequently moved to a competitor bank.
Implicit premises:
  • Customers can and reliably do detect when a stated explanation doesn't match their actual financial situation.
  • The observed customer departures are attributable to masking rather than to other confounding factors (pricing, general market conditions, unrelated service issues).
  • A bank's competitive position is materially sensitive to trust built during loan-decision communication.
Inferences:
  • Causal inference from qualitative field observation (interviews, observed consultations) to an organizational-level claim about trust erosion and attrition.

Narratives

  • A large German bank (followed 2019–2025) fully automated loan-approval decisions with a predictive AI; loan officers could not override the AI but remained responsible for explaining decisions to customers. Officers found the AI's technical, rule-based explanations (e.g., 'unstable financial situation' for a customer with no debt and steady income) didn't match their expert standards, so they substituted familiar banking-expert justifications that didn't reflect the AI's actual logic. Customers detected officers' confusion, lost trust, and some left for competitors.
    → Illustrates the 'masking' response and its cost: preserving individual expert authority at the price of organizational trust.
  • A global consumer-goods company's internal recruiters (studied 2018–2022) enthusiastically adopted an AI candidate-scoring tool to bolster their credibility with skeptical hiring managers. When managers struggled to interpret raw scores, recruiters redesigned the AI's outputs (benchmarks, highlighted traits, interview questions) to be self-explanatory. This succeeded at making the tool usable — but also made recruiters' substantial behind-the-scenes interpretive work invisible, since managers came to attribute the value to the AI rather than to the recruiters who shaped it.
    → Illustrates the 'embracing' response and its paradox: automating your own explanatory role can erase the visibility of your expertise.
  • A biotechnology company (studied 2021–2023) used an AI system to automatically sort seeds by quality. Because seed experts operated the sorting machine, they could inspect the underlying image data and work closely with developers. Given a dedicated imaging lab, time to study individual seeds, weekly meetings with developers, and daily consultation with supply-chain managers, they built durable new expertise translating the AI's classifications into operationally meaningful explanations — and could identify defects the model had never been trained on, triggering funded joint research.
    → Illustrates the 'complementing' response as the most functional outcome: an organizationally supported partnership between employee and AI that keeps expertise visible, valued, and improving.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What happens to employees who must explain and defend AI-generated decisions they did not make and do not fully understand?
    They diverge into (at least) three response patterns — masking, embracing, or complementing the AI — depending on their accountability relationships and organizational supports.
  • ✅ Why do some employees mask AI's role while others embrace or complement it?
    The divergence tracks whom the employee must convince (one-off customers vs. recurring internal stakeholders) and whether the organization provides data access, dialogue opportunities, and recognition for interpretive work.
  • ✅ Does improving an AI system's technical explainability, by itself, resolve the accountability problem for the employees who must explain its decisions?
    Implicitly resolved as 'no' — the article treats organizational and relational structure, not model transparency, as the operative variable across all three cases.
  • ⭕ Why did the biotech company invest in the organizational structures (imaging lab, weekly developer meetings, funded joint research) that produced the successful 'complementing' response, while the bank and recruiting firm did not build comparable structures?
  • ⭕ Are the organizational investments modeled by the biotech case (dedicated lab space, weekly cross-functional meetings, funded research into edge cases) economically feasible in high-volume, thin-margin decision contexts like consumer lending or high-volume recruiting — or is the 'complement' response only available in labor contexts with the biotech case's cost and volume structure?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article never explains why the biotech company invested in the structures that enabled the 'complement' response (dedicated imaging lab, weekly developer meetings, funded joint research) while the bank and recruiting firm did not build comparable structures. Is the difference driven by decision stakes, regulatory pressure, per-decision cost, or organizational culture?
    Read next: Literature on why organizations invest differently in human-AI collaboration design — regulatory-stakes and decision-criticality frameworks (e.g., EU AI Act risk tiers) and studies of resourcing decisions in AI deployment.
  • incompleteNo discussion of cost or scale feasibility: the biotech-style organizational investment (dedicated lab, weekly meetings, funded research) may not be economically viable in high-volume, thin-margin decision contexts like consumer lending or mass recruiting, where the 'complement' response may simply not be affordable regardless of leadership intent.
    Read next: Operations and cost-of-service literature on human-AI collaboration at scale; comparisons of per-decision cost across banking, recruiting, and biotech contexts.
  • uninformedThe article does not engage the regulatory dimension of the banking case at all: in consumer lending, banks in many jurisdictions have legal obligations (e.g., US Regulation B adverse-action notices, EU consumer-credit rules, and the EU AI Act's explanation rights for high-risk AI) to provide accurate, specific reasons for credit denials. Masking the AI's actual rationale with plausible but inaccurate reasons could carry compliance and legal risk the article never mentions.
    Read next: US Regulation B / ECOA adverse-action-notice requirements and case law; EU AI Act Article 86 right to explanation for high-risk AI systems.
  • illogicalThe three responses are presented as roughly comparable strategic choices employees and organizations make, but the cases differ from the outset in how automated/black-box the AI was by design: loan officers and recruiters faced systems they could not inspect, while seed experts could directly examine underlying image data because they operated the machine. This conflates 'which response an employee chose' with 'which response was structurally available given the AI's design' — understating how much of the outcome variance is explained by initial system design (level of automation and data access) rather than by post-hoc organizational choices about dialogue and recognition.
    Read next: Literature on levels of automation (e.g., the Sheridan-Verplank scale) and how automation level constrains downstream human sense-making options, independent of organizational culture.
