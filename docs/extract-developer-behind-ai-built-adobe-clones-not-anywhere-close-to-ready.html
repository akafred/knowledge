Developer Behind the AI-Built Adobe Clones Says They Are “Not Anywhere Close to Ready

Steef-Jan Wiggers · rss · 2026-10-22 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent HarnessAI Confidence TheaterCoding AgentsMeasuring AI ImpactNear-Zero-Cost CodeVerifiability (as the Quality Bar)Vibe Coding
An AI-agent-built clone of seven Adobe apps shows both what coding agents now do at startling speed (539k lines of Rust in two weeks) and where the hard part remains: the project's own honest roadmap puts it at 45% ready for real work against 76% feature breadth, because verifying fidelity to a complex proprietary target, not generating code, is what costs the time, and its public 'parity in a month' promise did not survive contact with the repository.

theoretical · mixed — News analysis (InfoQ) of the PhotoCraft project: a reported case study plus community testimony, with a mildly argumentative frame (the project's own self-assessment is harsher than its critics). It argues for understanding what agent-built software really is, not for a specific action.

Key terms

  • Feature breadth vs readiness for real work
    Two separate project metrics: breadth counts surface features present (menus, tools, panels, formats; ~76%); readiness estimates whether a user can do real work (about 45% overall, 43% for a mainstream practitioner, 61% for core-feature users).
  • Agent wall-clock hours
    Effort remaining (1,100-1,800 hours to beta; 2,300-4,100 to full parity) counted as Opus 5.5 hours for one agent working sequentially.
  • Clean-room (implementation)
    In the project's AGENTS.md: studying Photoshop for behaviour and look only, never copying code, shaders, profiles or assets, implementing from public specs and observation.
  • panic_hunt
    A test that fuzzes every command with adversarial parameters to find panics.
  • JSON control channel and MCP server (agent-drivable app)
    The apps expose an interface letting the coding agent operate the running app and screenshot it to check its own UI work.

Arguments

AI-agent-built replacements for complex commercial software are far from ready despite impressive output volume, and promotional parity timelines should be discounted. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • The roadmap itself rates readiness for real work at ~45% versus 76% feature breadth.
  • 579 open issues, 22 of 25 performance budgets missed, unreliable PSD output.
  • Users report basic failures on the Photoshop clone and RAW pipeline; only the simplest app works.
  • A practitioner building a similar clone with Claude estimates a year to parity, and Thomas himself later softened his timeline.
Implicit premises:
  • Self-reported project metrics are honest and comparable to real user experience.
  • A handful of forum comments are representative of the user base.
  • Photoshop-level fidelity (PSD interchange, RAW) is the right bar for 'replacement'.
Inferences:
  • Feature counts are an inflated progress metric for agent-built software; readiness needs user-task-level measurement.
  • The same gap likely applies to other 'AI rebuilt X in N days' demos where nobody publishes a readiness figure.
  • Agent-hour estimates likely understate calendar time because human testing is not counted.
The clean-room claim is doubtful for LLM-built clones. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Clean-room doctrine normally assumes two separated teams: one writing specs, one implementing without seeing the original.
  • The project says it studied Photoshop's behaviour and look and implemented from public specs and observation.
Implicit premises:
  • A single agent that observes the original and then implements violates the separation requirement.
  • The legal and ethical value of 'clean-room' depends on process separation, not on the stated intention not to copy.
Inferences:
  • The model's training data may already contain Adobe-related material, which would make any separation claim unverifiable.
  • Open-source licensing (MIT/Apache) of the output carries unresolved provenance risk.

Narratives

  • Brandon Thomas announces on Reddit that seven Adobe clones will reach beta and ~100% parity within a month, then tells Hacker News the project is 'not anywhere close to ready' and promises to repost after beta.
    → Shows the audience-dependent calibration of AI project claims: promotional venues invite inflated projections and technical venues deflate them.
  • Hew, an open-source SketchUp replacement built with Claude: first version came fast and looked great but was buggy; each feature costs a prompt, hours of Claude, and many hours of human testing.
    → Independent practitioner corroboration that verification, not generation, dominates the timeline.
  • A Reddit user spends two minutes in the Photoshop clone and declares it 'not a replacement at all'; photographers hit RAW banding and missing Nikon support; PDFCraft just works.
    → Illustrates that readiness depends on depth of domain fidelity and that simple domains fare best.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How close is an AI-agent-built open-source replacement of seven Adobe applications to real usability?
    About 45% ready for real work (76% by feature breadth), with beta 1,100-1,800 agent-hours and parity 2,300-4,100 agent-hours away.
  • ⭕ Is the work a legitimate 'clean-room' implementation?
    Raised via commenter dissent and the doctrine's two-team assumption; not adjudicated.
  • ⭕ Can a codebase of 539k lines generated in two weeks be maintained and understood by humans?
    Not raised by the article.
  • ⭕ What does the agent-hour estimate cost in dollars and human review time?
    Not stated.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe readiness percentages (76%, 45%, 43%, 61%) and the 1,100-4,100 agent-hour estimates are the project's own computed figures against a moving proprietary target; the article does not say how readiness is defined or measured, who or what computed it, or whether anyone independent has checked it. A project run by agents that scores its own readiness may be systematically optimistic or pessimistic.
    Read next: Independent evaluations of agent-built software against real user tasks; work on the validity of self-reported agent progress metrics and METR-style time-horizon measurements.
  • incompleteThe clean-room question is raised but not analyzed: the model has presumably seen Adobe documentation, reverse-engineering writeups and PSD-related code in training, and the project observed an installed copy of Photoshop. No discussion of whether provenance, EULA reverse-engineering clauses or copyright make the MIT/Apache licensing safe.
    Read next: Clean-room precedents (Phoenix/Compaq BIOS), Google v. Oracle, and current writing on LLM training-data provenance and license laundering.
  • incompleteMaintainability of 539k lines of Rust produced in 14 days is never examined: who understands it, what the structure and test quality look like, how the 317 identities divide human from agent work. The cognitive-debt cost of agent-scale output is invisible in feature and issue counts.
    Read next: Technical-debt and cognitive-debt analyses of agent-generated codebases; the Bun Zig-to-Rust rewrite as a parallel case.
  • incompleteAgent wall-clock hours for a single sequential agent are given as the cost unit with no dollar, token or human-review cost, and no account of why the remaining 24 points of breadth and 55 points of readiness take 2,300-4,100 hours (the usual 90-90 tail). Forecasts in this unit cannot be compared with human-team estimates.
    Read next: AI unit-economics and estimation literature; accounts of how agent-hour forecasts compare with actual completion.
  • uninformedEvidence on why simple apps work and complex ones fail rests on a few Reddit and HN comments; there is no systematic bug taxonomy, no breakdown of the 579 issues, and no comparison of what kind of verification (fuzzing, corpus) catches which failures.
    Read next: Bug taxonomies of LLM-generated code and studies of verification coverage in agent-built systems.
