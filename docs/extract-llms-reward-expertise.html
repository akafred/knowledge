LLMs Reward Expertise

Sean Goedecke · rss · 2026-09-12 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Capacity to Verify AI OutputCode Was Never the BottleneckDomain Expertise as AI Force-MultiplierExpert GeneralistPrompting as Communication
The single most important variable in getting valuable output from an LLM is the user's pre-existing domain expertise — not prompting technique — because expertise is what lets a person signal sophistication to the model, evaluate and redirect its output, and supply the human judgment the model itself cannot originate, which means the human, not the model, is increasingly the bottleneck.

mixed — primarily theoretical (an explanatory claim about what determines the value a person extracts from an LLM) with a practical undertow: it implicitly counsels investing in domain mastery rather than prompt-technique tricks, and reassures technical readers that expertise remains valuable · argumentative blog essay (republished from the author's own blog) built around a single extended case study — Terence Tao's public ChatGPT conversation about a Jacobian-conjecture counterexample — plus a personal-experience analogy from the author's own programming practice, closing with a postscript that engages Hacker News pushback — Short (~950 words), highly compressed op-ed form; the argument rests on one exceptional case (Tao) generalized to a broad claim about all LLM use, plus one first-person anecdote. Bolded sentences mark the two load-bearing claims (thesis and its restatement).

Key terms

  • expertise / domain knowledge
    A structural, internalized understanding of a subject that lets a person evaluate an LLM's output, redirect it, spot what 'looks weird,' and supply the judgment calls the model can't originate — not just factual recall.
  • skilled prompters
    Used ironically/in scare quotes to name a category the author argues doesn't really exist as a distinct thing — people who are good at LLM interaction independent of what they know about the subject.
  • theory of your codebase
    A borrowed concept (linked to the author's own prior essay, in Naur's 'programming as theory building' lineage) meaning the internal mental model of a specific system that lets an engineer judge whether a proposed change fits — not general programming knowledge.
  • the human is the bottleneck
    A reversal of the common 'AI removes bottlenecks' narrative: as models get more capable, the binding constraint on value shifts to the human's ability to communicate exactly what solution is wanted, since the model already 'contains' the answer.

Arguments

Domain expertise, not generic prompting technique, is the most important variable in getting valuable output from an LLM.
Explicit premises:
  • Terence Tao gets dramatically better mathematical results from ChatGPT than the author does, despite (implicitly) using 'the same' model.
  • Tao's observable prompting behaviors (brevity, register-matching, indirect pushback, self-generated leaps) differ from typical prompting style.
  • Copying those stylistic behaviors without Tao's mathematical understanding would not reproduce his results — the substance, not the style, is doing the causal work.
  • The author's own analogous experience (a 'theory of the codebase' in programming) shows the same pattern in a second, unrelated domain.
Implicit premises:
  • Prompting technique and domain expertise are cleanly separable causal factors, such that a single case study can attribute the outcome gap to one and not the other — rather than, e.g., Tao's fame/authority causing the model to behave differently, or Tao simply being a better communicator independent of mathematical knowledge.
  • The model's register-shift (concise vs. explanatory mode) is genuinely instrumental to getting a better answer, rather than merely a stylistic artifact that could equally mislead a non-expert who successfully mimics the register into false confidence.
  • Two adjacent knowledge-work domains with relatively clear correctness criteria (formal mathematics, a specific codebase) are representative enough to generalize the claim to 'prompting' in general, including domains with more contested or subjective correctness criteria.

Narratives

  • Terence Tao's publicly documented ChatGPT conversation about a counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture.
    → Central evidentiary case study; the essay's entire argument is scaffolded on this one example, both for the positive thesis (expertise shapes prompting) and the anti-imitation move (style without substance won't work).
  • The opening anecdote about anyone now being able to write 'sort-of-okay CSS' via an LLM despite having no CSS skill.
    → Sets up the naive view ('LLMs make everybody a generalist,' 'there's no skill involved') that the rest of the essay complicates and partially rejects.
  • The author's own first-person experience of pushing an LLM harder on a codebase he has a strong mental model of, contrasted with talking to GPT about mathematics (where he has no expertise).
    → Extends the exceptional Tao case to an ordinary, relatable domain, broadening the thesis's evidence base from n=1 genius to a second personal case.
  • The postscript's roundup of Hacker News comment threads, including one raising OpenAI's own 'inexpert' math prompts as a counterexample.
    → Shows the author engaging live pushback and defending the thesis against a specific empirical objection by relocating expertise to a downstream filtering step.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Does prompting skill matter at all, given that everyone uses the same underlying models?
    Yes it matters, but the mechanism is domain expertise in the subject matter, not a generic, transferable 'prompting skill.'
  • ✅ What, concretely, does expert-level prompting look like?
    Described via four observed Tao behaviors (brevity, register-matching, indirect pushback, self-generated leaps) — but the author explicitly disclaims that these behaviors are teachable in isolation from the underlying subject knowledge.
  • ⭕ What should someone without domain expertise in a given area actually do to close the gap, beyond 'clinging on' to the LLM?
  • ✅ Will human expertise remain economically/professionally valuable as AI models keep improving?
    Yes — because the bottleneck shifts to communicating precise intent to the model, which itself requires domain knowledge that isn't automated away merely by the model getting stronger.
  • ✅ Does the existence of real mathematical discoveries from reportedly 'inexpert' OpenAI prompts undermine the expertise thesis?
    No — because OpenAI's own expert mathematicians filtered and checked the model's suggestions before they counted as discoveries; expert judgment was still required, just applied downstream rather than in the prompt itself.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe essay's two case studies (pure mathematics, a specific software codebase) are both domains with relatively clear, checkable correctness criteria. It never addresses domains where expertise itself is contested, subjective, or still forming (design taste, brand-new subfields, interpersonal/emotional judgment) — where 'domain expertise' may not straightforwardly translate into the same kind of prompting leverage.
    Read next: Compare against the corpus's expertise×verifiability framework (extract with the taste/design-judgment gap around whether taste is inherently non-formalizable) and against extract-eight-years-wanting-three-months-building-ai#p4, which explicitly names verifiability as a second, independent axis alongside domain expertise.
  • illogicalThe essay treats 'skill' and 'domain expertise' as effectively synonymous, but its own evidence (Tao's brevity, indirect pushback, and habit of proposing his own next steps) is arguably a distinct interpersonal/communication skill layered on top of subject knowledge. The essay never disentangles whether a subject-matter expert with poor communication habits would get Tao-level results, or whether communication skill is doing independent causal work alongside — not merely downstream of — domain knowledge.
    Read next: See this corpus's 'Prompting as Communication' concept, which frames good AI output as fundamentally a communication/attention problem distinct from subject knowledge; a piece that separates the two variables experimentally would resolve the equivocation.
  • incompleteThe register-shift observation (the model gets more concise and 'expert-mode' when it detects sophistication) is presented purely as a benefit for the genuine expert. The essay never considers the risk this same mechanism creates for a semi-expert who successfully signals expertise but is not actually at Tao's level: a confident, concise, expert-register answer is more likely to be trusted without verification, precisely because it now looks authoritative.
    Read next: Corpus material on the capacity to verify AI output and on interfaces that work well for experts but leave non/semi-experts under-supported (the register mismatch between diagnosis and prescription noted elsewhere in this corpus).
  • incompleteThe postscript's own rebuttal (OpenAI's expert mathematicians filtered the model's suggested discoveries) reveals a second, structurally different locus for expertise — applied downstream as institutional filtering/checking, rather than upstream as prompting skill — that the essay does not integrate into its main thesis. If organizational filtering can substitute for individual prompting expertise, the essay's individual-level framing ('the human is the bottleneck') may understate how much of the real bottleneck is organizational/institutional rather than personal.
    Read next: Compare with corpus material on how organizations institutionalize AI-output verification and expert review as a substitute for (or complement to) individual user expertise.
