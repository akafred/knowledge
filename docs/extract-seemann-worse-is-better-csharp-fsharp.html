Worse is better: C# versus F#

Mark Seemann · rss · 2026-09-08 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Clean Architecture (Ports & Adapters / Hexagonal Architecture)Modularity & CouplingSoftware Design FundamentalsWorse Is Better
F# is superior to C# along most axes of comparison — decisively so in F#'s built-in, compiler-enforced prevention of circular dependencies, which C# is unlikely ever to match — even though F# is, ironically, the least popular of Microsoft's three officially supported .NET languages.

practical · Argumentative opinion essay (contrarian technical advocacy), the second installment of the author's 'Worse is better' series — The END is practical — to change which .NET language the reader reaches for by default (choose F#). The BODY is a sequence of evaluative-theoretical judgments about relative language merit, organized as: tractability of the comparison, non-merit explanations for the popularity gap, a feature-superset argument, a single standout architectural feature, honest concessions of C#'s current advantages, and an explicit refusal to lean on the separate FP-vs-OOP argument. Unlike the predecessor JSON-vs-XML article, the author's own stated practice ('I would choose F# by default') matches his thesis rather than diverging from it.

Key terms

  • Worse is better Worse Is Better
    Seemann's series category for technologies that became popular despite superior alternatives — here casting F# as the superior-but-unpopular option and C# as the popular winner, applied for a second time within the series.
  • Functional-first
    Seemann's description of F#: a multi-paradigm language biased toward functional programming, as distinct from C#, a multi-paradigm language biased toward object-oriented programming — deliberately not claiming F# is 'purely' functional.
  • Feature parity
    The property that F#, being newer than C# and needing to interoperate with it on .NET, was by design forced to match everything C# could do — used to explain why F#'s historical position relative to C# was never evidence of technical inferiority.
  • Built-in software-architecture support
    Seemann's framing for F#'s default disallowance of circular dependencies: a language feature that does at compile time what architects normally achieve only through module decomposition and constant human vigilance — elevating a language feature to an architectural control.

Arguments

F# is superior to C# along most axes of comparison. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • F# was, by interoperability necessity, designed to match C#'s feature set, so its historical position was never evidence of inferiority (p7).
  • F# exceeds parity: its unique feature set is larger than C#'s (computation expressions, strong type inference, DRY scoping, structural equality, dependency management) (p8, p9).
  • F# has one killer architectural feature — default acyclic-dependency enforcement — that C# is unlikely ever to match (p10).
  • The .NET ecosystem is held constant across both languages, isolating the comparison to language properties alone (p3).
Implicit premises:
  • A larger unique-feature superset is itself evidence of a superior language — a value judgment applied even though the author admits the feature-space comparison 'can't even be measured' (p19).
  • The reader/architect values built-in, compiler-enforced constraints over convention-based, human-enforced discipline.
  • Ecosystem being fixed genuinely isolates the variables that matter to 'superiority,' when interoperability, hiring, and tooling maturity (which the article itself later treats as real costs) are arguably part of what makes a language good to use in practice, not external to it.
Inferences:
  • If capability-superset size determines superiority, and C# is steadily catching up feature-by-feature (per the author's decade-old prior analysis), superiority should be converging over time — yet the article asserts one feature (acyclic dependencies) that will likely never converge, without explaining why this particular feature resists the pattern that applied to every other feature C# eventually absorbed.
  • From 'F# has more unique features' to 'F# is the better default choice' skips over weighing those features against the tooling and hiring costs conceded later in the same article.
F#'s disallowance of circular dependencies gives it built-in software-architecture support that is priceless to an architect. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Dependency management is a major, book-worthy concern for software architects (p11).
  • Module decomposition is the normal, coarse-grained, human-vigilance-dependent way teams currently enforce acyclic dependency graphs, and this discipline is widely misunderstood by juniors and constantly policed by seniors (p11).
  • F# enforces the same constraint at the language level instead (p10, p12).
Implicit premises:
  • Compiler-enforced constraints are categorically more valuable than convention-enforced ones — echoing (without citing) the general software-engineering principle that violating rules should fail to compile rather than rely on review discipline.
  • Acyclic dependency graphs specifically are a good proxy for 'software architecture' as a whole, when architecture concerns are considerably broader (testability, deployment topology, team/data boundaries) than dependency direction alone.
  • The value of preventing circular dependencies is high enough to be called 'priceless' without any attempt to quantify or bound that value.
Inferences:
  • If language-enforced acyclicity is priceless, teams whose architectural pain is dominated by dependency-cycle problems specifically stand to gain the most from F# — but teams whose architecture problems lie elsewhere may not see the same 'priceless' payoff the article implies applies broadly to 'software architects' as a class.
Despite several real, current C# advantages, F# should still be the default choice for greenfield .NET development. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Each cited C# advantage (Razor/GUI support, IDE tooling, cyclomatic-complexity/coverage/mutation-testing tooling, programmer availability) is real but can be worked around or may improve over time, as F#'s feature gap with C# has (p13, p14, p15).
Implicit premises:
  • F#'s tooling and ecosystem deficits are transient costs, while F#'s core language-level advantages are permanent — asserted by analogy to feature convergence, not demonstrated for tooling specifically.
  • The value of F#'s permanent language-level advantages outweighs the current, real costs of weaker tooling, weaker mutation-testing support, and a smaller hiring pool, without an explicit weighing being shown.
Inferences:
  • Each individual C# advantage is granted and then set aside ('I should look into that in the future') rather than integrated into a net verdict — the same aggregation-without-weighing move flagged as a weakness in the JSON-vs-XML predecessor article.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Is F# worse than C# because it's less popular?
    No — popularity isn't determined by technical merit; F# is argued to be superior despite being the least popular of the three officially-supported .NET languages.
  • ✅ Doesn't F#'s later arrival (2005, after C#) mean it started out as the catching-up, lesser language?
    No — F#'s obligation to match C#'s features was an interoperability design constraint, not a sign it was technically behind; it was required to reach parity by design and then exceeded it.
  • ⭕ Why did C# become more popular than F# despite F#'s claimed technical merits?
    Partially: maturity/establishment and 'imperative is easier to learn than declarative' are offered as explanations, but the author explicitly declines to determine whether the learning-ease claim reflects innate cognition or first-exposure artifact, leaving the actual causal mechanism open.
  • ✅ Should you avoid F# because you might not find F# programmers?
    Generally not a real obstacle, though regional labor-market differences may matter.
  • ✅ Does the case for F# over C# depend on functional programming being superior to object-oriented programming?
    No — deliberately bracketed off as a separate, undefended claim reserved for a future article in the series.
  • ⭕ Will C# eventually close the entire gap with F#?
    C# is portrayed as steadily catching up feature-by-feature, but the article also claims at least one 'killer feature' (default acyclic-dependency enforcement) C# probably never gets — without explaining why this specific feature would resist the convergence pattern the article otherwise assumes.

Gaps

  • illogicalThe central 'F# has a larger unique-feature set, therefore it's superior' argument rests on a Venn diagram the author himself calls 'not to scale' and describing something that 'can't even be measured' — the same unweighed-capability move flagged as a central weakness in the predecessor JSON-vs-XML article, and in direct tension with the Best Simple System for Now view that more speculative capability and optionality make systems harder, not easier, to reason about and change.
    Read next: Re-read 'Applying Best Simple System for Now' (extract-applying-best-simple-system-for-now) alongside this piece to test whether 'F# has more features' is evidence of superiority or simply of more surface area, by the same standard BSSN uses against speculative extensibility.
  • incompleteThe claim that C#'s compiler is 'unlikely to ever' gain default acyclic-dependency enforcement is asserted, not argued — especially given the article's own repeated framing that C# has been steadily catching up to F# on feature after feature for over a decade. No reason is given for why this particular feature would be structurally unportable (e.g. because it would be a breaking change to a decade of existing C# codebases) versus merely additive, as most of the other converged features were.
    Read next: Read Seemann's earlier 'C# will eventually get all F# features, right?' (2015, linked in this article) for his own longer analysis of which F# features are and aren't likely to migrate to C#, and check whether dependency-cycle prevention was already flagged there as structurally unportable.
  • incompleteThe 'what C# does better' section lists real, current C# advantages (Razor/GUI support, IDE tooling, cyclomatic-complexity/coverage/mutation-testing tool support, programmer availability) but never weighs them against F#'s advantages in any explicit cost-benefit — each point is granted and then set aside ('I do occasionally find cyclomatic complexity useful, so it'd be nice to have tool support for that') rather than integrated into the overall superiority verdict, mirroring the same aggregation-without-weighing pattern flagged in the JSON-vs-XML predecessor article.
    Read next: Look for a practitioner cost-benefit account of migrating a real C# codebase to F#, weighing tooling gaps (mutation testing, coverage, IDE refactoring support) against the architectural and expressivity gains claimed here.
  • incompleteThe article names 'declarative programming is harder to learn than imperative programming' as a likely driver of C#'s greater popularity, but explicitly declines to investigate whether this is inherent to human cognition or an artifact of how most programmers first encounter programming — leaving the actual mechanism behind F#'s unpopularity, and thus the practical case for or against teaching F# (or FP) first, unresolved.
    Read next: Read CS-education or cognitive-science literature on whether imperative-first instruction creates a lasting bias against declarative/functional thinking, versus functional programming being intrinsically harder to learn regardless of instruction order.
  • incompleteThe claimed F# tooling deficit for mutation testing rests on information the author flags as possibly outdated ('last time I worked with mutation testing in .NET I found no F# support, but it looks as though Stryker has at least rudimentary F# support... I should look into that in the future') — the article's own concession that C# has a testing-tooling advantage is not verified as current at time of writing.
    Read next: Check Stryker.NET's current F# support maturity directly rather than relying on this now-superseded claim, especially since this extraction may itself now be dated.
