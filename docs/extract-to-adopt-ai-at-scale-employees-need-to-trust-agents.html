To Adopt AI at Scale, Employees Need to Trust Agents

Thomas McKinlay, Stefano Puntoni, Serkan Saka · article · 2026-09-09 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agentic AICalibrated Trust (in AI Agents)Enterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessExplainable AIHuman-in-the-LoopTrust
Because employees' willingness to grant AI agents real autonomy is governed by trust rather than technical capability, organizations can deliberately engineer calibrated (not blind) trust by being transparent about an agent's limitations, projecting competence over friendliness, tying its actions to users' broader goals, framing it as a subordinate helper rather than a powerful entity, and preserving human control over consequential decisions.

practical · mixed — A practical, action-oriented piece (five concrete techniques for managers) built on a narrative hook, quantitative evidence, and cited experimental findings — expository/argumentative in service of an implied 'you should do X' rather than a theoretical claim about what trust is.

Key terms

  • calibrated trust Calibrated Trust (in AI Agents)
    An appropriately proportioned level of trust in which employees understand exactly where an agent is capable, where it is likely to fail, and when they retain control — the explicit design target, contrasted with both blind trust and blanket distrust.
  • moderate autonomy
    A specific middle level of agent independence — more than minimal (fully human-gated, high-friction) but less than total (fully autonomous, unsupervised) — empirically associated with successful adoption.
  • control tower
    ServiceNow's specific governance layer for agent workflows: guardrails set in advance, real-time monitoring of agent activity, and the ability to override or intervene — a concrete architecture that operationalizes 'preserved human control' without eliminating agent autonomy.
  • glorified chatbots
    AI agents that are technically capable of autonomous, multi-step action but get used only for narrow, low-stakes, conversational tasks because employees don't trust them enough to grant real autonomy — the specific failure mode all five techniques exist to prevent.

Arguments

Organizations should proactively disclose an agent's specific, task-level failure modes rather than relying on the AI vendor's generic error disclaimers. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • People informed of an AI's known weaknesses trust it more and work with it more effectively than people who aren't told, holding the AI constant.
  • Mainstream AI tools only provide blanket, non-specific disclaimers about possible errors.
Implicit premises:
  • In the absence of specific failure information, employees default to maximal uncertainty/distrust rather than assuming competence (ambiguity aversion).
  • Employees who are told where an agent is likely to fail will reliably act on that information (e.g., double-checking output) rather than becoming fatalistic or ignoring the warning.
Inferences:
  • Because major vendors do not supply task-specific failure guidance, the burden — and the associated liability if it's done poorly — falls on the deploying organization, not the AI vendor.
Agents should be designed and framed as competent-but-subordinate helpers, not friendly, autonomous, or powerful entities. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Competence beats friendliness/warmth in driving willingness to use an AI.
  • Framing an agent's actions as 'assisting' rather than 'deciding' measurably reduces perceived privacy/power risk.
  • Microsoft's 'Copilot' naming is cited as a successful instance of this framing.
Implicit premises:
  • Perceived warmth/friendliness and perceived competence trade off against each other rather than varying independently, so an agent cannot easily be both.
  • Framing an agent as a 'colleague' (used to motivate technique 1's disclosure of limitations) can be done without implying the peer-level status or power that technique 4 explicitly warns against — the piece never explains how to invoke colleague-like candor without colleague-like status.
Human control over consequential actions must be preserved through confirmation gates and continuous monitoring ('control tower'), with agent autonomy kept moderate rather than maximized. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Moderate autonomy is empirically associated with successful adoption; neither minimal nor total autonomy is.
  • ServiceNow's control-tower pattern (advance guardrails, monitoring, override) is offered as a working example.
  • Workato's CTO describes explicit action-level permission splits ('draft but not send').
Implicit premises:
  • The correct threshold for what counts as a 'consequential' action requiring confirmation is knowable and can be set consistently in advance — the piece never explains how an organization should determine that threshold.
  • Adding confirmation gates does not meaningfully erode the productivity gains that justified deploying an autonomous agent in the first place.
Inferences:
  • Because confirmation gates constrain exactly the kind of end-to-end automation the article's own opening statistic says only 9% of enterprises have achieved, the fifth technique is in some tension with closing the article's own headline adoption gap — an inference the authors don't draw or address.

Narratives

  • A manager sets up a new AI agent 'assistant,' is confronted with broad permission requests (file access, email, payments), denies them out of discomfort, then finds the crippled agent constantly getting stuck and abandons it, concluding 'I could never make my team use this.'
    → Implicitly supports the unity by making the mechanism concrete: distrust leads to permission denial, which leads to a crippled agent, which confirms and deepens the initial distrust — a vicious cycle the rest of the article's techniques are meant to break.
  • Cleo, a personal-finance assistant, ties every recommendation to the user's daily goal and long-term financial roadmap instead of surfacing isolated suggestions.
    → Supports proposition p8 (goal-alignment increases recommendation acceptance) by giving a concrete, named product example of the abstract technique.
  • Microsoft's choice of the name 'Copilot' rather than language like 'AI can take this over for you.'
    → Supports proposition p9 (subordinate/helper framing reduces perceived risk) via a well-known branding example.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why does trust act as the central obstacle to agentic AI adoption, rather than the AI's raw technical capability?
    Cited survey/study evidence (the 3,000-person financial-adviser study) shows trust-related concerns outweigh performance concerns among adopters.
  • ✅ What concrete, actionable techniques can managers use to build appropriate trust in AI agents?
    Five named techniques, each backed by a cited experimental finding and a practitioner example.
  • ⭕ How should an organization tell whether trust in a given agent is correctly calibrated (versus still too low, or already too high)?
    The article names calibrated trust as the goal and names both under-trust and over-trust as failure modes, but gives no measurement, test, or diagnostic for where an organization currently sits, or when it has reached calibration.
  • ✅ Why do so few organizations progress from experimenting with agentic AI to genuine autonomous workflows (the 59%-vs-9% gap cited at the top)?
    The article implicitly treats this gap as a trust problem, resolved by the five techniques — but never explicitly reconnects the gap statistic to the techniques at the end, and doesn't consider or rule out non-trust explanations (e.g., organizational readiness/legibility, process design) that the corpus elsewhere treats as a distinct, possibly more fundamental blocker.

Gaps

  • illogicalThe article's two central metaphors for the agent are in unaddressed tension: technique 1 ("think of the agent as a new colleague who's open about which parts of the job they're still learning") implies peer-like candor and status, while technique 4 explicitly warns against exactly that ("make sure your team knows your agent is just a tool," avoiding language that implies it is an equal or powerful entity). The piece never explains how an agent discloses limitations with colleague-like openness without triggering the peer-status framing technique 4 says to avoid.
    Read next: Look for HCI/anthropomorphism research on how self-disclosure of limitations and perceived-status framing interact — whether a 'tool' can disclose weaknesses without being perceived as gaining agency or intentions.
  • incompleteThe article gives no guidance on how to define or calibrate which actions count as 'consequential' enough to require human confirmation, even though that exact threshold sits at the crux of both the opening statistic (only 9% of enterprises have turned agentic-AI usage into autonomous workflows) and the closing prescription (moderate autonomy, confirmation gates).
    Read next: extract-the-unsexy-truth-of-ai-adoption argues the harder blocker is organizational legibility (the company can't describe its own workflows), not trust per se; extract-ai-agent-blame-network-team offers a concrete three-phase framework (shadow mode -> read-only human-in-the-loop -> limited automated response) for expanding autonomy in defined steps, which is a more operational version of this article's undeveloped 'moderate autonomy' prescription.
  • incompleteThe five techniques are illustrated with single-company anecdotes (Cleo, ServiceNow, Workato, Concentrix, Microsoft) chosen by authors who co-produced the underlying Wharton report; the article never discusses contexts where these techniques might trade off against speed or reintroduce friction — e.g., confirmation-gate-heavy designs risk recreating exactly the 'agent keeps getting stuck' frustration described in the opening scenario.
    Read next: extract-psychological-costs-of-adopting-ai catalogs competing costs (approval fatigue, autonomy debt) that confirmation-heavy, trust-preserving designs could themselves generate — a tension this article doesn't engage with.
  • incompleteThe article treats trust in an agent as something built fresh through interaction design (transparency, framing, control), but doesn't engage with the possibility that much real-world agent trust is inherited from pre-existing institutional/vendor relationships rather than earned through the agent's own behavior.
    Read next: extract-agentic-ai-sorcerers-apprentice-tracy-bannon argues hyperscalers (Google, Microsoft) don't need to newly earn trust for agentic workflows because they already hold latent access to email/drive/calendar from before any agent existed — meaning this article's interpersonal-design levers may matter far less for hyperscaler-embedded agents than for standalone or novel-vendor agents.
