Measuring AI's Unintended Consequences

James Shore · rss · 2026-09-22 Reader 🖥️

AI Unit EconomicsAI Vendor/Cost Lock-InBurnout & RecoveryDORA & Engineering MetricsGoodhart's LawMeasuring AI ImpactProxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)Technical Debt
Because AI's unintended consequences — declining maintainability, burnout, and vendor/token lock-in — carry real economic costs but, unlike delivery speed, cannot be measured by a controlled trial and instead surface only gradually and noisily, organizations should track a cluster of imperfect lagging indicators (chiefly value-add percentage, backed by DORA metrics and token usage) and build explicit long-horizon scenario models (a maintenance-cost 'tax,' an excess-code-churn model, and a lock-in variant of both) to inform — never replace — judgment, even though the most trusted of these indicators is itself unusually easy to game.

practical · mixed — Practical: prescribes how to measure and model AI's unintended consequences (not merely to understand them). Form is mixed — expository framing (why unintended consequences matter economically), constructive/argumentative content (proposing and defending 'cost of change,' value-add percentage, and three explicit scenario models), and practitioner narrative (a personal VP-of-Engineering anecdote, a named third-party's real tmux commit-history validation). This is part 3 of a 4-part-plus-epilogue series quantifying AI's impact on software development: part 1 argued why measurement matters and what not to measure (extract-shore-ai-money-worth-part1); part 2 (delivery speed) is being extracted in parallel to extract-shore-ai-delivery-speed-part2.json and does not yet exist in this corpus as of this extraction; this piece covers unintended consequences (maintainability, burnout, lock-in); part 4 (business outcomes, 29 Sep 2026) and a CFO-facing epilogue are still to come. Several propositions here explicitly depend on part 2's speed-trial output as an input, so this extraction should be read as incomplete without it.

Key terms

  • cost of change
    A single unifying construct for AI's unintended consequences: a 'tax' on making changes to software. When low, even complicated changes are achievable; when high, even simple changes can take weeks. Not itself directly measurable — the rest of the essay is indicators and models that approximate it.
  • value-add percentage
    The percentage of development effort spent on work that brings direct business value (new features) versus 'muda' (non-value-add work such as bug fixes, incidents, and manual release cycles). Shore's most-trusted cost-of-change indicator, tracked via lightweight categorization rather than detailed time sheets.
  • muda (discretionary vs. non-discretionary)
    Muda (Japanese, via the Toyota Production System/Lean Software Development) is non-value-add work. Shore splits it further: discretionary muda is chosen (e.g. investing in cost-reducing code changes); non-discretionary muda is imposed (bug fixes, incidents, dependency upgrades). A rising non-discretionary-muda baseline signals declining cost of change.
  • Spending Efficiency / Cost per Value-Add Day
    Now defined (previewed but left undefined in part 1): the output metric of the maintainability model — dollars spent per day of value-adding work. Shore's worked example shows it initially dropping under AI, then crossing back above the pre-AI baseline and ending roughly double the baseline by month 120.
  • lock-in AI Vendor/Cost Lock-In
    The risk that rising AI token costs force a forced choice between a much larger bill, cutting AI use, reducing headcount, or switching to a less capable model. Modeled as a variant of the maintainability model, including a separate estimate for humans maintaining AI-written code once AI use is reduced.
  • mental exhaustion (as a burnout proxy)
    A 1-10 self-report scale (Energized to Nothing Left) Shore proposes folding into the development-speed study and periodic one-question surveys, since he holds that burnout itself 'can't be modeled with any accuracy' but can still be tracked.

Arguments

Organizations should track cost of change primarily via value-add percentage, supplemented by DORA metrics and token usage, rather than seeking precise measurement. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Cost of change is a useful unifying construct for AI's unintended consequences.
  • Value-add percentage operates at the level that matters (where time goes) and doesn't require heavyweight tracking infrastructure.
  • DORA metrics are a standard framework most organizations likely already track.
  • Token usage is a literal cost signal, though confounded and noisy.
Implicit premises:
  • A leader can reliably and consistently categorize work as muda vs. value-add across many teams over time without much oversight infrastructure — an assumption the essay later undercuts in its own gameability discussion.
  • The value-add/muda categorization judgment is stable enough over time to support trend comparison, despite fuzzy edge cases (e.g., whether preventing a demanding customer's complaint counts as value-add or muda depends on framing not specified in the essay).
Inferences:
  • If value-add percentage is as gameable as the essay itself later admits, any organization lacking Shore's personal ability to police classification integrity should expect the indicator's reliability to degrade precisely under the professional-incentive conditions where measuring AI's impact matters most (i.e., when leaders are under pressure to show AI ROI) — a tension introduced in one section and never resolved in the section that recommends the metric.
Because unintended consequences are gradual, noisy, and can't be measured directly (unlike delivery speed), scenario-based economic models are necessary to see the full long-run shape of AI's cost-of-change impact. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Value-add percentage takes 3-6 months to show trends and a year to confirm causation.
  • Token usage and DORA metrics are confounded by many factors beyond cost of change.
  • The maintainability model, worked through, shows a 50%-speed-increase scenario netting to breakeven once headcount cuts and doubled maintenance costs are modeled.
  • The excess-lines-of-code model, validated against tmux's real 19-year commit history, shows maintenance costs can end up double value-add costs over time.
Implicit premises:
  • A model built on assumed multipliers (e.g., 'estimate how AI will change maintenance costs') is more decision-useful than not modeling at all, even when its key inputs are estimated guesses rather than measurements.
  • The tmux validation — one 19-year-old, human-authored, pre-AI-era open-source terminal-multiplexer project, classified after the fact by a third party's LLM — is representative enough of typical enterprise codebases (including AI-accelerated ones) to justify treating its guessed ratios as a credible starting point elsewhere.
Inferences:
  • Because the model's output is highly sensitive to assumed inputs (doubling the maintenance-cost multiplier flips the worked example from a clear win to a breakeven), the real analytical burden shifts from choosing a metric to defending the model's input assumptions — a burden the essay never gives readers a rigorous method for discharging, beyond 'wisdom of the crowd' and a single historical validation case.
Value-add percentage should still be used — 'with caution' — despite being unusually easy to game. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Gaming value-add percentage is real, and can be entirely non-fraudulent (a leader deferring maintenance to look good ahead of a career move).
  • There is no metric immune to gaming.
  • Part 1's general remedy — measure approaches, not people — is offered as the mitigation.
Implicit premises:
  • The 'measure approaches, not people' discipline, designed in part 1 primarily as a remedy for individual/team activity metrics like PR count (where a worker games a target imposed on them), transfers cleanly to a leadership-level resource-allocation metric like value-add percentage, even though the incentive to distort it here comes from an individual leader's own career interest, not from a worker responding to an externally imposed target.
Inferences:
  • If value-add percentage's gaming vector is the leader's own career incentive rather than a worker's response to a target set by someone else, then 'measure the approach, not the person' does not neutralize the risk the way it does for PR count — because the person choosing what to measure and report, and the person with incentive to distort it, are the same individual. The essay does not address this structural difference, leaving its headline remedy weaker here than in the case it was originally designed for.

Narratives

  • Shore's personal VP-of-Engineering anecdote: he made decreasing muda a priority and doubled value-add percentage over three years, freeing people for substantial new initiatives without increasing headcount.
    → Supports the claim that value-add percentage is a genuinely useful, trustworthy lagging indicator despite its noise — the essay's sole direct evidence for recommending it.
  • The open-source tmux project's real 19-year commit history, classified as value-add or maintenance by an LLM at the direction of external contributor Christophe Stjernlöf, closely matched Shore's a priori guessed churn ratios.
    → Validates (partially) the excess-lines-of-code maintenance model — supplies a named, checkable, non-anecdotal data source, unlike most of the essay's other evidence.
  • A worked numeric scenario: AI increases speed by 50%, but token costs force a 25% headcount cut and maintenance costs are estimated to double, resulting in only breaking even despite the large raw performance improvement.
    → Illustrates the maintainability model's core warning that unintended consequences can fully erase an apparently large productivity win.
  • The hypothetical 'demanding and crotchety customer' who asks 'why didn't you just do it right the first time?' and 'why don't you just ship it already?'
    → Defines and makes concrete what counts as muda (bug fixes, releases) versus value-add work, even when that work is necessary or valuable.
  • The unnamed hypothetical leader who defers maintenance for a couple of years to inflate their value-add percentage, lines up a bigger title at a different company, and leaves a successor holding the bag.
    → Illustrates value-add percentage's gaming risk and its delayed-consequence structure — the damage surfaces only after the person responsible has already left.
  • The 1-10 mental-exhaustion scale (Energized to Nothing Left), illustrated with an AI-generated image (explicitly disclaimed as made with GPT-5.6 Sol).
    → Operationalizes an otherwise 'can't be modeled with any accuracy' construct (burnout) into something concretely trackable via self-report.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Does maintainability (and other unintended consequences) actually matter, or is it a 'namby-pamby' concern?
    Yes — because of real, concrete downstream economic costs: slower future work, attrition/hiring costs, and forced big-bill-or-cutback lock-in tradeoffs.
  • ⭕ If AI is doing the maintenance, does human comprehension of AI-generated code still matter?
    Explicitly left open — Shore reports disagreement among practitioners and states there isn't much actual evidence either way.
  • ✅ How do you measure something as gradual and fuzzy as unintended consequences, when a controlled trial (like part 2's speed study) isn't available?
    Via a cluster of noisy lagging indicators (chiefly value-add percentage, plus DORA metrics and token usage) that inform, not drive, judgment, supplemented by explicit scenario models.
  • ✅ Which cost-of-change indicator is most trustworthy?
    Value-add percentage, despite its noise, its 3-6 month lag, and its high gameability.
  • ✅ How do you model AI's effect on long-run maintenance cost, lock-in exposure, and burnout when none can be precisely measured?
    Three explicit models — maintainability-as-tax, excess-lines-of-code (validated against tmux data), and a lock-in variant of the maintainability model. Burnout is explicitly stated as not modelable with accuracy, only trackable.
  • ⭕ Does the essay's own recommended fix for metric-gaming ('measure approaches, not people,' from part 1) actually neutralize value-add percentage's gameability, given that here the gaming vector is a leader's own career incentive rather than a worker responding to an externally imposed target?
