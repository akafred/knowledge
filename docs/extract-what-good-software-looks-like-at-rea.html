What good software looks like at REA

Alison Rosewarne · article · 2018-06-12 Reader 🖥️

Changeability & OptionalityCode QualityModularity & CouplingPlatform EngineeringSociotechnical ArchitectureSoftware Design Fundamentals
What makes software 'good' at REA is not a single abstract ideal but three concrete, criteria-based lenses — dev (changeability), ops (operability), and arch (bounded responsibility) — that the teams who own each system rate themselves against on a recurring basis, so architectural quality becomes an organizationally-supported habit rather than a one-time design decision.

practical · mixed — Primarily expository — it documents an existing internal practice — but structured argumentatively: it justifies why three lenses (not one, not many), why quality/security are folded rather than separate, and why the custodianship/devops/microservices trio matters. Ultimately practical: it argues for a way of assessing and driving change in software, not just for understanding it.

Key terms

  • Dev (lens)
    The changeability quality of a system: can a developer set up the codebase, understand it, and confidently make changes to it.
  • Ops (lens)
    The operability/reliability quality of a system: can it be deployed, understood along with its dependencies, recovered from disaster, and verified against SLAs.
  • Arch (lens)
    Whether a system encapsulates a single responsibility behind a clearly defined interface within understood domain boundaries — explicitly called out as cross-cutting (unlike quality/security, which are folded into the other two lenses) because architecture was judged to need its own forum for debate.
  • Application custodian
    The team (within a cross-functional 'tribe') that owns a system's architecture, technical decisions and quality; must approve any major change proposed by another team, and performs the quarterly health rating.
  • Health rating
    The quarterly output of a custodian's review of their system against the dev/ops/arch criteria; tracked centrally in a web UI and feeds into planning and other activities.
  • Internally open source
    All REA systems are visible and editable via pull request by any team internally, though the custodian retains sole approval authority over major changes.
  • Cattle not pets
    Used as a specific 'meets expectations' criterion under the Ops lens: infrastructure should be disposable/replaceable and managed via automation/infra-as-code, not hand-nursed individual servers.

Arguments

Software at REA should be assessed and driven toward change using exactly three named lenses (dev, ops, arch) rather than a single good/bad judgment or a longer list of many narrow categories. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • REA's software spans very different types of systems (internal tools, high-traffic mobile apps, big-data engines) with different functional/non-functional requirements.
  • Despite this variety, there is a consistent underlying set of expectations and architectural principles across all of them.
  • Capturing that shared consensus in succinct, high-level categories creates a foundation for discussion regardless of team, technology or timeframe.
Implicit premises:
  • A workable assessment framework must generalize across heterogeneous systems while staying concrete enough to drive real decisions — three broad, named lenses hit that balance where a single universal metric would be too vague and dozens of narrow ones too granular to sustain as a shared vocabulary.
  • Giving the underlying philosophy short, memorable names ('dev,' 'ops,' 'arch') has organizational value independent of the content behind them — it lets otherwise disparate teams have the same conversation.
Inferences:
  • Because each lens is phrased as a question from the perspective of someone encountering the system anew ("Can I setup...", "Can I deploy..."), the framework implicitly optimizes for onboarding and handoff — for a newcomer's or successor's experience of the system — more than for the original author's experience while building it.
The application custodianship model — distributed authorship via pull requests but centralized architectural accountability in a single owning team — is the right way to govern software quality at REA's scale. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • All systems are internally open source; any team may submit a change via pull request.
  • The application custodian nonetheless owns the architecture, technical decisions and quality of the system, and their approval is required for any major change.
  • Custodians perform the quarterly review and produce the health rating.
Implicit premises:
  • Distributed authorship and centralized accountability are compatible because architectural integrity is actually protected at the approval gate, not the authorship gate.
  • Someone must be accountable for a system's health or the published criteria remain aspirational rather than enforced — evidenced by the framework itself listing 'custodian assigned but not performing the role' as a failure mode.
Inferences:
  • This implies architecture ownership is fundamentally a people/accountability problem before it is a technical one: the same written criteria produce different real-world outcomes depending purely on whether an accountable custodian is actively exercising the role.

Narratives

  • REA's own 20-year history: from a single language (Perl) to a polyglot stack, from a garage-sized team to hundreds of engineers across continents, from ad-hoc servers to data centres to cloud.
    → Makes the 'why now' of the three big changes (micro-services, devops, application custodianship) causally legible as organic adaptation to scale, rather than a top-down fashion choice — supports the propositions about devops-as-enabler and microservices-as-response-to-monolith-pain.
  • The image of old monolithic systems that "typically could only be tested (manually or automatically) with specific versions of their monolith friends."
    → A vivid, concrete stand-in for the abstract case against tight coupling — instead of arguing for loose coupling in the abstract, it shows the specific pain (version-coordinated, fragile testing across coupled monoliths) that the arch lens's 'loosely coupled' criterion is designed to prevent.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What does 'good software' mean across radically different types of systems (internal tools vs. high-traffic consumer apps vs. big-data processing engines)?
    Operationalized (not philosophically settled) via the three-lens framework with tiered, bulleted criteria that apply regardless of the system's type or technology.
  • ✅ How do you keep architectural quality from decaying once a system has shipped?
    Periodic (quarterly) custodian review against fixed criteria, producing a tracked health rating.
  • ✅ How do you reconcile open internal collaboration (any team can submit a pull request) with the need for a single point of architectural accountability?
    The custodian retains sole approval authority for major changes regardless of who authored the pull request.
  • ✅ Should quality and security be first-class lenses of their own, alongside dev/ops/arch?
    Explicitly debated and rejected — folded into the existing three lenses as cross-cutting concerns, while arch itself is retained as a separately called-out lens despite also being cross-cutting.
  • ⭕ Does human review capacity (custodians performing quarterly reviews) scale as the number of systems and teams grows?
