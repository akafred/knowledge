Don't Let AI Make Bad Analytics Worse

Steven Tadelis · article · 2026-07-27 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI as AmplifierDecision-Grade AnalyticsDecision-Making PracticesGuardrailsHuman-in-the-LoopPsychological Safety
AI will make analytics abundant but not automatically better, so companies must deliberately build a 'decision-grade' operating model — starting every analysis from the decision it should improve, labeling descriptive/predictive/causal questions correctly, exposing assumptions and counterfactuals, governing metric definitions, risk-triaging validation of high-stakes claims, and rewarding truth-seeking over persuasion — or AI will simply accelerate existing analytical dysfunction.

practical · argumentative — The author argues for an organizational operating model (a six-part 'decision-grade analytics' discipline) that companies should adopt before scaling AI analytics — every section builds toward specific managerial actions, not toward a descriptive theory of analytics.

Key terms

  • self-service analytics
    An analytics model that starts with the user's question ('what do you want to know?') and optimizes for giving people fast access to answers.
  • decision-service analytics Decision-Grade Analytics
    An analytics model that starts one step earlier than self-service — 'what decision are you trying to improve?' — and designs the measurement (comparison, causal question, experiment) around that decision.
  • decision-grade Decision-Grade Analytics
    A standard analytics output should meet before it is allowed to influence a real decision — comparable to 'investment-grade' as a quality threshold rather than a binary correct/incorrect label.
  • labeling the question Decision-Grade Analytics
    Explicitly classifying an analytics output as descriptive (what happened), predictive (what is likely to happen), or causal (what will happen if we intervene) before treating it as evidence for a decision.
  • incrementality Decision-Grade Analytics
    The portion of an observed outcome (e.g., a purchase) that would not have happened without the intervention (e.g., an ad) — the object a causal, counterfactual analysis is trying to isolate.

  • explicitAs AI analytics scales, companies sort into three tiers by what they optimize for: most use it for efficiency (more automated queries, faster turnaround), better companies use it for reliability (semantic layers, documentation, provenance, validation), and the best use it for decision quality (redesigning analytics around the decisions that matter).conf: high

  • explicitCognitive tools like AI substitute for implementation skill while increasing the value of judgment, but in analytics specifically the pivotal managerial question is which organizational routines convert judgment into decision-grade evidence.conf: high
  • explicitBefore asking AI to analyze data, leaders should specify the decision the analysis is meant to improve, what action might follow, and what evidence would change their mind — without this discipline, analytics produces interesting facts or motivated reasoning with better charts.conf: high
  • explicitDescriptive, predictive, and causal questions require different standards of evidence, and AI systems tend to blur these distinctions by answering a descriptive or predictive question in a way that gets treated as causal evidence.conf: high
  • explicitGood analysis exposes its own assumptions and counterfactuals by default; every AI-generated answer should carry a 'reason to trust this' and a 'reason to be cautious.'conf: high

Arguments

Companies should build a six-part decision-grade operating model around AI analytics rather than treating AI as a plug-in that answers more questions faster.
Explicit premises:
  • AI can reduce rote work and free data teams for higher-value causal/strategic work.
  • That upside does not arrive automatically.
  • Without discipline, more automated answers produce more interesting facts or motivated-reasoning-with-charts, not better decisions.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizations do not currently have this discipline by default — the six parts are each framed as something companies must deliberately add, implying the default state lacks them.
  • The volume of analysis a company can produce and the quality of the decisions it makes are not the same axis, and increasing the former does nothing to the latter without added structure.
Inferences:
  • If a company adopts AI analytics without any of the six practices, its decision quality could plausibly get worse even as its analytics output increases — since faster wrong answers are more dangerous than slower wrong answers when nothing filters them before they reach a decision.
Companies need a risk-based triage system for validating AI-generated analytics claims, modeled on bank model-risk-management.
Explicit premises:
  • Banks already use risk-based prioritization: models with greater complexity or materiality get deeper, more frequent validation.
  • Not every AI-generated answer needs a committee, but some — those touching budgets, pricing, personnel, regulatory claims — do.
Implicit premises:
  • The banking analogy transfers cleanly from regulated financial models (built for a narrow, well-specified purpose, under external regulatory pressure) to general-purpose AI analytics assistants (broad, conversational, unregulated) — the article does not establish this transfer holds.
  • Organizations can reliably identify in advance which questions are 'high-stakes' before seeing the answer, even though stakes often only become clear once a surprising or actionable answer appears.
Inferences:
  • A company adopting this triage would need to define, in advance and independent of any specific answer, what counts as budget/pricing/personnel/regulatory-adjacent — a nontrivial governance exercise the article gestures at but does not specify.

Narratives

  • eBay's paid-search branded-keyword advertising looked highly effective by standard metrics, until Tadelis and colleagues tested the counterfactual with holdout experiments and found much of the apparent value was not incremental — after which eBay made counterfactual testing central to evaluating marketing spend.
    → Concretely demonstrates the difference between a descriptive/correlational metric ('branded search converts well') and a causal, counterfactual-tested one ('branded search caused incremental purchases') — grounding the abstract 'label the question' and 'expose counterfactuals' principles in a specific, high-stakes real-world reversal.
  • Airbnb, as it scaled, found different teams giving different answers to basic business questions because of inconsistent tables and metric definitions, and built Minerva as a single-source-of-truth metrics platform.
    → Supports the claim that self-service analytics increases (not decreases) the need for metric governance — shows what happens when democratized answers proceed without matching definitional discipline.
  • Amazon's Working Backwards process (writing a mock press release/FAQ before building a product) and its 'Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit' leadership principle, both drawn from the author's personal experience there.
    → The Working Backwards story supports 'start with the decision' (forcing clarity before debating evidence); the Disagree and Commit story supports 'reward truth-seeking' (people were expected, not merely permitted, to challenge a leader's reading of the evidence).

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why doesn't giving more people AI-powered access to data analysis automatically produce better business decisions?
    Because access and decision quality are different axes; without decision-first framing, question-type labeling, exposed assumptions, metric governance, risk-based validation, and truth-seeking incentives, more answers just means more (possibly wrong or irrelevant) answers.
  • ⭕ How should an organization decide which AI-generated analytics claims need human validation before acting, versus which can be trusted at face value?
    The article proposes the model-risk-management analogy (triage by stakes: budget, pricing, personnel, regulatory) but does not specify the actual mechanics of the triage — who decides stakes in advance, how disputes about classification get resolved, or how to catch high-stakes answers that arrive informally rather than through a designated review channel.
  • ⭕ Whether the six-part operating model works as a bundle or whether some parts matter more than others, and in what sequence a company without any of them should adopt them.
    Not addressed — the six parts are presented as a flat, unordered list with no discussion of dependencies, sequencing, or relative leverage.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article never specifies how an organization identifies a 'high-stakes' analysis before seeing its answer. Real decisions often become high-stakes only in retrospect (an innocuous-looking metric turns out to justify a major reallocation once a VP notices it), so a triage gate defined purely by topic (budgets, pricing, personnel, regulatory) will miss high-stakes claims that arrive through informal channels — a Slack-shared chart, a throwaway dashboard query — before any formal review point exists.
    Read next: Model-risk-management literature on how banks classify model materiality and handle 'shadow models' that escape formal inventories would show whether this same blind spot appears in finance and how (or whether) it gets closed there.
  • incompleteThe piece asserts that AI can 'blur' the descriptive/predictive/causal distinction but does not explain the mechanism by which it does so — is it that AI models are trained on correlational data and cannot distinguish causal from correlational patterns without explicit experimental data, or is it purely a communication/framing failure by users who ask loosely worded questions? These have different fixes (better model training and disclosure vs. better user education and question templates).
    Read next: Causal-inference-in-ML literature (e.g., work on distinguishing observational from interventional/counterfactual queries in LLM-based analytics assistants) would clarify whether this is a solvable interface problem or a deeper representational limitation.
  • incompleteThe six-part model is presented as equally applicable across company sizes and maturities, but the case narratives (Amazon, eBay, Airbnb) are all large, resource-rich organizations that could build dedicated platforms (Minerva) or run large-scale randomized experiments. The article doesn't address whether a small company without a data-science team or a marketing-experimentation budget can implement 'expose counterfactuals' or 'govern the metrics' at any meaningful fidelity, or what a minimum-viable version looks like.
    Read next: Practitioner writing on lightweight/low-cost causal inference and metrics governance for resource-constrained teams would test whether the model degrades gracefully or requires the scale these examples assume.
