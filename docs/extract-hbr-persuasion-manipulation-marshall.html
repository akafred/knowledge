When Persuasion Is (and Isn't) Manipulation

Eben Harrell (interviewing Colin Marshall) · article · 2026-09-14 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI as AmplifierEthical Persuasion vs. ManipulationLeader RestraintManaging Up & InfluencePsychological SafetyTrust
Persuasion is ethical, not inherently manipulative, when it treats the other person as a reasoning partner — engaged through honest reasons, respect, and compassion under conditions of real attention and trust — rather than as a psychological system to be managed, and this same criterion, not any particular technique or tool (including AI), is what determines whether an act of influence helps or harms.

practical · mixed — A Q&A interview that is practical in aim (how should you try to change someone's mind) but theoretical in method: Marshall first has to define manipulation and distinguish it from legitimate persuasion before any prescription follows. The two narrative anecdotes (Rogers, Davis) do argumentative work the abstract claims don't do alone.

Key terms

  • Manipulation
    Treating another person as a psychological system to be managed — through flattery, social pressure, selective storytelling, emotional triggers, or 'bullshit' — rather than as a reasoning partner. Defined by the persuader's stance and the target's actual interests/consent, not by which named technique is used.
  • Reasoning partner Ethical Persuasion vs. Manipulation
    The status Marshall says ethical persuasion must accord the other person: someone who can 'reason, feel, resist, misunderstand, and still deserve to be engaged honestly' — the structural opposite of a 'psychological system to be managed.'
  • Ethical amplifier Ethical Persuasion vs. Manipulation
    Marshall's claim about AI specifically: it does not introduce a new category of ethical risk to persuasion, but scales whatever the human user's underlying honesty and intent already are — 'it can make bad things worse, and it can make some good things better.'
  • Respect and compassion
    Not mere politeness, but an operational checklist: offering reasons sincerely, remaining open to being persuaded yourself, giving the other person room to live their own life, preserving your own self-respect, and attending to both parties' emotional limits.

Arguments

No persuasion technique is inherently manipulative or inherently ethical; the moral status of an act of influence is determined by context, intent, and relational stance, not by the mechanism used to produce belief or behavior change. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Cialdini's and Voss's techniques 'can be acceptable' — none is inherently wrong.
  • The same foot-in-the-door escalation can be innocuous or resentment-producing depending on whether it 'attends to moral values' and takes the other person seriously.
Implicit premises:
  • The moral status of an influence attempt is determined by the totality of the persuader's intent and attentiveness to the target's interests, not by the causal mechanism (e.g., incremental commitment, emotional appeal) used to produce the outcome.
  • A target's own after-the-fact endorsement or resentment ('they might resent you for it') is treated as evidence about whether the technique was used ethically in that instance.
Inferences:
  • Since the same instrument can be innocent or exploitative depending on stance, there is no fixed list of 'manipulative techniques' a reader can learn to avoid; recognizing a named tactic (e.g. foot-in-the-door) in the wild tells you nothing on its own about whether it is being used ethically in that particular case — which limits how useful a taxonomy of tactics (Cialdini's, or Marshall's own book) can be as a detection tool for targets, as opposed to a self-check for persuaders.
Ethical persuasion is not reducible to politeness; it requires meeting several distinct, non-fungible criteria at once — sincerity, openness to being persuaded, giving the other person room to live their own life, self-respect, and attention to emotional limits on both sides. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • "the task is to engage with respect and compassion—to offer your reasons sincerely, remain open to learning from them, give them room to live their life, preserve your own self-respect, and attend to both their emotional limits and your own."
Implicit premises:
  • Respect and compassion function as structural constraints on legitimate influence, not merely as affective postures ('being nice').
Inferences:
  • This implies flattery-based or purely-polite persuasion can still be manipulative (flattery is explicitly named as a manipulative tactic earlier in the interview), so politeness and ethical persuasion are distinct, non-overlapping axes — a scrupulously polite persuader can fail every other criterion on the list.
The time horizon of a persuasion attempt (a ten-minute Senate testimony versus a years-long relationship) is not itself an ethical variable; what matters in both cases is whether the persuader maintains the reasoning-partner stance throughout.
Explicit premises:
  • Rogers changed the atmosphere of the room 'in less than ten minutes' by demonstrating warmth and competence and not overselling himself.
  • Davis spent 'decades' in sustained relationship and repeated conversation with Klan members.
  • Marshall explicitly frames these as showing 'different sides of the argument.'
Implicit premises:
  • Speed of attitude change is not, on its own, evidence of manipulation — a fast conversion via demonstrated warmth and competence is not inherently more suspect than a slow one via sustained relationship.
Inferences:
  • This undercuts a common lay heuristic — 'if it worked that fast, something manipulative must have happened' — under Marshall's framework, since Rogers' speed came from honest, minimal self-presentation rather than from any bypass of Pastore's reasoning.
AI does not introduce new ethical questions into persuasion; it magnifies whichever relationship the human user already has to their own reasons, so the locus of ethical responsibility stays entirely with the human, before the tool is ever invoked.
Explicit premises:
  • Outsourcing an argument to AI when you 'just have a hunch' forfeits honestly presenting your own reasoning.
  • Using AI to help express something you already believe and have reasoned through (the colleague's email) is not ethically compromised.
Implicit premises:
  • The ethically load-bearing fact is whether the human had already done the reasoning work before invoking AI — a fact about the human's private process, not about the AI or the resulting text.
  • AI cannot be configured, prompted, or restricted in a way that makes outsourced reasoning ethical, because the defect (not having reasoned it through) exists upstream of anything the tool does.
Inferences:
  • If the ethically decisive fact is unobservable from outside (whether the human reasoned it through first, versus backfilled a hunch), then persuasion ethics in the AI era cannot be enforced by inspecting outputs — two textually identical AI-assisted messages could be ethical or manipulative depending entirely on a private fact about their author, which has real implications for any organization trying to write AI-use policy for internal communications or change-management messaging.

Narratives

  • Fred Rogers testifies before Senator John Pastore's Senate subcommittee in 1969, defending public-broadcasting funding. Pastore initially seems unimpressed; Rogers doesn't claim budget or policy expertise, speaks sincerely about what his show does for children's emotional lives, and opens by declining to read his prepared statement out of consideration for senators who'd already sat through hours of testimony. He changes the room's atmosphere in under ten minutes.
    → Supports p4 (warmth + competence, honestly and minimally presented, work even under high-stakes time pressure) and illustrates that respect (trusting the senators to read the statement themselves) can itself be a persuasive act rather than a concession of ground.
  • Daryl Davis, a Black musician, spends decades in relationship with Ku Klux Klan members, starting with high-ranking Klan leader Roger Kelly, driven by genuine curiosity about how someone could hate him for his skin color. The sustained conversations eventually lead Kelly to renounce racism and change his life, without Davis conceding any moral ground along the way.
    → Supports p13 (belief change accumulates through sustained interaction, not a single encounter) and shows that respectful engagement with morally repugnant views does not require moral compromise — the counterpoint pole to the fast Rogers case.
  • A faculty colleague of Marshall's sends a well-crafted message into a heated faculty email debate, calling for mutual understanding. Marshall compliments him on it; the colleague reveals he used AI to write it because he felt too strongly about the issue to write it himself, though he already knew what he wanted to say.
    → Supports p10 and p12 — the concrete case that anchors the 'ethical amplifier' claim: AI assistance is fine when it expresses reasoning the human already did.
  • At Marshall's own university, administrators who attempt to manipulate faculty are 'usually found out,' and the resulting resentment leaves them less able to be heard or trusted afterward.
    → Supports p6 (manipulation is hard to execute undetected, and detection is costly) with an institutional example close to the author's own experience rather than a distant case study.
  • A dean at the University of Washington's business school, described by a friend of Marshall's, genuinely listens to disagreement and lets people tell him 'I told you so' without retaliation — even though he doesn't always change his mind and sometimes must decide quickly without consulting everyone.
    → Supports p9 — trust accrues from being heard, not from always getting one's way or from having time to consult everyone; a psychological-safety mechanism operating alongside, not instead of, decisive leadership.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Can we persuade people effectively without manipulating them?
    Yes — provided persuasion is reframed around treating the other person as a reasoning partner (honest reasons, respect, compassion, attentiveness to setting) rather than as a target to be managed.
  • ✅ What actually distinguishes manipulation from legitimate persuasion, if it isn't the specific technique used?
    Intent, context, and relational stance — whether the persuader attends to moral values and takes the other person seriously — not the mechanism (flattery, incremental commitment, emotional questioning) itself.
  • ✅ Does AI change the ethics of persuasion?
    No new ethical category is introduced; AI is an 'ethical amplifier' that scales whatever the human's underlying honesty and reasoning process already were.
  • ✅ How can a leader who can't have an individualized respectful conversation with every subordinate persuade ethically at scale?
    Partially: Marshall himself calls this 'the thornier problem' and offers a partial tool (delegate to a chosen representative to surface the top issue) rather than a full solution, and separately argues that leaders overestimate how often they truly lack the time at all.
  • ⭕ Can a persuader reliably self-assess whether they are meeting the respect-and-compassion criteria, or does self-serving bias make people systematically overestimate their own respectfulness toward the person they're trying to persuade?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe respect-and-compassion checklist (sincerity, openness to being persuaded, giving the other person room to live their life, preserving self-respect, attending to both parties' emotional limits) has no arbitration procedure for when the criteria conflict with each other — e.g., preserving your own self-respect versus giving the other person room to live their life, or the time pressure of leadership versus the slow-relationship model exemplified by Davis.
    Read next: Applied ethics literature on conflicting prima facie duties (W.D. Ross) or practical-wisdom/phronesis accounts (Aristotle, via virtue ethics) for how competing duty-like criteria get weighed case by case.
  • incompleteThe test Marshall offers for whether AI-assisted persuasion is ethical — whether the human had already reasoned their way to the conclusion before invoking AI, versus backfilling a 'hunch' — is purely introspective and unobservable from outside. Two textually identical AI-assisted messages could be ethical or manipulative depending entirely on a private fact about the author's process, leaving no way for a target, colleague, or organizational AI-use policy to verify which case applies.
    Read next: AI governance / content-provenance literature (disclosure requirements, process audits rather than output audits) for mechanisms that try to verify process rather than product.
  • incompleteMarshall's own book teaches readers to recognize named manipulation tactics (flattery, social pressure, selective storytelling, emotional triggers, foot-in-the-door) 'in context,' but doesn't address the risk that this same knowledge makes a reader more effective at deploying those tactics if they choose to disregard the ethical criteria — the book implicitly assumes its readers are already disposed toward ethical use rather than defending against readers who aren't.
    Read next: extract-barker-good-life-ancient-wisdom#g2 raises the identical dual-use tension about manipulation-knowledge more sharply (the oncologist/poison analogy); read Cialdini's own later reflections on responsible teaching of influence principles, and offensive-security-education literature on teaching attack skills responsibly.
  • perspectiveThe entire framework is built from the persuader's-eye view of their own intent and process. It offers no epistemics for the person being persuaded — no way for a target to reliably distinguish, in the moment, a genuine reasoning-partner approach from a well-executed manipulation that merely performs warmth, competence, and sincerity.
    Read next: Literature on manipulation detection and epistemic vigilance (e.g. Sperber et al. on epistemic vigilance) from the target's side, rather than the persuader's.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • This Is How To Live A Good Life: 5 Secrets From Ancient Wisdom · g2 — That gap asks whether studying manipulation/persuasion techniques (as Cialdini catalogs them) makes a reader more effective at manipulating others, a risk the oncologist/poison analogy leaves unaddressed. Marshall directly engages this tension: his answer is that technique-knowledge is ethically neutral and what determines misuse is intent, context, and relational stance rather than exposure to a taxonomy of tactics — though as this document's own gap g3 notes, that answer doesn't fully resolve the skill-transfer risk, since it still assumes the reader is disposed toward ethical use.
  • How to Convince Your Boss They Need a Coach · g4 — That article, written entirely from the subordinate's perspective, flagged that its own upward-influence tactics (letting the boss believe coaching was 'his own idea,' reframing to preempt resistance) could feel manipulative if detected, and explicitly asked for a contrast with 'authentic leadership and ethical influence' literature. Marshall's framework supplies exactly that contrast: under his criterion (reasoning partner vs. psychological system to be managed), several of that article's tactics would read as manipulative rather than ethical persuasion.
