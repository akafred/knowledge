Every Organization Has Two Realities

Harvard Business Review (Gretchen Gavett, The Insider newsletter) · article · 2026-07-06 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI Regulation & Governance (External)Hierarchy & Org ChartsPsychological SafetyErosion of Independent Regulatory AgenciesRule-Breaking Typology (Motivation-Based)Trust
Organizations run on a gap between the version leaders formally present, govern, and are accountable for (board decks, compliance categories, regulatory structure) and the version that is actually lived, worked, and enforced day to day (employee experience, well-intentioned deviation, political reality) — and leaders who fail to actively surface and reconcile that gap accumulate risk (talent loss, misjudged misconduct, blindsided strategy) that a more legible, presented reality obscures.

Gill on rule-breaker typologies, and Blair Levin & Larry Downes on Trump v. Slaughter) plus a podcast recommendation. Key terms

  • Reported organization
    The version of the company leaders see, govern, and explain to the world, via dashboards, board materials, town halls, and analyst calls.
  • Lived organization
    The work, the state of the team, the customer experience, and the culture that employees actually experience day to day.
  • Prosocial rule-breaking
    Gill's category of rule-breaking driven by concern for others (e.g., an employee expediting an order against policy to help a customer) — treated as potentially the correct behavior in context, not automatically misconduct.
  • Edified rule-breaker Rule-Breaking Typology (Motivation-Based)
    One of Gill's four types: driven by a personal commitment to doing the right thing (implicitly, even when that conflicts with the stated rule).

Arguments

Leaders should institute a recurring, deliberate practice of surfacing the lived organization, not just monitor the reported organization, because the reported organization alone cannot reveal its own blind spots. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Leaders governing only from the reported organization miss the lived organization.
  • Ignoring the lived organization produces concrete costs (bad strategy, board blindness, festering operational issues, talent attrition).
  • The best CEOs deliberately and repeatedly ask where the two accounts diverge.
Implicit premises:
  • The lived organization does not surface itself into reporting channels by default — it must be actively sought out.
  • The gap between the two organizations widens as companies mature, so the practice must be sustained, not a one-time fix.
Inferences:
  • Board governance built solely on management-curated materials (dashboards, town halls, analyst calls) is structurally under-informed by design, not just by an individual leader's failure to look.
Leaders should use a two-axis diagnostic (constructive/destructive, choice/situational) rather than a uniform 'misconduct' response when employees break rules. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • 65% of employees report having observed workplace misconduct.
  • Rule-breakers have four distinct motivational types (self-interested, prosocial, corrupted, edified).
  • Treating all rule-breaking identically causes organizations to miss cases where the rule-breaking is the correct behavior or signals a flawed rule.
Implicit premises:
  • Motivation, not just the act of rule-breaking, is diagnostically meaningful and actionable by leaders.
  • Leaders can reliably distinguish these motivations in practice, given the right diagnostic questions and attention to patterns.
Inferences:
  • A zero-tolerance or uniform disciplinary policy toward rule-breaking will systematically punish prosocial and edified deviation alongside self-interested and corrupted misconduct, suppressing exactly the deviation that might otherwise correct bad rules.

Narratives

  • Egon Zehnder's Irina Wolpert sits in a board meeting where a financial-services CEO presents an immaculate AI-transformation dashboard to a satisfied board; 24 hours earlier she had sat with the engineers who built the tools, two of whom were already job-hunting because of the gap between the boardroom claims and their daily experience.
    → Makes the abstract 'two-organizations problem' concrete and shows the gap as a real, present-tense governance risk (attrition of senior engineers) rather than a hypothetical.
  • An employee expedites a customer's order in violation of official company policy.
    → Illustrates prosocial rule-breaking as a minimal, relatable case where the 'correct' organizational behavior and the 'compliant' behavior diverge, supporting the proposition that uniform misconduct responses are miscalibrated.
  • Delinea CEO Art Gilliland reportedly distinguishes true misconduct from well-intentioned rule-breaking and coaches his managers to apply that judgment consistently.
    → Offers a named executive as an existence proof that the typology is actionable at the top of an organization, not just an academic framework.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do leaders systematically misjudge the true state of their organization?
    Because they govern from the reported organization (legible, curated channels) and the lived organization does not surface into those channels without deliberate, recurring effort to seek it out.
  • ✅ Should all rule-breaking be treated as equivalent misconduct?
    No — Gill's typology and two-axis diagnostic argue leaders should differentiate by motivation and constructiveness before responding.
  • ⭕ How exactly should a CEO operationalize 'asking quarterly where the two organizations diverge' — what mechanism, who is asked, how are findings acted on?
  • ⭕ What should a company actually do to hedge against regulatory/political risk now that independent-agency protections have been narrowed by Trump v. Slaughter?

Without a described mechanism, the practice risks becoming another reported-organization artifact (a 'we discussed culture at the offsite' checkbox) rather than a genuine channel to the lived organization.

  • incompleteThe digest frames organizational truth as binary — a 'reported' account and a 'lived' account, both simply 'true' — without engaging the more general problem that there is no single lived organization either: different employees, teams, and levels each hold partial, interested, negotiated views of what's really happening. The two-organizations frame may itself be a simplification that a curious CEO could act on too literally (assume there are exactly two truths to reconcile, rather than many).
    Read next: extract-tbm-426-trouble-with-mirrors argues organizational truth is not hidden-but-discoverable, it is continuously negotiated among many partial, interested viewpoints — directly complicates the two-organizations frame's implicit assumption that the lived organization is a single, findable ground truth.
