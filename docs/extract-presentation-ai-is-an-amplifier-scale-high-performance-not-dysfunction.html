Presentation: AI is an Amplifier: Scale High Performance, Not Dysfunction

Nathen Harvey · rss · 2026-10-21 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI is an amplifier of whatever organizational system it lands in, so its returns come not from adopting the tools but from fixing the surrounding system (seven DORA capabilities, small batches, user focus, platforms that serve agents and non-engineer builders) and even from radically rethinking bottlenecks like code review, while treating DORA's findings only as hypotheses to test locally.

practical · mixed — A QCon London 2026 conference talk by Nathen Harvey (leads Google Cloud's DORA team), published by InfoQ with a full verbatim text transcript (verified: the Reader content contains the complete spoken text with live-audience QR-code polls, not just an abstract). Argumentative-expository core: it walks through the 2025 DORA survey findings (about 5,000 respondents, June-July 2025) and the DORA AI Capabilities Model, then turns practical (clear stance, small batches, user focus, platform-for-builders, and a provocation to retire async code review). Heavy on jokes, audience interaction and anecdote; propositions are often restated informally.

Key terms

  • Amplifier
    AI's primary role in software development is to magnify the existing organizational system: strengths of healthy systems and dysfunction of struggling ones, rather than adding a uniform lift.
  • Software delivery instability
    How frequently pushes to production produce the 'oh, expletive' moment: rollbacks, hotfixes, forward fixes. It is DORA's measure of the stability dimension of delivery performance.
  • J-curve (instability tax / verification tax) Adoption J-Curve (Instability and Verification Tax)
    The adoption pattern where low-hanging fruit produces early gains, then the hard work arrives and performance dips into a trough, after which it improves. The trough is composed of delivery instability, lack of trust, and the verification work (reading rather than writing code) that trust deficits force.
  • DORA AI Capabilities Model
    Seven capabilities and conditions (clear and communicated AI stance, healthy data ecosystem, AI-accessible internal data, strong version control, working in small batches, user-centric focus, quality internal platforms) that, combined with AI adoption, drive outcomes. An interactive model on dora.dev lets a team start from a desired outcome and follow arrows back to capabilities.
  • Team profiles DORA AI Capabilities Model (Team Profiles & Capabilities)
    Seven clusters of teams found by looking across eight factors (team performance, product performance, individual effectiveness, friction, burnout, throughput, instability, time on valuable work), with names such as 'Constrained by process', 'Harmonious high achievers', and 'Legacy bottleneck'.
  • Developer experience / agent experience / builder experience
    A progression of whom an internal platform serves: first developers, now also AI agents, and eventually every employee who can build software with AI (the 'Jane from accounting' case).
  • Kaizen vs Kaikaku
    Kaizen is continuous incremental improvement of the current system ('augmenting'); Kaikaku is radical redesign from scratch ('evolving'). Harvey personally favors Kaizen yet floats Kaikaku for code review.
  • Clear and communicated stance
    An explicit, widely known organizational position on how, when, where, and which AI may be used; its absence causes either non-use or covert, fearful use.
  • Shadow IT Shadow AI & AI Use Policy
    Business people solving their own problems outside the technology organization; read by Harvey as a signal of real unmet business need and of technologists' failure to enable, not as a pejorative.

  • explicitAI adoption follows a J-curve: early low-hanging-fruit gains are followed by a trough (an instability tax and a verification tax, as engineers shift from writing to reviewing code they do not trust), as happened with cloud and SRE adoption; how to climb out is not yet known and is being learned collectively.conf: high

  • explicitExcellence is a virtuous cycle of stable, low-friction environments and empowered teams delivering high-quality work sustainably without burnout (the 'harmonious high achievers'), while 'constrained by process' teams run on a treadmill of inefficient process with high burnout and friction, low impact, but lower instability than peers.conf: high
  • explicitAdoption plus seven capabilities drives AI outcomes; having a tool (like a gym membership) is not enough, and teams get value only when the capabilities are present: clear and communicated stance, healthy data ecosystem, AI-accessible internal data, strong version control, small batches, user-centric focus, and a quality internal platform.conf: high

    supportsAI-Assisted Software Development: Team Profiles and Capabilities for Putting Research into Action · p7 — Both describe AI pushing large change sets against the small-batch principle.
  • explicitA user-centric focus determines whether AI helps team performance: with it performance improves, without it AI adoption makes team performance worse; and because AI makes prototyping cheap, teams can build and test many options (an A-B-C-D-E-F-G rather than A/B) with real users before productionalizing one.conf: high
    supportsState Of AI-Assisted Software Development · p6 — Absent user-centric focus, AI adoption harms team performance.

  • explicitCode review is the new bottleneck, and it may be time for asynchronous code review to go: capacity invested in it would be better spent on guardrails and understanding the intent of what is being built.conf: high
    supportsAddy Osmani's Post · p4 — Both move quality control from human code review into guardrails and the surrounding harness.

  • explicitOrganizations can adopt AI either through Kaizen (continuously improving the current system) or Kaikaku (radical redesign), and because a bad system beats a good person every time (Deming), the system, not individual effort, is the unit of change.conf: high

Arguments

Organizations should invest in the surrounding system and capabilities rather than in AI adoption alone. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Nearly everyone uses AI yet team performance varies across seven profiles, so variation is not due to AI itself.
  • Raising AI adoption alone raises individual effectiveness but also software delivery instability.
  • Local productivity gains are lost to downstream chaos (build in hours, six weeks to reach customers).
  • Seven capabilities combined with adoption drive outcomes.
Implicit premises:
  • AI usage intensity and manner are roughly uniform within the 90% of 'adopters', so profile variance isolates organizational factors.
  • The profiles and capabilities are antecedents of outcomes rather than markers of pre-existing organizational maturity.
  • Downstream capacity (review, testing, release) is a fixed constraint that must be redesigned, not scaled.
Inferences:
  • An organization should assess which team profile it is before choosing which capability to build first.
  • AI rollout programs without capability work will show activity gains (lines, PRs) while organizational outcomes stagnate.
Asynchronous code review should be seriously considered for removal, replaced by guardrails, intent-validation and pairing. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Code review is the new bottleneck as AI makes code cheap.
  • Review exists to validate tests, to teach, and to share knowledge.
  • Automated test validation, LLM-assisted codebase learning, and synchronous pairing can serve those purposes.
  • No audience member named code review when asked what one thing would let them trust AI output.
Implicit premises:
  • Those three purposes exhaust what code review does (omitting accountability, architectural consistency, security scrutiny, regulatory audit trails).
  • Guardrails and test suites are trustworthy enough, even though 30% of respondents do not trust AI output and a verification tax is the trough's cause.
  • Pairing with a human scales with agent-generated throughput and fleets of agents.
  • Silence in an audience poll is evidence about where the bottleneck lies.
Inferences:
  • In regulated settings (a bank joked about in the room) removing human review requires an alternative control owner and audit evidence.
  • The proposal is Kaikaku, which sits uneasily with Harvey's stated preference for Kaizen and his advice to treat findings as locally tested hypotheses.
Platform engineering should expand its customer to agents and non-engineer builders and shift capacity toward enabling them safely. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Everyone in the organization can now build with AI.
  • Shadow IT is a signal of unmet business need.
  • Quality internal platforms turn AI adoption into organizational value.
  • Developer experience improvements also benefit agents.
Implicit premises:
  • Platform guardrails can make non-engineer-built applications safe enough to run in production.
  • Operational ownership of such applications will not simply fall back on the engineering team (a 'they build it, you run it' trap).
  • The opportunity cost of fewer shipped features is acceptable.
Inferences:
  • A platform's success metric might become the decline in shadow IT/AI and the number of safely shipped non-engineer apps.
Writing agent context files is worthwhile even if they do not improve agent output. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • A research paper found agent files do not help produce better output much.
  • Users feel more control and trust because of them.
  • Trust begets adoption, which begets trust.
Implicit premises:
  • Increased felt trust is valuable regardless of whether it tracks actual reliability.
  • The trust-adoption loop is beneficial rather than a path to miscalibrated reliance.
Inferences:
  • This is in tension with Harvey's own claim that trusting AI 'a great deal' is wrong, and could be a placebo effect.

Narratives

  • Opening riff on the other DORAs (Dora the Explorer's three-step journey with Swiper, the Digital Operational Resilience Act, Designated Outdoor Refreshment Areas).
    → Sets a light tone and implicitly claims that AI adoption is never the tidy three-step journey (there is always a Swiper), while also linking operational excellence to regulatory resilience.
  • A LinkedIn post lamenting that something can now be built in hours but still takes six weeks to reach customers in production.
    → Illustrates localized pockets of productivity lost to downstream chaos (the amplifier thesis).
  • Harvey himself saying 'just one more prompt' at dinner time, and ending up in 12-hour AI sessions; and finding himself telling one agent what he meant for another.
    → Anecdotal evidence for cognitive overload, overuse, and why burnout findings from mid-2025 may not hold.
  • An employee who is unsure whether AI is allowed either skips it or uses it secretly at work after learning its power at home, and fears being fired.
    → Supports the capability 'clear and communicated stance'.
  • Dr. Nicole Forsgren's gym-membership analogy and DORA's earlier cloud research (some teams got value, others found it slower and more expensive).
    → Shows that possession of a technology never explained outcomes; how it is used does.
  • A board that only asks 'are we using AI?' while Harvey notes next year's question will be about return.
    → Illustrates that the measurement question determines the metric; adoption is necessary but not sufficient.
  • Live audience QR polls on 'what is stopping you from shipping AI output to production' and 'what one thing would you change to trust it', answered with trust, governance, security, tests, guardrails, validation, observability, and 'my manager', but not code review.
    → Supports the claim that the real gap is trust infrastructure and, via absence, the argument that code review is an unexamined bottleneck.
  • 'Jane from accounting' building an application with AI overnight and wanting to ship it.
    → Motivates builder experience and a platform for safe non-engineer building as the next evolution of platform engineering.
  • A senior engineer who would never write documentation for a junior but writes it for a bot, because the feedback loop is minutes instead of a year.
    → Shows that tight feedback loops drive documentation behavior; supports 'docs help agents and developers alike'.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do AI gains at the individual level not translate into organizational outcomes?
    The organization is a system with fixed downstream constraints; AI amplifies existing strengths and dysfunction, and seven capabilities convert adoption into outcomes.
  • ✅ Why is software delivery instability rising with AI adoption while throughput now improves?
    A J-curve with an instability and verification tax; the 2024 to 2025 change is read as movement over time along that curve.
  • ✅ What differentiates teams that get value from AI from those that do not?
    Seven capabilities, with user-centric focus, small batches, and platform quality given as examples.
  • ⭕ How do you get to the other side of the J-curve?
    Explicitly open: 'we're all learning that together'.
  • ⭕ What is code quality, and how should it be measured?
    Harvey admits DORA does not know what respondents mean by code quality.
  • ⭕ Should human code review be retired, and what would replace its accountability function?
    Posed as a provocation; the learning and test-validation functions are addressed, accountability and regulatory control are not.
  • ⭕ What does running many agents do to cognitive load and burnout?
    Flagged as a worry and expected to appear in next year's data.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe case for retiring asynchronous code review rests on a poll in which no one named it, plus three assumed purposes of review (tests, learning, knowledge sharing). It offers no DORA data on teams that dropped review, ignores review's accountability, security, architectural-consistency and audit-trail functions, and never reconciles removing a human check with the same talk's finding that a verification tax and low trust (30%) define the current trough or with Harvey's preference for Kaizen.
    Read next: Compare with extract-agentic-ai-and-code-reviews (layered, risk-tiered review that preserves non-defect functions) and extract-agentic-code-review; look in the DORA AI Capabilities Model report for any evidence on review practices.
  • illogicalHarvey says trusting AI 'a great deal' is wrong and praises calibrated 'somewhat' trust, yet defends agent context files that reportedly do not improve output on the grounds that they make users feel more in control and trusting, and that trust begets adoption. Felt trust that does not track reliability is the miscalibration he warns against; the placebo-like benefit is not examined.
    Read next: Calibrated-trust and perceived-vs-measured productivity work (e.g. extract-ai-developers-busier-not-more-productive); the AGENTS.md effectiveness study he alludes to.
  • incompleteThe J-curve is asserted as the explanation for the 2024-to-2025 reversal (throughput up, instability less bad) and as a pattern seen in cloud and SRE data, but two annual cross-sectional surveys with different respondents are not a time series, no curve is shown for AI, and nothing is said about how long the trough lasts or what moves a team out of it.
    Read next: A longitudinal/panel follow-up from DORA, plus the Brynjolfsson productivity J-curve literature for what an actual IT-adoption trough looks like.
  • incompleteSurvey data is June-July 2025, before the agentic wave Harvey himself says changes things (fleets of agents, cognitive overload), so the finding that burnout and friction are unchanged may already be stale. Harvey predicts different results but offers no interim evidence, and the multi-agent cognitive load question remains unmeasured.
    Read next: The next DORA survey; extract-lenny-forsgren-measure-ai-developer-productivity (flow state and cognitive load of agent work as an open question); research on supervising multiple agents.
  • incompleteThe proposal to make every employee a safe builder and shift platform capacity from features to enabling builders says nothing about the mechanism for safety (data access, security review, operational ownership, compliance) or who runs apps that Jane from accounting ships; it treats shadow IT only as demand signal, not also as a source of risk.
    Read next: extract-looking-back-at-fose-2026 (they-build-it-you-run-it risk, platforms absorbing citizen developers) and extract-fragments-july-21 (deterministic controls against the lethal trifecta for citizen-developer apps).
