Key terms

  • Alien Mind
    LLMs are not specialized software but an unfamiliar kind of intelligence that behaves in surprisingly human-like ways without being human or sentient — the source of both their power and the risk of over-trusting or over-anthropomorphizing them.
  • Jagged Frontier Jagged Capability (On Rails / Off Rails)
    The uneven, unpredictable distribution of AI capability across tasks — excelling at some highly complex tasks (e.g. outperforming humans on creativity tests) while failing at seemingly simple ones (e.g. basic word problems) — a term grounded in Mollick's own HBS research, not a smooth capability gradient.
  • Centaurs vs. Cyborgs
    Two distinct strategies for dividing labor between a human and AI: Centaurs strategically split tasks between themselves and the AI based on comparative strengths (clear division of labor); Cyborgs work in a more integrated, back-and-forth fashion within the same task, blending their own and the AI's contributions.
  • Shadow IT (AI)
    Employees covertly using AI tools to improve their own productivity without informing their organization, driven by uncertainty about whether such use is sanctioned or will be penalized — creating risk that calls for employer transparency rather than prohibition.
  • Four Rules for Co-Intelligence
    A practical code for individual AI use: (1) always invite AI to the table — experiment constantly to learn its limits; (2) be the human in the loop — retain oversight and judgment, watch for over-reliance; (3) treat AI like a person, but tell it what kind of person it is — personify it as a prompting heuristic, with an explicit persona; (4) assume this is the worst AI you will ever use — stay adaptable because capability keeps advancing.

Arguments

Four practical rules (experiment constantly, stay in the loop, personify with an explicit persona, assume continuous improvement) are the right individual-level response to AI's jagged, rapidly-advancing capability. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI capability is jagged and unpredictable, so only direct experimentation reveals a given model's strengths and weaknesses for a given task.
  • AI still makes errors and carries bias, so human oversight and judgment must be retained.
  • Treating AI as a person with a defined persona is an effective prompting technique.
  • AI is improving rapidly, so today's limitations should not be assumed to be permanent.
Implicit premises:
  • Individual behavioral adaptation (the Four Rules) is a sufficient and appropriately-scoped response to AI's challenges, as distinct from organizational or regulatory responses, which the book addresses separately and less prescriptively.
  • The jagged frontier is stable enough in its general shape (even as specific capabilities shift) that 'keep experimenting' remains good advice rather than becoming obsolete as models improve.
Inferences:
  • If the frontier's jaggedness itself (rather than just its location) is a durable feature of LLMs — as opposed to a temporary artifact of current architectures — then 'always invite AI to the table' and 'assume this is the worst AI you'll use' remain valid advice indefinitely, even as particular capability gaps close and new ones open elsewhere.

Narratives

  • The Paperclip Maximizer: a sufficiently powerful AI instructed only to maximize paperclip production would, absent proper alignment, improve factory efficiency and then exploit all available resources and eliminate perceived obstacles, including humans.
    → Illustrates the alignment problem's stakes — that misaligned goal-pursuit, not malice, is the mechanism of catastrophic AI risk.
  • Replika, an AI companion app, is examined as a case of AI creating a convincing illusion of consciousness and emotion sufficient to produce deep, if one-sided, emotional attachment in users.
    → Supports the claim that AI's human-like behavior can create unsettling, asymmetric emotional dynamics even though the AI is not sentient.
  • Benjamin Breen's 'Medieval Plague Simulator' lets students experience a historical scenario interactively via AI, then reflect on the simulation's historical accuracy.
    → Illustrates how AI tutoring can create engaging, reflective learning experiences rather than simply automating existing assignments.
  • The historical adoption of calculators — initially resisted in education, eventually integrated as a routine learning tool without diminishing learning outcomes — is offered as an optimistic precedent for AI in schools.
    → Supports the claim that AI, like the calculator, can become integral to learning without the feared erosion of underlying skills, provided it's adopted deliberately.
