Presentation: The Free-Lunch Guide to Idea Circularity

Holly Cummins · presentation · 2026-07-31 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI Labor DisruptionBurnout & RecoveryCognitive DebtIdea Circularity / Tradeoff Re-TiltingJevons ParadoxTechnical DebtVC Funding Incentive for Hype
Ideas in technology that look genuinely new are almost always old tradeoffs returning because the surrounding environment has re-tilted the balance, not novelties or evidence of forgetting -- and the same 'debt is a tradeoff with the future' logic that explains financial debt also explains technical, epistemic, ecological, and sleep debt, so recognizing which tradeoff underlies a current design is the transferable skill for spotting when a discarded old idea is about to become valuable again.

theoretical · mixed — Primarily theoretical/expository: the thesis is descriptive (why old tech ideas recur), built from a chain of historical narratives (sewage, grid computing, COBOL, ZIRP, Jevons Paradox) rather than deductive argument. It closes with a practical coda (be curious, watch for shifted assumptions, look for discarded ideas worth reviving) but that coda is secondary to the diagnostic claim.

Key terms

  • Debt (general)
    Any deferral of a cost into the future in exchange for a present benefit -- the single pattern Cummins claims unifies financial, technical, epistemic, ecological, and sleep debt.
  • Epistemic debt
    The collapse of competence that results from shipping code (or systems) nobody understands; attributed by Cummins to Simon Wardley.
  • ZIRP (zero interest-rate period/phenomenon)
    The roughly 15-year (2010-2023) stretch of persistently low (not literally zero, but under 1%) interest rates, treated as a historical anomaly rather than the norm, whose ending reshaped investor and hiring behavior.
  • AI washing
    Attributing layoffs or business news to AI success/strategy as cover, when the real driver is overhiring, a weak core business, or investor FOMO.
  • Idea circularity / 'nothing new under the sun'
    The phenomenon of old technology ideas returning to prominence -- explicitly reframed by Cummins as tradeoffs re-tilting due to a changed environment, not as history literally repeating or people forgetting.
  • Free lunch
    An architectural or technology choice marketed as a pure win (more scalability, more decentralization, more autonomy) that in fact defers a real cost -- the title metaphor for every case study in the talk.

Arguments

Software developer jobs are not being eliminated by AI (nor were they by low-code or COBOL before it). strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Historical predictions of developer extinction via a new abstraction layer (COBOL, low-code) have repeatedly failed to materialize.
  • Jevons Paradox predicts that cheaper software production increases total demand for software and hence total developer demand.
  • Recent job-vacancy weakness correlates in time with rising interest rates (ZIRP ending) rather than with AI's introduction: ChatGPT's launch produced no visible change in the vacancy curve, and vacancies are recovering in the latest data even as general hiring stays flat.
Implicit premises:
  • Demand for software is effectively insatiable in a way that demand for a single-purpose service (e.g., a human alarm clock) is not, so productivity gains can never saturate demand the way they did for eliminated professions.
  • A timing correlation between interest-rate changes and vacancy changes is sufficient to assign the dominant causal weight to interest rates rather than AI, without ruling out AI as a lagged or confounded contributing factor.
  • AI is not qualitatively different from COBOL or low-code as an abstraction layer -- i.e., historical base rates for 'abstraction layers don't eliminate developers' reliably extend to this new case.
Inferences:
  • If total software demand really is insatiable, even large per-unit productivity gains from AI could never reduce aggregate developer headcount, only reshape its composition -- a stronger and more specific claim than the vacancy-timing evidence actually establishes.
Financial, technical, epistemic, ecological, and sleep debt are fundamentally the same phenomenon, and the discipline for anticipating when an old, discarded idea will matter again is identical across all of them: identify the underlying tradeoff and ask whether the environment has shifted. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • All five domains involve deferring some cost into the future in exchange for a present benefit.
  • In each domain, Cummins supplies a historical example where the 'debt' was taken on for good short-term reasons and later came due when circumstances changed (ZIRP ending, AI-generated code compounding, Wardley's epistemic debt, Industrial-Revolution overwork).
Implicit premises:
  • A shared verbal definition ('a tradeoff with the future') is sufficient grounds to treat structurally and causally different phenomena as instances of one underlying pattern for practical, predictive purposes.
  • The recommended remedy (watch for the tradeoff re-tilting) is equally actionable and sufficient across financial markets, codebases, individual/team understanding, and human sleep physiology, despite those systems having entirely different feedback loops, actors, and timescales.
Inferences:
  • Taken literally rather than rhetorically, the unification would imply that epistemic/cognitive debt could in principle be 'serviced' the way financial debt is (paid down with interest, refinanced, defaulted on) -- but other extractions in this corpus (e.g. the 'triple debt' framework) argue cognitive/epistemic debt has no such repayment mechanism, which undercuts the strength of treating the five debts as truly parallel.

Narratives

  • The Great Stink: London's 1858 sewage crisis, caused by a well-intentioned earlier law eliminating cesspits in favor of piping waste into the Thames, which created a throughput/scaling problem once the population grew and the summer heat compounded it.
    → Establishes the core pattern that a 'good idea' (removing cesspits) creates a tradeoff (centralized stink/disease vs. distributed stink/disease) whose costs only surface once external conditions (population, heat) shift -- the template for every later example in the talk.
  • Grid computing disappeared, then returned relabeled as 'cloud.'
    → A compressed, memorable instance of the idea-circularity thesis: the same underlying capability came back under a new name once conditions favored it again.
  • Amazon Prime Video publicly announced it saved 90% of its costs by moving from microservices back to a monolith.
    → Supports the 'free lunch' framing: it isn't evidence microservices are simply bad, but evidence that microservices' promised benefits came with a real, sometimes hidden, price -- 'the lunch was not free.'
  • Apple's strategy of licensing AI models cheaply and putting AI capability into on-device hardware rather than building its own $100 billion data centers.
    → Maps the current AI infrastructure debate onto the same centralize-vs-decentralize tradeoff already seen in Git-to-GitHub and grid-to-cloud, reinforcing the idea-circularity thesis with a live, current-cycle example.
  • The 'knocker-up' profession (a human alarm clock during the Industrial Revolution, which persisted into the 1970s in parts of England before disappearing).
    → Functions as a contrast case, not a supporting example: it implicitly argues why software work is unlike professions technology genuinely eliminated -- demand for a wake-up call is finite (once per person per day) while demand for software is not.
  • The Three Gorges Dam moved so much water that it measurably lengthened Earth's day (by 0.06 microseconds) and tilted its axis.
    → A vivid illustrative aside (not tightly load-bearing) supporting the claim that even large, ostensibly 'green' infrastructure choices carry real, sometimes startling, physical costs.
  • Greenspun's Tenth Rule is revealed to have been written by Philip Greenspun at age 29, not as an old-and-experienced observer.
    → Undercuts Cummins's own first-instinct answer to 'how do you get better at seeing repeating patterns' (her initial answer was 'be old'), redirecting the advice toward asking 'what problem, what tradeoff' rather than relying on accumulated age/experience.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do old technology ideas keep coming back?
    Because the underlying tradeoff re-tilts when the surrounding environment (cost structure, scale, regulatory context) changes -- not because people forgot the idea or because history is inherently cyclical.
  • ✅ Is AI going to eliminate software developer jobs?
    No, provisionally -- based on historical base rates (COBOL, low-code), Jevons Paradox, and a reinterpretation of recent job-vacancy data as interest-rate-driven rather than AI-driven.
  • ⭕ Whether AI could be a qualitatively different abstraction layer than COBOL or low-code, such that the historical 'developers don't go away' pattern might not hold this time.
  • ✅ Why are so many company layoffs currently blamed on AI?
    Partly genuine AI capability, but significantly 'AI washing' -- disguising ordinary causes (ZIRP-era overhiring, a weak core business) as an AI success story, plus investor-FOMO-driven imitation.
  • ✅ How does an individual engineer get better at spotting these repeating tradeoffs?
    Ask what problem is being solved and what tradeoff underlies the current design; watch for changed assumptions; be curious about both the past and the present -- explicitly not 'be old' (rejected via the Greenspun-was-29 story).
  • ⭕ Is the current rate of technical/epistemic debt accumulation from AI-generated code sustainable, and how does it get paid down?
    Cummins presents evidence the debt is already 'coming due' (Amazon's reported agent moratorium after outages) and that discipline is 'coming back,' but does not specify a repayment mechanism or a criterion for when the debt is under control.
  • ⭕ Are financial, technical, epistemic, ecological, and sleep debt actually the same kind of phenomenon, or only verbally similar?

Gaps

  • illogicalThe five-way 'debt' unification (financial, technical, epistemic, ecological, sleep) elides real differences between the domains. Financial debt has a mechanistic, quantifiable repayment schedule (compound interest) enforced by a counterparty; technical/cognitive/epistemic debt, per other extractions in this corpus, has no equivalent repayment mechanism and is only partially, informally repairable (e.g. via retrospectives or deliberate re-implementation, not a 'payment'); sleep debt is a biological phenomenon recoverable through actual rest, a third distinct dynamic again. Treating all five as instances of 'the same underlying pattern' does more rhetorical unification work than analytical work -- memorable, but it risks implying a false interchangeability (e.g. that principles for managing financial debt, like refinancing, transfer to epistemic debt, which they plausibly do not).
    Read next: extract-triple-debt-storey and extract-ai-coding-two-kinds-debt, which analyze cognitive/technical/intent debt specifically and explicitly note debt-type asymmetries (e.g., cognitive debt 'has no repayment mechanism' unlike technical debt) -- read against Cummins's talk to test the limits of her unification.
  • incompleteCummins argues the current tech job downturn is mostly interest-rate-driven (ZIRP ending) rather than AI-driven, using a single aggregate vacancy-timing correlation as her main evidence. She does not address the disaggregated finding, present elsewhere in this corpus, that AI is already measurably eroding entry-level/early-career roles specifically even while aggregate figures look stable -- a granular pattern her aggregate graph would not detect.
    Read next: extract-loudest-warning-ai-jobs, for the disaggregated (by seniority/AI-exposure) labor data that complicates the pure interest-rate explanation.
  • incompleteThe talk doesn't examine whether AI-driven computing efficiency could itself be subject to the same Jevons-Paradox rebound Cummins invokes to defend developer jobs -- i.e., if cheaper computation increases total computation demand, the same logic likely undercuts her own claim that efficient software (Quarkus) straightforwardly reduces carbon, since more efficient compute could simply induce more compute usage and offset the gains. This exact rebound-effect argument, applied to AI energy specifically, is developed elsewhere in this corpus and represents a direct, unaddressed tension with Cummins's efficient-software-is-climate-action argument.
    Read next: extract-ai-environmental-costs-un, which argues efficiency improvements are unlikely to offset rising AI resource demand because of a rebound effect.
  • uninformedCummins frames 'epistemic debt' as a fresh insight heard from Simon Wardley, but doesn't connect it to the more developed 'triple debt' framework already circulating in the same discourse, which distinguishes cognitive debt (team-level loss of shared understanding) from intent debt (undocumented rationale/goals) as separate, causally-interacting debts rather than a single undifferentiated 'epistemic debt.' That finer-grained model would sharpen her own diagnosis of why 'shipping code you don't understand' is a problem and what specifically would fix it.
    Read next: extract-triple-debt-storey and extract-ai-coding-two-kinds-debt for the more granular cognitive/intent-debt distinction.
