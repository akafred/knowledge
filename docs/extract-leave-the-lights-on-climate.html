How to Get Happier and Fight Climate Change

Elizabeth Dunn and Jiaying Zhao (guest post via Annie Duke's "Thinking in Bets" newsletter) · email · 2026-07-08 Reader 🖥️

Decision-Making PracticesFraming Effects (Opportunity vs. Sacrifice)
Climate-friendly behavior change doesn't require guilt-driven sacrifice: because hedonic adaptation and sensory-specific satiety mean the marginal pleasure of a high-carbon food declines with frequency and portion size, cutting back (not eliminating) can shrink your carbon footprint while preserving or even increasing enjoyment -- so, the authors argue, reframing climate action as an opportunity for joyful decisions rather than as loss/sacrifice will produce more sustainable behavior change than guilt-based messaging, though this argument is grounded in happiness/consumption psychology rather than in decision-science reasoning about framing effects, loss aversion, or motivation under uncertainty (Duke's usual territory).

practical · guest advocacy essay / book-promotion piece — Confirmed NOT written by Annie Duke. Key terms

  • Leave the Lights On
    The book's title and central framing device: a permission-giving metaphor for climate action that doesn't require extinguishing the comforts and pleasures that make life enjoyable -- the opposite of an austerity/guilt frame.
  • hedonic adaptation
    The psychological finding that repeating a positive experience diminishes how much it is enjoyed over time -- and, per the chocolate study, can be reversed by temporarily abstaining.
  • sensory-specific satiety
    A physiological principle: the pleasure derived from eating a specific food peaks in the first bites and then declines, independent of overall hunger -- explaining why larger portions don't proportionally increase enjoyment.
  • Happy Climate workshop
    The authors' own behavior-change intervention: a small-group exercise where participants learn carbon-emission facts about foods, then choose personal changes intended to cut carbon and increase happiness simultaneously.
  • joyful decisions
    The book's reframe of climate-relevant choices as opportunities for pleasure and happiness rather than as obligations or sacrifices -- the piece's central persuasive claim.

Propositions

  • explicitClimate action should be reframed as an opportunity for joyful decisions rather than as guilt-driven sacrifice or total deprivation.conf: high
  • explicitEating a high-carbon food less often (e.g., beef once a week instead of daily), without eliminating it, meaningfully cuts an individual's carbon footprint (over 3 tons/year for that single swap).conf: high
  • explicitReducing how often a pleasurable food is eaten can preserve or increase the enjoyment derived from it, because abstention counteracts hedonic adaptation.conf: high
  • explicitPeople overestimate how much more pleasure a larger food portion will bring; actual enjoyment plateaus early (sensory-specific satiety), so a smaller portion of a high-carbon food (e.g., 8oz vs. 16oz steak) can roughly halve its carbon impact with little loss of actual enjoyment.conf: high
  • implicitOpportunity/joy framing produces more effective or more sustained climate-relevant behavior change (and, by extension, greater collective/political will) than guilt-based or sacrifice-based framing.conf: medium
  • implicitThe frequency/portion-size psychology demonstrated for food decisions generalizes to other major life decisions -- spending, vacations, having children, and career choice.conf: low

Arguments

You can meaningfully shrink your carbon footprint while preserving or increasing your own happiness, by adjusting the frequency and portion size of high-carbon pleasures rather than eliminating them or feeling guilty about them. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Eating high-carbon foods less often cuts significant carbon (the beef math).
  • Reduced-frequency consumption preserves or increases savoring, per the chocolate-abstention experiment.
  • Smaller portions cut carbon roughly proportionally while enjoyment plateaus early, per sensory-specific satiety and the portion-size studies.
Implicit premises:
  • A framing that avoids guilt and preserves pleasure will be adopted and sustained more readily than a framing built on sacrifice and deprivation.
  • The psychological mechanisms shown for food (hedonic adaptation, sensory-specific satiety) transfer to other consumption and life-decision domains without further argument.
  • Enough individuals making small, joyful low-carbon swaps can add up to a 'tipping point of mass action' on climate.
Inferences:
  • From 'a student was sad about total sacrifice' plus 'partial reduction preserves pleasure in a lab food experiment,' the piece infers that joy-preserving reframing is the right general communication strategy for climate behavior change.

Narratives

  • A workshop student plans to give up cheese entirely upon learning its carbon impact, and looks visibly sad about it.
    → Establishes the emotional cost of guilt/total-sacrifice framing, motivating the need for an alternative approach.
  • Jordi Quoidbach's chocolate experiment: one group is given unlimited chocolate for a week, one group abstains, one is a control; the abstaining group savors chocolate most and gets the biggest happiness boost a week later.
    → Provides the piece's strongest empirical grounding -- a real controlled study -- for the 'less often, more enjoyed' mechanism (hedonic adaptation).
  • Ruth's Chris Steak House offers a 16oz New York strip (100 lbs of carbon) versus an 8oz 'petite filet' (half the carbon).
    → Makes the portion-size argument concrete and relatable with a specific, checkable real-world example.
  • French children are offered snacks ranging from a modest suggested serving to more than double that size; they expect to enjoy the larger snack more, but actual enjoyment (unless very hungry) doesn't track size.
    → Generalizes the portion-size finding beyond adults and beyond the authors' own steak example, and introduces the sensory-specific-satiety mechanism via monkey neuron studies.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Does cutting carbon from diet require giving up high-carbon foods entirely?
    No -- reducing frequency (e.g., beef once/week instead of daily) already yields large carbon savings (>3 tons/year).
  • ✅ Does eating a pleasurable food less often reduce how much you enjoy it?
    No -- per the chocolate study, abstaining preserves (and can increase) the capacity to savor it, by counteracting hedonic adaptation.
  • ✅ Does a smaller portion meaningfully reduce enjoyment?
    Largely no, beyond a hunger threshold -- sensory-specific satiety means enjoyment plateaus early regardless of portion size.
  • ⭕ Does opportunity/joy framing actually produce more sustained climate behavior change or greater political will than guilt-based framing?
    Not addressed with evidence -- this is the piece's central persuasive claim, but no framing-effect or climate-communication study is cited to support it; it rests on the emotional resonance of one anecdote.
  • ⭕ Do individual dietary behavior changes meaningfully affect aggregate/global emissions relative to industrial and energy-sector sources?
    Not addressed at all -- the piece stays entirely at the level of individual consumer choice.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece's central claim -- that opportunity/joy framing produces more effective, more sustained climate behavior change than guilt/sacrifice framing -- is asserted via a single anecdote (the sad cheese-abandoning student) rather than evidenced by any cited study comparing framing conditions on actual sustained behavior. None of the three studies cited (chocolate abstention, portion-size perception, carbon math) tests communication or messaging strategy; they test consumption/hedonic psychology, not framing effects on behavior adoption or political will. Given this newsletter's usual decision-science pedigree (Annie Duke on calibration, resulting, avoiding narrative fallacy), the gap between the rigor applied to the narrow food claims and the rigor (none) applied to the load-bearing framing claim is notable.
    Read next: Gain-vs-loss message framing research (Rothman & Salovey's classic meta-analyses); climate communication literature (Elke Weber; the Yale Program on Climate Change Communication); comparative studies of guilt appeals vs. positive-emotion appeals in environmental campaigns.
  • incompleteNo engagement with moral licensing -- the well-documented finding that a low-cost 'virtuous' action can license compensatory increases in other consumption (e.g., a 'green' purchase licensing unrelated indulgence). If joyful, guilt-free climate choices reduce the psychological friction people feel around consumption generally, they could license higher consumption elsewhere, partially offsetting the carbon savings the piece claims. As trained behavioral scientists, Dunn and Zhao are very likely aware of this literature and simply don't engage it here.
    Read next: Moral licensing literature -- Sachdeva, Iliev & Medin on 'moral self-licensing'; Mazar & Zhong, 'Do Green Products Make Us Better People?'
  • incompleteThe piece frames climate action entirely through individual consumer choices (diet, spending, travel, having children, job) with no acknowledgment of the disproportionate share of emissions from industrial and energy production relative to individual consumption, and no discussion of collective or political action. This risks reproducing the 'carbon footprint individualization' critique -- the idea that focusing on personal consumption choices redirects responsibility away from systemic/corporate actors.
    Read next: Mark Kaufman, 'The Carbon Footprint Sham' (Mashable); Michael Mann, 'The New Climate War,' on the individualization of climate blame.
  • illogicalThe piece generalizes from a single, well-evidenced domain (food, where hedonic adaptation and sensory-specific satiety directly and literally apply) to 'everything from how to spend money and where to go on vacation to whether to have children and what kind of job to take' -- a much broader set of life decisions where the same mechanisms don't obviously transfer. Having children, for instance, is not a repeatable-frequency choice analogous to eating chocolate; the generalization is presented as a smooth extension without any argument for why the mechanism carries over.
    Read next: The book itself (not excerpted here) may supply this argument; absent that, treat the food-domain findings as the well-supported core and the life-domain claims as unsupported extrapolation.
