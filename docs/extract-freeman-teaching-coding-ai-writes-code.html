Teaching Coding When AI Can Write the Code

Eric Freeman · rss · 2026-07-28 Reader 🖥️

Cognitive SurrenderDesirable Difficulty & Productive StruggleLearning Culture & SlackSystemic Apprenticeship & Skill Transmission
Because AI has severed the link between a finished piece of code and evidence of the student's understanding, coding education must shift its center of gravity from grading the finished artifact to making the process of thinking — explained, revised, and performed in public — the thing that is observed and assessed.

practical · mixed — Argues for specific pedagogical action (three concrete assessment practices) grounded in a diagnostic claim (AI breaks code's function as a window into thinking). Expository/argumentative frame carrying narrative case studies (the Molnár chatbot, the algorave) as evidence.

Key terms

  • window into thinking
    The historical property of student code — its errors, structure, and awkward solutions — that let a teacher infer how the student reasoned and where they got stuck.
  • sketch (in creative coding)
    A program treated as temporary, exploratory, and open to change — made, tested, revised, and shared, borrowing the studio-art sense of a preparatory work rather than a final piece.
  • the process is the proof
    The author's summary principle: once a finished artifact (code, memo, PR) can be produced cheaply by AI, only observing how someone thought, chose, revised, and recovered from failure can certify understanding or skill.
  • algorave
    A live, public performance event (short for 'algorithmic rave') in which students write and modify code in real time on a projected screen while an audience watches the editor and the resulting audiovisuals simultaneously.

Arguments

Institutions should not make AI-authorship detection the primary defense against AI undermining coding assessment. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Popular AI detectors have high false-positive rates against nonnative English writers (61.22% of TOEFL essays in one study).
  • OpenAI itself retired its AI Text Classifier in 2023 for inaccuracy.
Implicit premises:
  • A detector's job is only to flag that AI was used, not to establish whether the student learned anything — so even a perfectly accurate detector would leave the actual pedagogical question (did the student learn?) unanswered.
  • Basing high-stakes honor-code decisions on an unreliable and ultimately beside-the-point signal produces false accusations without producing better learning outcomes.
Inferences:
  • Schools investing primarily in surveillance/lockdown/detection infrastructure are misallocating effort relative to the actual assessment problem.
Assessment of coding ability should shift from the finished program to the visible process (explanation, revision, live performance) that produced it. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • In the arts, the finished piece was never treated as the whole story — process has always been assessed there.
  • A finished AI-assisted program now reveals the student's prompts more than their reasoning.
Implicit premises:
  • A coding assignment's purpose was always to develop and demonstrate the student's own understanding, not to produce a working program as an end in itself — i.e., the artifact was always instrumental, not terminal, to the pedagogical goal.
  • Process signals (public explanation, live on-stage debugging, sustained conversational interrogation) are harder to outsource wholesale to AI than a single static file is.
Inferences:
  • The same reasoning extends beyond the classroom to any domain where AI can now cheaply produce a polished final artifact — code review, hiring, performance management — which the author draws explicitly in the closing section.
An AI conversational agent that questions and assesses a student (rather than answering the student's questions) can produce trustworthy, previously-unavailable evidence of understanding. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Prior teachable-agent research (Betty's Brain) shows that explaining material to a software agent forces students to organize knowledge and exposes gaps.
  • In small trials, the AI assessor's rubric-based evaluations of the Molnár conversations closely matched independent teaching-assistant evaluations.
Implicit premises:
  • An AI system can reliably distinguish genuine understanding from fluent-sounding mimicry when following a rubric — the same kind of trust in an AI's judgment that the article's earlier detector argument explicitly withheld from AI when the question was 'did a human write this.'
  • The bias risk raised against detectors (systematically penalizing nonnative or atypical communication styles) does not apply, or applies less, to an AI grading spoken/conversational explanations — a claim the article does not examine.
Inferences:
  • If the same fairness scrutiny applied to detectors were applied to the AI-as-assessor role, it might surface analogous bias risks (e.g., against less fluent or nonnative speakers in a conversational assessment) that the article does not consider.

Narratives

  • The Vera Molnár chatbot: a simple prompt-engineered avatar of algorithmic-art pioneer Vera Molnár converses with students about randomness, computation, and creative choice, follows up on unclear answers, and reviews the transcript against a rubric to assess understanding.
    → Concretely demonstrates the 'invert the roles: AI as teacher and assessor' practice and supports the claim that a conversational transcript can reveal understanding a finished submission cannot.
  • The AudioPixel Collider algorave: students from different disciplines stage a live, public performance where code is written and modified in real time on a projected editor while audio/visual output responds live, with the explicit risk of the whole projection failing ('turning into a beige rectangle') if the coder falters.
    → Shows 'make understanding performative' in action and grounds the proposition that live coding makes real-time faking impossible ('it is very hard to plagiarize panic').
  • The end-of-term object-oriented game assignment, where teaching assistants with limited OOP experience used an AI-generated rubric-based analysis of each submission to structure their own grading, with the author available as an OOP expert for unresolved questions.
    → Illustrates AI used to scale substantive process-aware feedback despite student-to-TA ratio constraints, but also implicitly shows TAs leaning on AI's analysis to compensate for their own domain gaps — a case the article treats as a success story without examining the risk that TAs might defer to a plausible-looking AI analysis they cannot independently verify.

Questions addressed

  • ⭕ If AI can write code, should schools still teach coding at all?
    The author states his own answer is yes, 'at least for some students and situations,' but explicitly defers the justification: 'that's another topic.'
  • ✅ If coding is still taught in a world where AI can write code, how can teachers know whether students are actually learning?
    Answered with three practices — public studio-style work, AI-as-assessor conversation, and live performative coding — that shift assessment from the finished artifact to the visible process.
  • ⭕ Do these three practices actually improve or reliably measure learning, compared to conventional assessment?
    Explicitly left open by the author: 'we haven't conducted a controlled study... these efforts are experiments, not final answers.'
  • ⭕ Do these creative-coding-specific, small-class, performance-culture practices generalize to standard computer-science instruction that lacks a studio/arts tradition and operates at much larger scale?
    Not resolved; the author notes the practices 'work especially well in creative coding' but does not address transfer to conventional CS1-style courses.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe three practices are validated only by the author's own uncontrolled classroom observation — no comparison group, no measured learning-outcome data, and no discussion of cost/scale requirements (small classes, an arts-department studio culture, custom-built chatbot avatars, TA availability for public critique) that make the approach hard to generalize to large, non-arts-adjacent CS programs.
    Read next: Look for controlled or larger-N studies of process-based/performative assessment in programming education, and for follow-on evaluations of teachable-agent-as-assessor systems (extending Betty's Brain-style research to the assessor role rather than the tutee role).
  • illogicalThe article distrusts AI's reliability as a detector of whether a human wrote a piece of text (citing bias against nonnative English speakers and OpenAI's own retired classifier) but does not extend the same scrutiny to AI's reliability as an assessor of student understanding — despite grading spoken/conversational explanation being exactly the kind of task where fluency-related bias (the same failure mode documented for detectors) could plausibly recur.
    Read next: Research on fairness and bias in AI-as-grader / conversational-assessment systems, particularly regarding nonnative-speaker or atypical-communication-style students, given the article's own evidence that AI text-judgment tools already show this exact bias in a different context.
  • incompleteThe article's proposed replacement signal for 'code as reliable evidence of thinking' is the process trace itself (public explanation, live performance, AI-mediated conversation) — but it does not establish how durable this new signal is against its own future gaming: a rehearsed AI-coached explanation, a memorized live-coding routine, or answers effectively drafted by AI and read aloud in the chatbot conversation. It swaps one hard-to-fake signal for one whose own gaming-resistance is asserted, not demonstrated, and the reading direction implied by the source brief (what replaces code once code stops proving understanding) is left as an open empirical question rather than a settled design.
    Read next: History and literature on the gaming and eventual erosion of other 'process' evidence forms (oral exams, viva voce, live demonstrations, in-person coding interviews) — do process-based signals have their own track record of being coached, memorized, or otherwise gamed over time?

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • Det skal være vanskelig · g1 — Offers a concrete, structural (not self-discipline-dependent) answer to how a 'simulator' can be made gaming-resistant at the point of use: public studio critique makes AI-only work visible to the room, an AI-as-assessor conversational agent follows up until it gets independent evidence of understanding rather than accepting a first answer, and live on-stage coding performance makes real-time paste-and-pray structurally impossible. This is a partial closure only — Freeman's own evidence is uncontrolled, and he concedes the practices work 'especially well' in creative coding's public/performative culture, not shown to generalize to standard, large-scale CS instruction, which is exactly the 'at scale' qualifier the original gap raised.
