AI-Assisted Software Development: Team Profiles and Capabilities for Putting Research into Action

DORA's 2025 research reframes AI adoption in software development as an organizational-system problem rather than a tooling problem: AI amplifies whatever team dynamics already exist, and the return on AI investment comes from developing identifiable, data-derived capabilities (from a clear AI stance to quality internal platforms) matched to your team's actual profile, not from adopting the technology itself.

practical · news article + Q&A interview, reporting a conference talk — InfoQ's Ben Linders summarizes a QCon London talk by Nathen Harvey (Google/DORA) and follows it with a short interview. It relays DORA's empirical research but is itself a secondary, compressed write-up, not the primary report.

Key terms

  • team profiles DORA AI Capabilities Model (Team Profiles & Capabilities)
    Seven empirically-derived clusters from DORA's 2025 survey data, grouping teams by measured factors (team performance, product performance, individual effectiveness, friction, burnout) — a data-derived typology, not a maturity ladder or self-assessment quiz.
  • capabilities
    DORA's specific research construct (in the Accelerate/DORA capability-model tradition): practices statistically associated with better outcomes when combined with AI adoption — e.g. 'strong version control practices,' 'working in small batches' — measured, not aspirational competencies.
  • AI as amplifier AI as Amplifier
    AI magnifies whatever organizational system already exists — good practices compound, dysfunction accelerates — so AI's effect is conditional on the underlying system, not intrinsic to the tool.
  • organizational system
    The combination of technology, teamwork, business drivers, and customer focus DORA researches together — deliberately broader than 'software delivery' alone.
  • software delivery instability
    A DORA delivery-performance metric family (rollback rate, hotfix/incident-driven rework following deployment) — a specific measured quantity, not a colloquial sense of things feeling shaky.

  • explicitDORA identified seven capabilities — clear and communicated AI stance, healthy data ecosystems, AI-accessible internal data, strong version control practices, working in small batches, user-centric focus, and quality internal platforms — that, combined with AI adoption, drive better outcomes.conf: high

  • explicitSoftware delivery instability (deployments rolled back, hotfixes required to correct deployment-introduced errors) also increased as AI adoption increased, even as perceived code quality and individual effectiveness improved.conf: high
    supportsAI isn't making developers more productive – it's making them busier · p4 — DORA's own instability-rising finding corroborates the 'weak-link hypothesis' — AI-driven volume increases outrun fixed downstream (review/integration/production-stability) capacity, producing exactly the kind of downstream cost this claim identifies.
  • explicitThis co-occurrence — perceived quality up, delivery instability also up — may signal misaligned incentives or lack of visibility into the production impact of changes, such as engineers optimizing for shipping features without ownership of how those features land in production.conf: medium
    supportsAI productivity is burning out your best engineers · p4 — Both describe a visibility gap: velocity/quality-perception metrics look good while a real downstream cost (production instability; validation burden and its 'lag effect') accumulates unmeasured.
  • explicitAI puts pressure on DORA's long-standing finding that small batch sizes improve delivery performance, because models tend to suggest large change sets or pull requests that are harder to review and carry more risk; the remedy is collaborating with AI to decompose large changes into smaller, independently deployable units.conf: high
    refinesThe Future Of Software Engineering · p8 — The other piece asserts AI is already causing a regression to larger batch sizes 'reversing a decade of DORA findings' as a settled trend; Harvey's framing treats this as a manageable pressure teams can counteract by actively collaborating with AI to decompose changes, not an inevitable reversal.
  • explicitQuality internal platforms extract value from AI investment in two ways: making a capability accessible to the whole organization (driving adoption), and shifting complexity down into the platform so it is hidden from and doesn't have to be reasoned about by individual builders (e.g. centrally enforcing a security policy).conf: high
  • implicitApplying DORA's findings well requires treating them as a falsifiable hypothesis to test against local context rather than as a universal prescription — which team profile you're in should change which capability you prioritize first.conf: medium

Arguments

Organizations get the most return from AI when they invest in organizational capabilities and system strength rather than in AI tooling adoption alone. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI is an amplifier of the existing organizational system.
  • Seven identified capabilities, combined with AI adoption, drive better outcomes.
  • Without a strong foundation, AI creates localized productivity pockets that are lost to downstream chaos.
Implicit premises:
  • The aggregate cost of downstream chaos outweighs the value of localized productivity gains.
  • The capability-outcome relationship DORA reports is causal, not merely correlational — despite the underlying method being a survey (correlational by design).
Inferences:
  • The article never asks whether organizations with the seven capabilities already had them because they were well-run for other reasons (a confound), which would make 'capabilities cause AI value' partly reverse causation or a shared cause rather than a clean lever.
Teams should actively collaborate with their AI assistants to decompose AI-suggested large changes into smaller, independently deployable units.
Explicit premises:
  • DORA's data has long shown smaller batches improve delivery performance.
  • AI models tend to propose large change sets or pull requests.
  • Larger changes are harder to review and carry more risk.
Implicit premises:
  • Human review capacity for large AI-generated diffs is a fixed, binding constraint — the same 'weak-link' dynamic documented elsewhere in this corpus at scale.
  • Teams retain enough process discipline to insist on decomposition even under the productivity pressure to accept large AI-generated changes wholesale.
Inferences:
  • The article doesn't connect this dynamic to the broader empirical finding (from a separate 100,000+ developer study in this corpus) that AI-driven code volume growth already outpaces PR and release growth precisely because human review is the bottleneck — a natural corroborating data point it doesn't cite.

Narratives

  • The 'constrained by process' team profile's spider chart: high burnout, high friction, low individual effectiveness, low time spent on valuable work, yet comparatively good software delivery instability and only slightly lower throughput than other profiles.
    → Demonstrates that team profiles are genuinely multi-dimensional — a team can look fine on delivery-stability metrics while being burned out, frustrated, and low-value, which is exactly the blind spot a narrow 'AI improved our deploy metrics' narrative would miss.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Does AI improve individual productivity and code quality, as perceived by developers?
    Yes — self-reported individual effectiveness and perceived code quality both increased with AI adoption, per DORA's 2025 survey.
  • ✅ Does AI improve software delivery stability?
    No — software delivery instability increased alongside AI adoption, the opposite direction from the perception-based measures.
  • ⭕ What causes the quality-perception-up / delivery-instability-up contradiction?
    Harvey offers a hypothesis (misaligned incentives, lack of visibility into production impact) but the article does not report DORA testing this hypothesis against the data — it remains interpretive commentary, not an established causal finding.
  • ⭕ Are the seven team profiles and seven capabilities validated as robust across industries, company sizes, and geographies, or specific to DORA's 2025 sample?
  • ⭕ Given the seven capabilities, how should a team decide which one to prioritize first for its specific team profile?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article never defines what distinguishes six of the seven team profiles beyond naming them; only 'constrained by process' gets a description via its spider chart. A reader cannot self-diagnose which profile their own team matches from this piece alone.
    Read next: The full DORA 2025 'State of AI-Assisted Software Development' report at dora.dev/ai for the profile definitions and their measured criteria.
  • incompleteThe piece uses causal language ('drive better outcomes') for the capability-outcome relationship but never states DORA's research design in enough detail (survey design, longitudinal vs. cross-sectional, controls) to know whether that relationship is measured causally or is a correlational finding from self-report survey data — a distinction this reader's stated preference for 'benchmark data over vendor claims' would specifically flag.
    Read next: DORA's published methodology section, or the AI Capabilities Model report (December, referenced in the article) for the underlying statistical treatment.
  • incompleteThe article surfaces the counterintuitive quality-up/instability-up finding and Harvey's own hypothesis for it, but never reports whether DORA tested that hypothesis against the data (e.g. correlating instability with specific missing capabilities) — it's presented as plausible interpretation, not a verified mechanism.
    Read next: DORA report sections specifically on drivers of delivery instability, or a follow-up interview probing this finding.
  • uninformed'Individual effectiveness' and 'code quality' improvements are explicitly self-reported/perceived, but the article's framing doesn't consistently flag this as perception data rather than a measured outcome — risking a reader treating 'quality improved' as objective rather than a survey-response artifact, especially since the same report shows an objective-leaning metric (delivery instability) moving in the opposite direction.
    Read next: extract-ai-developers-busier-not-more-productive (measured throughput/value vs. self-reported busyness) and extract-8-engineering-metrics-ai-broke (why self-reported satisfaction/effectiveness metrics can mask real degradation) for the general pattern.
  • incompleteNo discussion of sample size, company size range, industry mix, or geography for the underlying 2025 DORA survey — a reader trying to judge how far the seven-profile/seven-capability taxonomy generalizes to their own organization (e.g. a Norwegian telco) needs the sample composition, which the article omits entirely.
    Read next: DORA 2025 report methodology appendix.
