Is Your AI Training the Competition?

William Marks · article · 2026-09-08 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

AI Industry LitigationEnterprise AI Adoption & ReadinessKnowledge Distillation (Teacher-Student Compression)Moats & DefensibilityOrganizational Legibility
Beyond protecting raw data, companies must actively defend 'logic sovereignty' — ownership of the specific expertise and reasoning their employees apply through AI tools — because AI providers absorb that expertise through feedback-clause carve-outs, synthetic-training pipelines, and the structural flywheel of heavy use, in ways standard 'no training on our prompts' contracts do not prevent.

practical · mixed — Primarily practical/argumentative — each of the three 'channels' ends in an explicit 'What managers should do' prescription — but built on an expository reframing (data vs. logic, AI Sherlocking, logic sovereignty) and grounded with narrative case evidence (Apple/Watson, Anthropic's Feb 2026 disclosure, Samsung's 2023 leak).

Key terms

  • AI Sherlocking
    An AI platform absorbing a company's hard-won, specific problem-solving logic through ordinary tool use — not by copying a visible feature, but by learning the reasoning pattern behind good answers — so that a competitor's next similar question is answered by a model already sharpened on your expertise.
  • logic sovereignty
    Ownership not just of a company's data but of the thinking — the specific judgment, workflow, and reasoning — it applies to that data; the thing the author argues is now scarcer and more valuable than data itself.
  • distillation Knowledge Distillation (Teacher-Student Compression)
    Using a stronger model's outputs to train a weaker one to imitate it — illustrated by the Anthropic disclosure, where three Chinese labs allegedly used fraudulent accounts to harvest Claude's answers at scale for this purpose.
  • shadow AI
    Employees using free, personal AI accounts for work — out of habit, convenience, or to route around enterprise restrictions — so that whatever they paste in goes straight into an unprotected training pipeline with no enterprise agreement involved.

Arguments

Standard 'no training on our prompts' contract protections do not actually protect a company's most valuable AI-era asset. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Enterprise contracts typically cover only the literal prompt and the raw model output.
  • Feedback (ratings, corrections) is contractually carved out as a separate category the vendor may use freely.
  • Providers also train future models on their own systems' outputs at scale (synthetic data) and on the aggregate pattern of similar high-quality work happening across the user base.
Implicit premises:
  • The value AI Sherlocking targets is the blend of model capability and applied human expertise, not the literal prompt/output text — so protecting only that text protects the wrong unit of value.
  • Providers have a structural business incentive (the 'flywheel') to keep learning from usage regardless of what any single contract clause says.
Inferences:
  • A company cannot eliminate this exposure through contract language alone; it must also change what kind of work it routes to which deployment tier.
AI Sherlocking is structurally different from, and harder to defend against than, traditional Sherlocking.
Explicit premises:
  • Traditional Sherlocking (Apple/Watson) copied a visible, discrete feature.
  • AI Sherlocking absorbs 'something you can't see: the reasoning that produced a good answer.'
Implicit premises:
  • Legal and technical defenses (patents, contract clauses naming specific data, sandboxing) are built to protect discrete, nameable artifacts — not a diffuse pattern learned across millions of interactions.
  • If the thing being absorbed has no discrete legal form, no defense keyed to discrete artifacts can fully stop it.
Inferences:
  • Patent and trade-secret law, built around identifiable IP, may be structurally mismatched to this threat — consistent with the corpus finding (extract-a-big-week-for-ai-denialism#p16) that labs have not been sued over distillation despite likely ToS violations, for lack of a clean legal hook.
Managers should treat AI feedback mechanisms as a contract-negotiation issue, not merely a product setting. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • OpenAI's and Anthropic's contracts both carve feedback out from 'no training' protections.
  • OpenAI's documentation states that even a user who opted out of training generally still contributes the whole associated conversation via a single thumbs-up/down.
Implicit premises:
  • Most legal/security reviews currently stop at confirming 'no training on our prompts' and treat that as sufficient — the argument's force depends on this being widespread executive complacency.
Inferences:
  • Vendor contract templates will likely need a newly negotiated clause category ('feedback and interaction-signal protection') as this awareness spreads — consistent with the article's own prediction that this becomes 'a standard boardroom topic.'
Being an AI vendor's most sophisticated user is a competitive liability rather than, as with ordinary tools, an advantage. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • AI platforms improve by learning from their most sophisticated users.
  • The resulting improved capability is then sold to the whole market, including direct competitors.
Implicit premises:
  • The improvement transfers as general capability for a class of problem, not as raw data the company could control — so there is no way to license the resulting capability lift exclusively.
  • The company doing the teaching receives no compensation or exclusivity for the capability lift its usage produces.
Inferences:
  • Under this logic, deliberately staying an unsophisticated or intermittent AI user for the most strategic work becomes a rational, if uncomfortable, competitive strategy in some cases — an inference the article gestures at (task-splitting, in-house fine-tuning) without stating it this starkly.

Narratives

  • Apple built Sherlock, a search tool that copied the third-party Watson app feature-for-feature in the early 2000s and put its maker out of business almost overnight — coining 'getting Sherlocked.'
    → Establishes 'Sherlocking' as a familiar, named, retrospectively-obvious pattern of platform absorption before extending it to the harder-to-see AI version — the analogy does the persuasive work the article's abstract definition alone couldn't.
  • In February 2026, Anthropic disclosed that DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, and MiniMax ran over 16 million queries against Claude through roughly 24,000 fraudulent accounts to train competing models via distillation, identified by unusual concentration on Claude's most distinctive reasoning/coding capabilities.
    → Makes the abstract 'flywheel' mechanism concrete and undeniable by showing its adversarial extreme, licensing the pivot to 'the same underlying dynamic in a milder, entirely legal form' operating on every paying customer.
  • Samsung engineers inadvertently uploaded proprietary semiconductor source code to ChatGPT in 2023 while using it to debug code, prompting Samsung to ban the tool internally.
    → Grounds 'shadow AI' as an already-materialized incident rather than a hypothetical, and shows the loss occurring through the most mundane possible channel — ordinary employee habit, no sophisticated AI Sherlocking mechanism required.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Is my company's AI use protected from training the provider's future models?
    Partially: literal prompts and outputs are typically protected under enterprise contracts, but feedback signals, shadow/non-enterprise use, and the structural capability transfer from heavy usage are not.
  • ✅ What should a company concretely do differently?
    Three channel-specific fixes: renegotiate contracts to explicitly cover feedback/ratings/interaction logs; move the highest-value work to private/on-prem deployments walled off from any training pipeline; decompose the most strategically sensitive tasks so the 'stitching' step that reveals the overall method stays in-house.
  • ⭕ How much competitive damage does AI Sherlocking actually cause — to whom, how fast, and at what scale?
    Never quantified; addressed only impressionistically ('gradually and then all at once').
  • ⭕ Is this dynamic genuinely new, or a rebranding of the already well-understood 'data flywheel' / network-effects concept in tech strategy?
    The author concedes in passing 'it's not entirely new' but never engages existing flywheel/network-effect literature to say what, specifically, is new about the AI version versus the pre-AI platform-economics pattern.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article never quantifies how much of a platform's improvement on a class of problem is actually attributable to any single customer's usage versus the aggregate of millions of users solving similar problems. Without evidence isolating attribution, 'your best people are training your competitors' could be true only in a diffuse, per-company-negligible sense — a materially different risk than the article's framing implies.
    Read next: ML research on marginal per-customer contribution / attribution in RLHF and continued pretraining; any published data on how concentrated vs. diffuse a platform's capability gains are across its customer base.
  • incompleteThe piece treats all AI use as equally exposed to AI Sherlocking, but never addresses whether exposure should scale with how genuinely novel a company's 'logic' actually is — a company solving well-known problems the standard way has little unique reasoning to lose, while a company with a truly novel approach has much more at stake. The article's advice (contract renegotiation, private deployment, task decomposition) is presented as universal rather than triaged by this distinction.
    Read next: Literature on which capabilities are genuinely defensible versus commodity-grade (moats / adaptive-advantage corpus threads); frameworks for triaging AI use cases by IP sensitivity.
  • incompleteThe article's proposed defenses — splitting a task apart and keeping only the 'stitching together' step in-house, or using a public model for rough ideas and fine-tuning a smaller in-house model on the real work — are never evaluated for practicality: how much of the value of frontier-model reasoning is lost when the integrating step is withheld, and whether a smaller in-house model can absorb frontier-quality reasoning without becoming a materially weaker substitute.
    Read next: Research on task-decomposition costs in AI-assisted workflows; small-model fine-tuning/distillation-from-a-public-model literature and its quality gap versus frontier models.
  • uninformedThe article notes Anthropic added feedback-management controls (Rate Chats toggle) for Team/Enterprise plans in March 2026 but explicitly flags it as 'not a tried and proven guarantee' — without examining what, technically, turning it off actually disables. It may stop only the explicit thumbs-up/down UI signal while implicit correction signals (edits, re-prompts, task abandonment/completion) remain uncontrolled and unaddressed by the toggle.
    Read next: Independent technical verification or documentation of exactly what Anthropic's Rate Chats setting does and does not disable.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • This Week in AI: Production Viability · g5 — That gap flagged a conflation of 'training data leakage' with 'prompt-time context use' as having very different IP-erosion profiles, undistinguished in the source piece.
