The gap in shared understanding of LLM capability is no longer two groups speaking past each other but a widening funnel, driven by problem ambition, jagged (verifiable-and-lucrative) capability and tier of access, in which a tiny sliver of professionals now watches the curve go vertical while most of the world sees a derpy chatbot, and at the very top human review and comprehension are starting to lag behind what the agents produce.

theoretical · mixed — A descriptive, argumentative social-epistemic claim (the shape of the divide in how people understand LLM capability) with an explanatory model of its causes. It is not a call to action; the closing 'weirdness of the moment' is an observation, not a prescription. The October post is expository (tiers with napkin figures, three drivers); the April thread is argumentative and anecdotal.

Key terms

  • funnel (of AI understanding)
    A nested, narrowing distribution of experience with LLM capability, from billions with almost no contact down to ~5,000 with internal frontier access, in which what each tier has directly seen differs radically from the tier below it.
  • napkin math
    Order-of-magnitude, unsourced back-of-envelope estimates of how many people fall in each tier (6B, 1-2B, 20M, ~5,000). The author flags them as estimates; they are not measured.
  • jaggedness Jagged Capability (On Rails / Off Rails)
    Capability peaks in domains that are digital, verifiable and economically valuable, because model capability emerges from reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards over a curated environment mixture driven by revenue potential. It primarily produces the 'area' (math and code) dimension of the funnel.
  • verifiable rewards
    Training signals with an explicit, automatable pass/fail check (e.g. unit tests passed yes or no), contrasted with writing, which is hard to judge explicitly; domains that have them are easy to reinforcement-learn.
  • AI psychosis Emotional Impact of AI Work
    The heightened gravity, vertigo or conviction about AI capability and its slope (including cyber repercussions) felt by the group that is both paying for frontier agentic models and using them professionally in technical domains. Colloquial, not clinical.
  • derpy chatbot
    The impression of AI held by free-tier or casual users: error-prone, quirky, hallucination-prone, a better Google or writing aid.
  • archeology (of an incident)
    The slow, after-the-fact human reconstruction of what agents did and why, used as the example of human review and comprehension lagging behind agent output.
  • tier of access (free / paid / internal)
    The third driver: which grade of model a person can reach - free-tier consumer products, paid tiers (e.g. $200/month), or internal frontier systems not yet released.

Arguments

The gap in understanding of LLM capability is best described as a funnel of experience tiers, not as two camps. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Roughly 6B people have barely used LLMs; 1-2B use free tiers casually; ~20M use frontier LLMs professionally in math and code; ~5,000 have internal frontier access (napkin math).
  • Each tier directly observes something different about what LLMs can do.
Implicit premises:
  • People's views of AI capability are formed mainly by what they have directly seen, not by second-hand reports or abstract understanding.
  • The tier sizes, even at order-of-magnitude accuracy, are meaningful and the tiers are roughly nested.
  • A single dimension (depth of exposure) can summarize a divide that actually has several axes.
Inferences:
  • Public and executive opinion about AI is systematically anchored on the lowest-exposure tiers, so surveys of 'what people think about AI' mostly measure tier membership.
  • Debate across tiers will keep failing because each side's evidence is accurate for its own tier.
The funnel has its shape because of three factors: ambition (consumer vs professional), jagged capability (area), and access (tier). strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Impact scales with ambition, problem size and horizon; short questions barely stress the system.
  • Capability peaks where tasks are digital, verifiable and economically valuable, because RL on verifiable rewards is steered by revenue potential.
  • Free, paid and internal tiers expose different model grades.
Implicit premises:
  • Lab priorities (and thus the RL environment mixture) follow revenue potential, and this will persist.
  • Verifiability is the main technical gate for rapid capability gains.
  • The three factors are roughly independent axes that combine multiplicatively to produce the narrow top.
Inferences:
  • Non-verifiable professional domains (strategy, writing, management) will stay in the middle tiers until good verifiers or new training methods appear.
  • Insiders at labs see further than outsiders partly because of access, so outsider debates about 'plateaus' are structurally under-informed.
At the top of the funnel, human review and comprehension are starting to fall behind agent output. strength: low
Explicit premises:
  • Swarms of thousands of agents work together for weeks and produce work equal to years of top-professional effort.
  • The post-hoc 'archeology' of the OpenAI-HF incident is still going months later.
  • Mathematicians may be poring over 722 frontier-mathematics manuscripts for a long time.
Implicit premises:
  • Human reading and verification throughput is roughly fixed while agent output scales with compute.
  • Agent output is not self-explaining or cheaply auditable by other agents at the needed trust level.
  • Time spent understanding an artifact is a good proxy for review capacity being exceeded.
Inferences:
  • Verification and comprehension, not generation, become the limiting factor at the frontier - a more extreme version of the review bottleneck already reported in ordinary software teams.
  • Trust in top-tier outputs will increasingly have to rest on automated verification or on delayed human review.

Narratives

  • Karpathy's own personal-experience anecdote: less than a year ago he was writing code by hand, typing memorized commands character by character and occasionally pressing Tab to autocomplete a chunk; now agents complete multi-week projects from a prompt.
    → Shows how fast the tier-three experience arrived and gives the claim first-person credibility ('this has all happened very quickly and recently').
  • Non-tech friends who (reasonably, imo) say they have not much use for AI, and executives who understand AI only second-hand because their work is talking to people.
    → Supports the claim that lower-tier skepticism is rational given the evidence those users encounter.
  • The viral clips of OpenAI's free Advanced Voice mode fumbling 'should I drive or walk to the carwash' contrasted with the top paid Codex model working an hour to restructure a whole codebase or exploit vulnerabilities.
    → Embodies the 'speaking past each other' claim: both observations are true for the same vendor at the same time.
  • People still doing 'archeology' on the OpenAI-HF incident months later, and mathematicians poring over 722 frontier-math manuscripts.
    → Illustrates the claim that human review and comprehension are falling behind at the top.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why do people disagree so sharply and talk past each other about how capable LLMs are?
    Because they have directly experienced very different things: experience depth is a funnel set by ambition, jagged capability and access tier, and each tier's evidence is accurate for that tier.
  • ⭕ How many people occupy each tier of exposure to LLM capability?
    Only order-of-magnitude napkin math is offered, explicitly as such; no sources or definitions of tier boundaries.
  • ⭕ Will the divide close as the broader tiers catch up, or will jaggedness keep most knowledge work in the middle tiers?
    Not addressed; the post implies both a vertical curve and a persistently jagged profile without reconciling them.
  • ⭕ What follows, for institutions and society, when human review and comprehension fall behind agent output at the frontier?
    Only flagged as a symptom ('starting to fall behind'); no analysis of consequences or responses.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe funnel numbers (6B, 1-2B, 20M, ~5,000) are explicitly napkin math and are given without any sources, definitions of tier boundaries (what counts as 'barely come in contact' or 'frontier-grade professional use'), or error bars. They carry most of the rhetorical force of the 'sharp funnel' image but cannot be audited, and several (the 20M figure, the ~5,000 with internal access) are especially hard to derive.
    Read next: Usage-diffusion data on who actually uses frontier agentic tools (see extract-how-organizations-use-ai-evidence-chatgpt) and adoption surveys such as the Anthropic Economic Index or Pew; compare against the tier sizes.
  • uninformedThe top-tier claims are reported rather than shown: swarms of thousands of agents over weeks, zero-days, new science, the 'OpenAI-HF incident' and '722 manuscripts' are named without links or context, so a reader outside the loop cannot check them. The narrator is himself a prominent practitioner with a stake in the narrative, and the 'external world has seen the preview' point is the only external anchor, so the upper tiers of the funnel are partly taken on authority.
    Read next: Primary reports on the incidents and manuscripts mentioned; independent evaluations of long-horizon multi-agent runs (compare extract-project-glasswing-mythos and the vendor-incentive gaps recorded against it).
  • illogicalThe funnel metaphor collapses three roughly orthogonal axes (ambition, domain, access) into one nested ordering. A heavy paid-tier user working on ambitious non-verifiable problems (e.g. a strategist or lawyer) is high on access and ambition but low on domain, so does not sit cleanly in any tier; the single funnel implies a monotone path that the drivers themselves do not produce. The staysaasy correlation (awe tracks coding use) is also read as exposure causing conviction, with no consideration of selection (the already-impressed use more).
    Read next: Evidence on adoption and perceived value by profession (see extract-lawyers-are-quietly-becoming-builders for a non-code deep-user case); studies separating selection from exposure effects in AI attitudes.
  • incompleteThe post is silent on whether the funnel is a transient lag or a stable structure: if the top tier is a leading indicator, the broader tiers should converge toward it; if jaggedness is driven by verifiability and lab economics, large parts of knowledge work may never leave the middle tiers. 'Curve going vertical' and 'jaggedness' pull in different directions and are not reconciled.
    Read next: Extract-mollick-co-existence-end-of-co-intelligence#g1 and extract-llms-cant-jump-zahavy on whether coding gains transfer to non-verifiable domains; Karpathy's skill-issue vs jaggedness tension recorded in extract-karpathy-code-agents-autoresearch#g6.
  • incompleteThe most consequential claim - that human review and comprehension are starting to fall behind at the top - is supported by two anecdotes and no analysis of what it means: how fast it will worsen, who is accountable when outputs cannot be understood, or whether automated verification can substitute for human comprehension. It is flagged as a symptom of the moment but left unexamined.
    Read next: Work on review capacity and comprehension debt in the corpus (extract-github-stacked-pull-requests, extract-osmani-human-judgment-relocates, extract-williamson-trust-but-verify-ai-tech-writing) and on verification capacity as the scarce resource.
  • incompleteThe post describes the divide but offers no implication or prescription: it does not say what a mid-tier professional, an executive, or an organization should do differently once they accept the funnel (for example, how a middle-tier leader can calibrate decisions about a technology whose frontier they have not seen). It ends on 'the weirdness of the moment'.
    Read next: Practitioner and leadership material on closing the experience gap in organizations, e.g. extract-fowler-fragments-february-13 (hands-on practice converts skeptics) and extract-anders-nygaards-post (tool choice as a funnel lever).
