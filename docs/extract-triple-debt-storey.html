From Technical Debt to Cognitive and Intent Debt: Rethinking Software Health in the Age of AI

Margaret-Anne Storey · pdf · 2026-07-28 Reader 🖥️

Architecture Decision RecordsCognitive DebtCognitive SurrenderIntent DebtMental Models & UnderstandingOrganizational LegibilityProxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)Technical Debt
Software debt should be reasoned about across three interacting layers of a software system rather than one -- technical debt (in code), cognitive debt (in the team's shared understanding), and intent debt (in externalized rationale and goals) -- because generative AI is simultaneously shrinking technical debt through automation while accelerating the other two, previously under-recognized and increasingly consequential, forms of debt; managing software health in the AI era therefore requires treating shared understanding and externalized intent as first-class deliverables with the same discipline historically reserved for code quality.

theoretical · expository/conceptual-framework paper (arXiv preprint) — Introduces a named framework (the 'triple debt model') via a mix of a single illustrative anecdote (an entrepreneurship-course team), synthesis of prior literature (Naur, Cunningham, Kruchten, Hutchins, Wegner, Kahneman, Shaw & Nave), and citations of the author's own concurrent/forthcoming empirical work (Starr & Storey 2026; Miller et al. 2026; Alakmeh et al. 2026). It is not itself an empirical study — no new data is collected or analyzed here — and the author explicitly flags three questions as unresolved ('Points for Debate') rather than argued to a conclusion, which is unusually honest epistemic hygiene for a framework-introduction paper. This is the primary source that a chain of derivative pieces already in this corpus (Osmani's 'The Intent Debt', likely Bilić Arar's 'two kinds of debt', Fowler's Fragments Feb 13, the 'five studies' extraction) cite or paraphrase without the corpus previously having ingested the original.

Key terms

  • Triple debt model
    A framework positing three interacting, independently-varying types of software debt -- technical (code), cognitive (people), intent (artifacts) -- each mapping to one of three layers a software system 'really' consists of.
  • Cognitive debt Cognitive Debt
    A team-level, project-level property: the accumulated erosion of shared understanding (Naur's 'theory of the system') across a team over time -- not an individual's confusion, and not reducible to the state of the code. Explicitly distinguished from Kosmyna et al.'s use of the same term for measurable individual neural-engagement reduction, and from Alakmeh et al.'s 'comprehension debt' (the code-vs-understanding gap) -- Storey's usage is specifically the team-level, longitudinal dimension.
  • Intent debt Intent Debt
    The absence or erosion of explicit, externalized rationale, goals, and constraints that guide how a system evolves -- lives in artifacts (or their absence), not in code or in people's heads. Distinguished from mere 'missing documentation': it specifically concerns whether goals/constraints/rationale are captured anywhere a human or an AI agent can consult, and is framed as the layer that is hardest to recover once lost ('intent is best captured at the moment key decisions are made... recovering it later can be difficult and sometimes impossible').
  • Cognitive surrender Cognitive Surrender
    (Attributed to Shaw & Nave 2026) Adopting AI outputs with minimal scrutiny, bypassing both intuition and deliberate reasoning (System 1/System 2, after Kahneman). Distinguished sharply from cognitive offloading (a rational, strategic delegation of a discrete task to a tool, e.g. a linter) -- surrender can be intentional but corrodes critical engagement, and it inflates the surrendering developer's confidence even when the AI's output is wrong.
  • Theory of the system (Naur 1985) Proxy Validity (Construct Validity of Measurement Proxies)
    The dynamic, distributed mental model that developers, architects, and other stakeholders hold of how a system works and can be reasoned about -- explicitly not something any single individual needs to hold in full; what matters is that sufficient shared understanding exists across the team.
  • Executable intent
    BDD specifications and tests deliberately designed to capture purpose rather than merely verify current behavior (citing Wynne 2017 -- the Cucumber Book); these artifacts can be executed, and their failure signals that the system has drifted from its intent, as distinct from ordinary regression tests that only encode prior behavior without capturing why.

Arguments

Managing software system health in the AI era requires proactive, monitored attention to cognitive and intent debt, not just technical debt. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • AI is reducing technical debt (via automated refactoring/review/test-generation) while removing the friction that used to force developers to build understanding as they wrote code.
  • The three debts causally reinforce each other (intent -> cognitive -> technical -> cognitive), so neglecting any one undermines efforts on the others.
  • Cognitive and intent debt are harder to detect than technical debt (no rich tooling ecosystem exists for them yet) and, per the paper's own claim, intent debt can become unrecoverable once lost.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizations will not naturally reallocate investment toward understanding and intent capture unless explicitly told to, because delivery-speed pressure defaults attention to the most visible, most measurable debt (technical).
  • The practices proposed (walkthroughs, ADRs, executable intent, retrospectives) are sufficient countermeasures if adopted -- the paper does not test this, and does not address the organizational-incentive question of whether teams under throughput pressure will actually adopt costly practices whose payoff is diffuse and delayed.
Inferences:
  • From 'AI removes friction that built understanding' + 'the three debts reinforce each other' to 'attention must be proactive across all three layers, not reactive to the one that shows up first' (technical debt, being visible, would otherwise absorb all the attention).

Narratives

  • An entrepreneurship course Storey taught: student teams built software products over a semester; by week eight one team hit a wall where simple changes broke things unexpectedly and progress stalled. The team initially blamed technical debt (messy code, shortcuts), but on inspection no one could explain why design decisions had been made or how parts of the system were supposed to interoperate -- their shared 'theory of the system' had fragmented and they had never written down the rationale behind decisions. They had accumulated cognitive and intent debt faster than technical debt, and it paralyzed them.
    → Opens the paper and is explicitly framed as not an isolated case (cross-referenced to Willison 2026); it is the paper's sole concrete illustrative case and is the empirical seed from which the whole triple-debt taxonomy is generalized.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What is the 'triple debt model' and how do its three debts differ?
    Technical debt lives in code and limits how systems can change; cognitive debt lives in people and limits how teams can reason about change; intent debt lives in artifacts and limits whether the system still reflects what was meant to be built.
  • ✅ How does cognitive surrender differ from ordinary cognitive offloading?
    Offloading is a rational, strategic delegation of a discrete task to a tool; surrender is adopting AI output with minimal scrutiny, bypassing reasoning altogether, and it inflates confidence even when the AI is wrong.
  • ⭕ Should teams invest in documenting intent, given the cost?
    Explicitly left as an open 'Point for Debate' -- the paper states some practitioners consider it not worth the effort, others consider it critical, and calls the debate 'far from settled.'
  • ⭕ Can AI reliably help make implicit/tacit knowledge explicit (i.e. can it pay down intent debt for you)?
    Left open; the paper notes some practitioners propose this while others argue the value of documentation lies precisely in the human understanding gained while producing it, which AI-generated documentation would bypass.
  • ⭕ Is the three-layer taxonomy (technical/cognitive/intent) exhaustive, or are there other candidate debt categories (e.g. process debt, dependency debt, data debt, organizational debt)?
    Not addressed anywhere in the paper -- the three layers are asserted as 'a software system... exists across three distinct layers' without discussion of alternative or additional candidate categories.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe paper never argues that the three-layer taxonomy is exhaustive, nor does it engage candidate additional debt categories (process debt, dependency debt, data debt, organizational debt) that this corpus's own downstream reading of Osmani's derivative article already flagged as missing. This confirms the omission originates in the primary source itself, not merely in secondary treatments of it.
    Read next: Avgeriou & Kruchten's broader technical-debt-classification literature; this corpus's organizational-debt concept and extract-tbm-430 (portfolio economics) for a competing single-axis treatment of 'debt' that might subsume or reframe some of these.
  • incompleteThe paper lists qualitative diagnostic signals for both cognitive debt (five signals) and intent debt (three patterns) but proposes no quantitative measurement instrument or threshold -- 'Monitor the three layers in tandem' suggests onboarding-time tracking and knowledge-concentration metrics only in passing, without operationalizing any of them.
    Read next: DORA/SPACE-style measurement-framework literature; this corpus's own osmani-intent-debt#g2 gap raised the identical measurement-thinness problem for Osmani's derivative treatment -- it turns out the primary source is no more quantified.
  • illogicalThe claim that intent debt, once lost, can be 'difficult and sometimes impossible' to recover is contrasted with technical debt as something that 'can be addressed later' -- treating technical-debt reversibility as a near-universal property. This corpus's own portfolio-economics treatment of debt (TBM 430) argues reversibility is quadrant-dependent, not intrinsic to the debt type -- a liquidated, over-coupled legacy asset can be just as practically unrecoverable as lost intent.
    Read next: extract-tbm-430-incubate-compound-refinance-liquidate (the value/carrying-cost portfolio framing) directly complicates this asymmetry claim.
  • uninformedThe paper's four practice recommendations are entirely team/individual-practice level (walkthroughs, ADRs, executable intent, monitoring) and never engage organizational incentive structures -- e.g. whether managers who measure throughput or visible agent activity will actually reward the diffuse, delayed payoff of externalization work over shipping speed. Kjetil's systems-thinking (Meadows) lens would flag this as an intervention at a low-leverage 'parameter' level (adopt this practice) rather than the higher-leverage 'rule' or 'goal' level (what does the organization actually reward).
    Read next: Donella Meadows' leverage-points framework; this corpus's own osmani-intent-debt#g1 gap, which raised the identical individual-discipline-vs-organizational-incentive blind spot for Osmani's derivative piece and now applies equally to the primary source it draws on.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • The Intent Debt · g2 — Storey's paper provides substantially more diagnostic detail than Osmani's derivative treatment: five named signals for cognitive debt (resistance to change, unexpected results, slow onboarding, loss of transactive memory, low bus factor) and three for intent debt (behavior drift, AI agents struggling despite technically correct output, loss of articulated constraints). This partially closes the diagnostic-thinness gap Osmani's g2 raised, though it remains qualitative rather than a proposed metric or instrument -- see this document's own gaps for the remaining quantification shortfall.
  • The Intent Debt · g5 — Storey's paper explicitly names and develops 'executable intent' (BDD specifications and tests designed to capture purpose, not just verify behavior) as a practice, plus Architecture Decision Records and Domain-Driven Design -- exactly the fourth paydown lever (intent-specifying tests / specification-by-example) Osmani's g5 flagged as implied but undeveloped in his derivative piece.
  • The Intent Debt · g6 — Read directly, Storey's arXiv paper does not address taxonomy exhaustiveness or engage alternative debt categories (process, dependency, data, organizational debt) at all -- confirming that this gap originates in the primary source itself, not merely in Osmani's secondary treatment of it. The gap is not resolved, but the open question 'does Storey address this?' is now answered (no), and is recorded as this document's own g1.
