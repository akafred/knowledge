State Of AI-Assisted Software Development

DORA · pdf · 2025-09-23 Reader 🖥️

AI's effect on software delivery is not a property of the tool but of the organizational system it is embedded in: across nearly 5,000 developers, AI consistently amplified whatever team dynamics already existed — toward 'harmonious high-achievement' or toward a 'legacy bottleneck' — and organizations only convert individual AI-driven effectiveness gains into durable team, product, and organizational performance by deliberately building a cluster of systemic capabilities (AI-policy clarity, data health, version-control discipline, small batches, user-centricity, and platform quality) and treating AI adoption as a whole-system transformation rather than a tooling rollout.

practical · mixed — The primary DORA 2025 'State of AI-assisted Software Development' report: a ~39k-word mixed-method survey report (nearly 5,000 respondents, 78+ interviews) interleaved with signed guest essays from DORA/Google researchers and outside experts (Gene Kim, Eirini Kalliamvakou, Matt Beane). Practical throughout — every empirical finding is paired with prescriptive advice for technology leaders — but grounded in statistical analysis (cluster analysis, SEM/CFA) rather than opinion.

Key terms

  • AI as amplifier AI as Amplifier
    AI's primary causal role in software development: it magnifies the strengths of high-performing organizations and the dysfunctions of struggling ones, rather than having a uniform effect.
  • Team archetype / team profile
    One of seven statistically-derived clusters of teams (e.g. 'Harmonious high-achiever', 'Legacy bottleneck', 'Constrained by process') defined jointly by throughput, instability, team performance, product performance, individual effectiveness, valuable work, friction, and burnout — not by delivery metrics alone.
  • DORA AI Capabilities Model
    A set of seven organizational capabilities (clear AI stance, healthy data ecosystem, AI-accessible internal data, strong version control, working in small batches, user-centric focus, quality internal platform) empirically shown to interact with AI adoption — i.e., to amplify (or in one case, without them, reverse) AI's effect on outcomes.
  • Value stream management (VSM)
    The practice of visualizing, analyzing, and improving the end-to-end flow of work from idea to customer; in this report specifically framed as the mechanism that keeps local AI-driven acceleration from being absorbed or wasted downstream.
  • Systems view / organizations as systems
    The view (credited to Deming and the Theory of Constraints) that organizational performance emerges from the interaction of interdependent parts — teams, processes, policies, infrastructure — such that improving one part (e.g. code-generation speed) does not improve overall throughput unless the constraining downstream parts (testing, review, deployment, compliance) evolve too.
  • Continuous AI
    A proposed AI-native collaboration pattern (credited to GitHub Next) in which an AI system persists as a living, continuously-updated participant in the team's workflow rather than a one-shot tool invocation — perceiving project events, adjusting direction with the team.

Arguments

Organizations seeking AI ROI should prioritize investment in their internal platform and data ecosystem over simply procuring more AI tool licenses. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Tool-only AI adoption creates localized productivity pockets that are often lost to downstream chaos.
  • Platform quality and data-ecosystem health both show high-certainty amplification of AI's effect on organizational performance.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizational investment attention and budget are effectively zero-sum, so platform investment competes directly with AI tool spend for priority.
  • The organization's existing platform is a reasonable foundation to build on rather than itself being technical debt that would need to be rebuilt first.
Inferences:
  • A platform or data-engineering team with low political visibility should, on this evidence, receive budget priority over additional AI seat licenses.
Software organizations must deliberately measure and manage skill development alongside productivity, or they will erode the junior-to-senior pipeline that produces their own future senior engineers.
Explicit premises:
  • Default AI use reduces hands-on opportunities for novices across more than 31 studied occupations, with an emerging parallel pattern in software engineering.
  • In Beane's broader research, the best productivity outcomes were achieved only when organizations simultaneously insisted on skill development.
Implicit premises:
  • The productivity metrics organizations currently track and act on (adoption rate, lines of code, PRs merged) will crowd out attention to any skill-development metric that isn't similarly tracked.
  • The three-generation apprenticeship model was functioning adequately before AI, so its disruption is attributable to AI rather than to pre-existing organizational dysfunction.
Inferences:
  • Organizations that don't instrument AI-mediated skill transmission now will face a senior-engineer shortage once the current senior cohort ages out, with no mid-level cohort trained to replace them.
A clear, communicated AI stance is a comparatively low-cost, high-leverage intervention for organizations adopting AI-assisted development. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Interview evidence shows developers routinely under-use AI out of fear of overstepping an unstated organizational norm.
  • Survey evidence shows, with high certainty, that a clear and communicated AI stance amplifies AI's positive effect on individual effectiveness, organizational performance, and friction.
Implicit premises:
  • The capability's benefit comes from the clarity and awareness of the stance, not its specific content — implying the mechanism is organizational trust/psychological safety rather than any particular permission granted.
Inferences:
  • An organization could realize this benefit faster by issuing an imperfect-but-clear policy immediately rather than spending months deliberating the 'correct' policy.

Narratives

  • At Adidas's gen-AI pilot (nearly 1,000 developers), teams with loosely-coupled architectures and fast feedback loops saw 20-30% productivity gains and 50% more 'Happy Time,' while teams tightly coupled to ERP systems with slow feedback loops saw little or no AI benefit at all.
    → Concretizes proposition 1 (AI as amplifier) and the implicit premise that architecture is a prerequisite, not a side effect, of AI payoff.
  • At Booking.com (3,000+ developers), uneven uptake of coding assistants was traced not to tool quality but to missing training; once developers learned to give AI more explicit instructions and context, merge requests rose up to 30% and job satisfaction increased.
    → Foreshadows the Capabilities Model's central claim that building organizational/behavioral capability — not distributing the tool — is the actual lever on AI payoff.
  • An interviewed developer describes self-censoring their AI experimentation because they didn't know whether their organization's unstated norms would approve of it, saying they didn't want to use AI 'in secret' but also weren't sure it was encouraged.
    → Concretizes proposition 5/argument 3 (clear AI stance) by showing the lived cost of ambiguity: both under-use from fear and the discomfort of operating in a policy vacuum.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Does AI actually enable better software delivery and organizational performance, the same question DORA asked of DevOps a decade ago?
    Conditionally yes: AI now improves throughput (a reversal from 2024) but still increases instability, and the net benefit depends on whether the organization has built the seven capabilities in the DORA AI Capabilities Model.
  • ✅ What organizational conditions determine whether AI adoption helps or harms a team, rather than just how much AI individuals use?
    The DORA AI Capabilities Model: seven capabilities (AI-stance clarity, data health, AI-accessible data, version control discipline, small batches, user-centricity, platform quality) that interact with AI adoption to amplify its benefits — and, for user-centric focus specifically, whose absence turns AI adoption harmful.
  • ✅ Does using AI assistance erode developers' sense of ownership, meaning, or motivation in their work?
    Mostly no measurable change across most sociocognitive constructs tested, with a modest increase in authentic pride and a small (21%) risk of reduced psychological ownership.
  • ⭕ What happens to the junior-to-senior skill pipeline when experienced engineers can self-serve answers from AI instead of mentoring juniors through hands-on work?
    Named and partially explored only in Matt Beane's standalone guest essay, which reports early, cross-occupation evidence of apprenticeship disruption but offers no resolution specific to software engineering and proposes only a future research agenda (AI-assisted measurement of skill development).

Gaps

  • incompleteThe DORA AI Capabilities Model's seven capabilities — the report's headline, survey-validated deliverable — include no apprenticeship or skill-transmission capability, even though Matt Beane's own essay in the same report argues default AI use measurably erodes the junior-to-senior pipeline across many occupations and (early signs suggest) in software engineering too. The model and the warning sit side by side without being reconciled.
    Read next: Track whether DORA's next annual survey operationalizes a skill-development or apprenticeship-health measure as an eighth capability; read Matt Beane's ongoing SkillBench research directly for software-specific findings.
  • illogicalThe report's statistical methodology explicitly disclaims causal interpretation (its own footnotes describe the regressions as comparisons, not effects), yet its practical-advice sections phrase every one of the seven capabilities as something leaders should causally 'invest in' or 'build' to amplify AI's benefit. This leaves open whether building a capability causes the amplification, or whether already well-run organizations simply both have the capability and separately extract more value from AI.
    Read next: Look for a longitudinal/panel-data follow-up DORA report, or independent experimental/quasi-experimental evidence isolating any one of the seven capabilities from general organizational maturity.
  • incompleteQuality internal platforms are reported to increase AI-era developer friction even as they improve organizational performance, but the report doesn't distinguish how much of that friction is the deliberate, 'right kind' of guardrail (e.g. security gatekeeping) versus avoidable platform-usability debt — a distinction a platform team would need in order to act on the finding.
    Read next: A practitioner-level study decomposing AI-era platform friction into 'intended guardrail' vs. 'usability debt' categories.
  • incompleteThe report's two major empirical halves — the seven team archetypes (cluster analysis) and the sociocognitive impact findings (pride, ownership, skill reprioritization) — are never cross-tabulated against each other, so it's unknown whether, say, the 'Legacy bottleneck' cluster shows different ownership or pride effects from AI than the 'Harmonious high-achiever' cluster.
    Read next: A follow-up analysis joining cluster membership with the sociocognitive constructs in DORA's underlying dataset.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • KI, utvikling og følelser · g2 — Partially closes it: Beane's research gives an operational, institutional mechanism for the transmission problem (pair programming as tacit-knowledge transfer; the three-generation model; using AI itself to measure skill development from developer-AI interaction logs) — but doesn't engage the craft-history analogy (Sennett, Crawford) the gap also calls for, so the empirical/institutional half closes while the historical-analogy half remains open.
  • It Ain't Broke: Why Software Fundamentals Matter More Than Ever · g5 — Beane's cross-occupation research (31+ occupations) directly answers whether AI-mediated 'gray box delegation' creates a competency gap: yes, by default, because experts self-serve with AI and stop creating the hands-on opportunities novices need — with early signs of the same pattern specifically in software engineering.
  • The Future Of Software Engineering · g4 — Beane's essay supplies the apprenticeship-model literature and specificity the gap asked for: the at-risk 'fundamentals' are the tacit, hands-on skills transmitted via the three-generation (junior/mid/senior) model, and effective retraining means jointly measuring and incentivizing skill development alongside productivity rather than offering generic AI-tool training.
