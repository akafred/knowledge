Shifting from outputs to outcomes fails not because the idea is wrong but because teams adopt the language of outcomes while keeping the psychology of outputs — chasing certainty, easy measurement, and reputational credit instead of committing to the qualitative judgment, tolerance for ambiguity, and honest attribution that outcome-orientation actually demands.

practical · expository — An enumerative list of failure modes, each stated as a diagnosis-plus-implicit-prescription. It is practical (aimed at what teams should stop doing) but delivered expository/aphoristically rather than as an argued case — there is no sustained chain of reasoning, just eleven independently standing observations unified by a shared underlying claim about what 'outcomes over outputs' actually requires.

Key terms

  • outcomes
    Changes in customer or business reality (behavior, value delivered) that a team's work is meant to produce — as opposed to the artifacts shipped. Cutler treats 'outcomes' as inherently harder to pin down, slower to confirm, and contestable in their causal attribution to any one team's work.
  • outputs
    Shipped artifacts/features — the thing 'outcome-centricity' was supposed to move teams away from privileging, but which persists in disguised form (e.g. #4's performative outcome-claiming is output-thinking wearing outcome language).
  • leading vs. lagging outcomes
    A distinction within outcomes themselves: leading outcomes are near-term and closable within a feedback loop a team can observe; lagging outcomes are foundational, qualitative, and only visible over a longer horizon. Cutler's #1 claims teams routinely fail to hold both in view at once.
  • metrics as hypotheses
    Metrics are provisional instruments for testing a belief about value, not a target to be perfected before acting — perfection-seeking (#2) is itself evidence of misunderstanding what a metric is for.
  • claiming outcomes (performatively)
    The act of declaring credit for an outcome through narrative framing rather than through a defensible causal link — 'retrospective rationalization' that makes outcome-claiming as gameable as output-shipping ever was.

Arguments

Outcome-centricity, as typically implemented, does not actually replace output-thinking — it re-encodes the same failure modes (gaming metrics, seeking easy credit, avoiding ambiguity) in outcome vocabulary. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Teams chase near-term, easily attributable outcomes over foundational, lagging ones (#1).
  • Teams demand metric perfection instead of treating metrics as testable hypotheses (#2).
  • Teams favor easy-to-measure ideas over powerful, harder-to-measure ones (#5).
  • Outcome-claiming becomes performative credit-seeking once the spirit of experimentation is lost (#4).
  • Reported outcomes cluster around quarter-end/promo cycles, evidencing narrative management rather than honest reporting (#9).
  • Attribution requirements push teams toward avoidance or fabricated causal stories (#11).
Implicit premises:
  • The psychological drivers behind output-chasing (certainty-seeking, desire for individual credit, aversion to ambiguity) are stable dispositions that survive a change in vocabulary unless the underlying incentive structure changes.
  • An organization's stated methodology (outcomes-over-outputs) and its actual measurement/reward mechanics can diverge, and it is the mechanics, not the stated methodology, that determine behavior.
Inferences:
  • If the diagnosis is correct, simply renaming output KPIs as 'outcome' KPIs (without changing what is rewarded, timed, or attributed) would predictably reproduce the same eleven failure modes under new labels.
  • A team could exhibit genuine outcome-orientation only if it can tolerate a metric it hasn't perfected, hold conviction about a bet it can't yet attribute cleanly, and report a setback without professional cost — which makes outcome-orientation as much a psychological-safety and incentive-design problem as a metrics-design problem.
Demanding causal attribution of outcomes to specific work is itself a design flaw in how many organizations implement outcome-centricity. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Teams start believing they must prove their particular work caused the outcome (#11).
Implicit premises:
  • Most valuable outcomes are overdetermined — produced by the interaction of many teams' work, market conditions, and timing — so clean single-team causal attribution is frequently impossible to obtain honestly.
  • An organization that requires attribution as a condition for credit will get either avoidance of ambiguous-but-valuable work, or fabricated attribution narratives, because those are the only two ways to satisfy an impossible-to-honestly-satisfy requirement.
Inferences:
  • The fix implied but not stated by the author is to fund and reward contribution to outcomes probabilistically or qualitatively (a portfolio/systems view) rather than requiring proof of individual causal ownership — which is the same corrective the corpus's proxy-validity material proposes for AI-ROI measurement.

Narratives

  • The imagined C-suite executives who lived through an earlier 'product transformation,' got 'on a high horse' about outcomes, and are now rebelling because teams 'lost the thread.'
    → Supports proposition #10 (evangelism vs. efficacy) — it is Cutler's compressed anecdote for how outcome-centricity decays into a slogan divorced from the daily practice (discovery, shipping, learning, absorbing setbacks) it was meant to describe, and for why leadership sours on a good idea once it becomes theater.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why does the well-intentioned shift from measuring outputs to measuring outcomes so often fail to change team behavior in practice?
    Cutler resolves it by enumerating eleven concrete failure modes, converging on the implicit claim that teams adopt outcome vocabulary without adopting the underlying tolerance for ambiguity, patience, and qualitative judgment the shift actually requires.
  • ⭕ What should an organization do instead, given these eleven failure modes?
    The post is entirely diagnostic; it names the ten (eleven) ways teams go wrong but does not prescribe a positive replacement framework, incentive redesign, or measurement structure that would avoid the failure modes.
  • ⭕ How should attribution of outcomes actually be handled if strict causal proof is neither honestly obtainable nor desirable to demand (per #11)?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe post diagnoses eleven failure modes but offers no positive alternative: no proposed measurement cadence, incentive structure, or organizational practice that would let a team hold 'metrics as hypotheses' and 'strategic conviction' simultaneously without falling into paralysis (#3) or perfectionism (#2). Cutler names the trap but not the exit.
    Read next: Read Cutler's own longer-form writing (e.g. his 'North Star Framework' and 'Outcome/Output/Opportunity Tree' pieces) or product-discovery literature (Cagan, Torres) for the positive framework this post assumes but doesn't restate.
  • incomplete#11's attribution problem — 'confusing outcomes with attribution' — is stated as an observation but not analyzed: the post doesn't distinguish cases where attribution is genuinely impossible (overdetermined outcomes) from cases where it is merely hard but tractable with better instrumentation (e.g. A/B tests, holdout groups), which would call for different remedies.
    Read next: This corpus's extract-tbm-437-tokens-hours-points-proxies.json works this exact problem for AI-spend ROI, proposing 'the team as the indivisible cost unit' plus an explicit causal model instead of attributing outcomes to individual units of work — a template for resolving Cutler's #11 in a specific domain.
  • illogicalThe post implicitly treats 'outcome-based measures' as harder to game than output measures (the standard critique it's reacting against), yet its own #4/#9/#10 show outcomes are gameable too — just via narrative/timing manipulation (retrospective rationalization, quarter-end clustering, evangelism-over-efficacy) rather than volume inflation. The post doesn't reconcile this tension: it never explicitly revises the premise that switching to outcomes solves the gaming problem, even though its own examples show it merely relocates the gaming.
    Read next: Read alongside Goodhart's Law material in this corpus (extract-8-engineering-metrics-ai-broke.json, extract-shore-ai-money-worth-part1.json) which makes explicit what Cutler leaves implicit: any measure that becomes a target, outcome or output, is subject to gaming — the fix is retiring metrics after they've been learned from, not choosing 'harder to fake' metric categories.
  • incompleteNo mention of psychological safety or incentive/reward-system design, even though several failure modes (#4 credit-seeking, #9 cherry-picking around promo time, #11 fabricating causal stories) are natural responses to a reward system that punishes honest reporting of ambiguity, setbacks, or unattributable work. The post diagnoses the symptom (behavior) without naming the likely structural cause (what gets rewarded).
