The Work Behind Delegation: A Framework for Supervising AI Coding Agents

Yeon Su Park; Nadia Arvi; Hae Ri Lee; Sehoon Lim; Qianou Ma; Juho Kim · pdf · 2026-09-22 Reader 🖥️

Agent Planning ArtifactsContext StoreHooksHuman-in-the-LoopMulti-Agent OrchestrationScope DisciplineSupervisory Programming
Developer supervision of AI coding agents is not a single activity performed at fixed checkpoints but a connected process organized into seven distinct, loop-connected stages (Plan, Monitor, Wait, Review, Teach, Manual Fix, Update Assets) -- a reconfiguration of Sheridan's classical five-stage human-supervisory-control framework made necessary by agents' greater autonomy -- and developers manage the resulting cross-stage demands (alignment, quality, understanding) by concentrating effort in planning, distributing supervisory work to other agents and automated hooks, and externalizing recurring corrections into reusable assets.

theoretical · mixed — An HCI research paper: primarily theoretical/descriptive (building and empirically validating a framework for how developer supervision of AI coding agents is organized), with a practical coda (design implications for agent builders and tool builders) drawn from the theoretical findings rather than argued independently.

Key terms

  • supervisory stage
    One of seven distinct phases of developer activity when overseeing a delegated agent task (Plan, Monitor, Wait, Review, Teach, Manual Fix, Update Assets), each with its own supervisory goal, distinguished empirically from Sheridan's five by how developers themselves segmented and diagrammed their own workflows.
  • Wait
    A newly identified stage, distinct from Monitor, in which a developer deliberately disengages attention from a running agent -- turning to another task, another agent, or an unrelated activity -- because they trust the agent to continue productively without observation for an extended period.
  • Review
    A distinct stage (absent as a separate category in Sheridan's framework, where assessment is folded into Monitor) in which developers assess a completed, bounded chunk of agent work against their original intent -- checking for the gap between intent and result that, per participants, 'only becomes visible once you get to this stage.'
  • Update Assets
    An optional stage, replacing Sheridan's Learn, in which developers externalize lessons from the current task into persistent, shared resources (AGENT.md/CLAUDE.md files, documentation, linters, retrospective-generated skill files) that feed forward into future Plan stages -- learning encoded into the system rather than retained only in the supervisor's own memory.
  • delegated supervision
    Supervisory loops (monitoring, review, correction) configured in advance to run via other agents or deterministic hooks without developer involvement at each occurrence -- distinguished from collaborative supervision (shared with an agent) and developer-retained supervision (kept entirely by the human).

Arguments

Developers should invest disproportionate effort in planning (Plan) rather than spreading equal supervisory effort across all seven stages.
Explicit premises:
  • Well-structured plans let developers shift from active Monitor to disengaged Wait, freeing attention for other agents or tasks
  • Stronger plans are associated with fewer repeated Teach cycles -- in the best case 'the whole thing can run in literally one pass, no looping needed'
  • Planning builds the developer's own understanding of the architecture before any code is delegated, which later supervisory stages (Review, Manual Fix) depend on
Implicit premises:
  • Supervisory effort invested early is more leveraged than the same effort invested after problems have already accumulated in the implementation
  • A developer's limited attention is the scarce resource being allocated across the seven stages, so moving cost from later, compounding stages to a single bounded Plan stage is a net efficiency gain
Inferences:
  • Systems and practices that help developers articulate, stress-test, and keep a plan visible throughout execution should yield outsized reductions in total supervisory cost compared to tools that only improve later-stage review or correction

Narratives

  • P18's account of why Review had to become its own stage: 'The plan cannot capture all of my intent -- some things get compressed, or it fills them in by guessing, and those only become visible once you get to this [Review] stage... What I look for in Review is the gap between my intent and the result.'
    → Grounds the structural claim that Review is a qualitatively different act from Monitor -- assessing a completed, bounded artifact against original intent, not watching an agent work in real time -- and explains why the framework departs from Sheridan's original single Monitor stage.
  • The MISTAKES.md pattern: one developer, 'tired of writing the same review comments,' built a static linter to catch the issue automatically before commit; another maintains a MISTAKES.md file where every agent mistake gets documented, paired with a CLAUDE.md rule instructing the agent to record them; a third runs a milestone-end retrospective where 'the retro edits or writes skill files and meta-learnings as memory.'
    → Illustrates concretely how the 'externalizing repeated supervision into assets' strategy is actually implemented day to day, and why developers describe the result as a 'compounding effect' rather than a one-off fix.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Can Sheridan's classical five-stage human-supervisory-control framework (built for automated systems like autopilots executing human-specified instructions) adequately describe how developers supervise AI coding agents, which determine their own approach to a delegated goal?
    No, not without reconfiguration -- the 19-developer study shows Teach is absorbed into Plan, Monitor splits into Monitor and Wait, Intervene splits into Teach and Manual Fix, and Learn becomes an optional Update Assets stage that externalizes learning into persistent assets rather than only updating the supervisor's own understanding.
  • ✅ Does the seven-stage framework generalize beyond the 19 observed lab sessions to the broader population of developers supervising AI coding agents in real-world use?
    The paper applies the framework as an analytical lens to 102 Reddit threads (12,912 comments) from three developer subreddits spanning a 3-month window, and reports that the demands and strategies identified in the lab study also appear at scale in these naturalistic discussions.
  • ⭕ Does greater investment in planning actually cause less supervisory work later, or do developers who already do less corrective work simply also happen to plan more (a confound of skill or task type)?
    The paper's own §8.3 explicitly names testing 'whether greater effort in Plan reduces later correction' as future work, and acknowledges the current findings are a qualitative pattern across interviews and Reddit discussion rather than a controlled or causal test.
  • ⭕ What happens to the seven-stage framework, and to developers' skills, agency, and sense of ownership, as agent autonomy continues to increase beyond what was observed in this study?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe workflow-diagramming method relied on retrospective reconstruction after a single ~40-minute task, and participants were introduced to Sheridan's five-stage framework before drawing their own diagrams -- a sequencing the authors themselves flag (§9) as a possible anchoring effect on how participants segmented their own supervision, even though they were explicitly told not to follow it.
    Read next: A within- or between-subjects comparison of workflow diagrams elicited with versus without prior exposure to Sheridan's framework, to measure the anchoring effect directly rather than only naming it as a limitation.
  • incompleteThe three supervisory strategies (concentrating effort in Plan, distributing work, externalizing into assets) are presented as complementary, but the paper doesn't examine whether they trade off against each other within a single developer's limited time budget -- e.g. whether time spent building and maintaining MISTAKES.md-style assets displaces time that could have gone into planning, or whether developers who delegate review heavily to other agents plan less thoroughly because they expect agent-review to catch gaps.
    Read next: A time-allocation study tracking how individual developers split effort across Plan, asset maintenance, and delegated-review configuration, to test whether the three strategies are additive or substitutive.
  • incompleteThe paper documents that developers retain some Manual Fix work and periodically redo tasks themselves specifically 'to keep the map in my head,' but doesn't examine whether this countermeasure actually holds up over longer timeframes than the ~40 minutes observed in the lab study -- i.e. whether comprehension of agent-produced systems measurably degrades for developers who increasingly shift to Wait and heavy delegation across many parallel tasks over weeks or months.
    Read next: A longitudinal study (as the authors themselves call for in §9) tracking a developer's ability to explain or modify agent-produced code weeks or months after heavy multi-session delegation, to test whether comprehension debt accumulates despite the Manual Fix countermeasure this paper documents.
  • incompleteThe framework documents WHERE developers position themselves across seven stages, but doesn't examine whether that positioning constitutes genuine oversight in the stronger sense this corpus's extraction of 'Human oversight is not human-in-the-loop' describes -- whether developers in Review or Monitor actually have sufficient time, independence, and authority to meaningfully govern the outcome, versus merely occupying a formal checkpoint under increasing parallelism and throughput pressure.
    Read next: Cross the seven-stage framework against the human-oversight-is-not-a-human-in-the-loop conditions (context, competence, time, independence, authority) to test which of the seven stages, under high parallelism, actually retain real governing power versus becoming nominal checkpoints.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • What's an Orchestrator—and Why Does Software Need One? · g4 — This paper's 'Maintaining Understanding of Agent-Produced Work' demand (§7.1.3) supplies exactly the counter-position g4 flags as missing: experienced developers across 19 interviews and 102 Reddit threads describe reading agent reasoning, reviewing diffs, and periodically redoing risky work themselves specifically because understanding-without-reading is not sufficient to stay in control of a system they remain accountable for.
