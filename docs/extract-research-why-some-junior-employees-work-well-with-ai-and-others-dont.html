Research: Why Some Junior Employees Work Well with AI—and Others Don’t

Ashish Agarwal, Anitesh Barua, Anu Puvvada, Fangchen Song, Wen Wen · article · 2026-07 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Amplifier / Delegator / Apprentice TypologyAI as AmplifierAI Labor DisruptionCapacity to Verify AI OutputCognitive SurrenderLearning Culture & SlackSystemic Apprenticeship & Skill Transmission
What separates early-career employees who create value beyond an AI baseline from those who merely match or fall below it is not their foundational skills (critical thinking, domain knowledge, AI literacy) but how they behave while working with AI — orchestrating, interrogating, and refining its output — so organizations should redesign entry-level training and performance assessment around that interaction behavior rather than around screening or upskilling foundational skills alone.

practical · mixed — Reports an empirical field study (theoretical component: a three-way performance typology and its correlates) but the whole piece is organized in service of practical organizational prescriptions — how to redesign entry-level training and assessment. It is HBR's standard 'research summary → so-what for leaders' structure.

Key terms

  • AI baseline
    The quality of output an AI agent produces working alone on a task, established first in the study as a fixed benchmark; all human performance is measured as above, at, or below this line.
  • AI apprentices Amplifier / Delegator / Apprentice Typology
    Early-career employees with foundational skills (critical thinking, domain knowledge, AI literacy) statistically indistinguishable from amplifiers, who nonetheless underperform the AI baseline because they critique AI output without translating that critique into improved results, or focus on irrelevant issues.
  • AI delegators Amplifier / Delegator / Apprentice Typology
    Employees who scored lowest on foundational skills and who match the AI baseline by passively accepting AI output with minimal interrogation, adding little independent reasoning.
  • AI amplifiers Amplifier / Delegator / Apprentice Typology
    Employees who exceed the AI baseline not through stronger underlying skill but by actively orchestrating the workflow — framing problems, anchoring in domain frameworks, defining evaluation criteria, interrogating and iteratively refining AI output, treating AI as a 'thought partner.'
  • Value beyond AI / value beyond the AI baseline
    The marginal human contribution that exceeds what the AI could have produced unassisted — the organizing question the whole study and article are built around.

Propositions

  • explicitFoundational skills — critical thinking, domain knowledge, and AI literacy — did not reliably predict which performance profile an employee fell into; apprentices scored as high as amplifiers on these measures yet still underperformed.conf: high

  • explicitWhat differentiates the three profiles is behavior during AI interaction, not underlying skill: amplifiers orchestrate the workflow, frame problems, set evaluation criteria, and iteratively interrogate/refine AI output; delegators passively accept output with minimal intervention; apprentices critique AI responses without that critique improving the output.conf: high

Arguments

Organizations should redesign entry-level training and performance assessment around AI-interaction behavior (orchestration, interrogation, iterative refinement) rather than around screening or upskilling foundational skills alone. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Foundational skills don't predict which performance profile (apprentice/delegator/amplifier) an employee falls into.
  • The three profiles differ by observable interaction behavior with AI, not by underlying skill level.
  • Deliverable-only assessment cannot distinguish a delegator (passive, at-baseline) from an amplifier (active, above-baseline).
Implicit premises:
  • Interaction behavior with AI is observable and coachable — legible enough to be trained and assessed, not a fixed trait.
  • The behaviors that make amplifiers effective can be taught to apprentices and delegators, not just selected for.
  • Results from a single firm (KPMG), in professional-services-style client-engagement tasks, generalize to other organizations, industries, and knowledge-work domains.
Inferences:
  • If interaction behavior, not credentials, drives value creation, hiring/screening criteria for entry-level roles may need to shift from testing foundational skills toward simulated AI-collaboration assessments.
  • The same three-way split likely exists in other AI-augmented professions (e.g., software engineering), where an adjacent literature on 'posture toward AI' (offloading vs. surrender) already describes a similar behavioral fork.

Narratives

  • In the documents-and-recommendations task, apprentices "spent most of the conversation requesting, rewriting, or reorganizing information, or pursuing less relevant questions" rather than using AI to test their own thinking.
    → Illustrates the apprentice paradox — capable people who fail to translate critique into improved output.
  • Amplifiers, in the same task, "treated AI as a thought partner, asking it to test assumptions, explain its reasoning, consider alternative explanations, and identify missing evidence before refining the final recommendation."
    → Gives concrete behavioral content to the abstract claim that orchestration/interrogation drives outperformance.
  • Delegators "provided the relevant materials and desired deliverable, then largely accepted the AI's output with minimal intervention."
    → Illustrates passive acceptance and grounds the claim that delegator underperformance is invisible when only the deliverable is judged.
  • KPMG's own internal training program — skill-check baseline, personalized learning pathways combining courseware/podcasts/on-the-job activities/an AI champions network, and its National Intern Training at KPMG Lakehouse evolving toward scenario-based exercises for Audit interns.
    → Functions as a proof-of-concept case study for the article's prescriptions, showing them as already-in-motion practice rather than purely theoretical recommendations.
  • Quote from Alison Levin (NBC Universal): "The best AI strategy in the world won't matter if we stop producing people who can actually lead this industry forward."
    → Rhetorical device underscoring the stakes of the disappearing on-ramp premise, borrowed from outside the KPMG study itself.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What enables early-career employees to add value beyond an AI baseline in AI-enabled workflows?
    Interaction behavior with AI — orchestrating the workflow, framing problems, setting evaluation criteria, interrogating and iteratively refining output — not the level of foundational skill.
  • ✅ How can those value-creating capabilities be intentionally developed?
    Contextual, task-based, continuous training embedded in real workflows; making senior judgment visible and narratable; workflow-based (not deliverable-only) assessment. Presented via KPMG's in-progress program, with results described as preliminary ("early results suggest").
  • ⭕ Does training or intervention actually move an employee from one profile to another (e.g., delegator to amplifier), and if so, how long does that take?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article asserts that behavior-focused, workflow-embedded training builds 'amplifier' behaviors, citing KPMG's own program as a test case, but reports no before/after outcome data, control group, or effect size — only "early results suggest." The load-bearing premise underneath every prescription (that these behaviors are teachable and that training measurably shifts people between profiles) is asserted, not demonstrated.
    Read next: Longitudinal or intervention studies testing whether AI-collaboration training changes an individual's performance-profile classification over time; a follow-up KPMG/UT Austin publication with pre/post data.
  • incompleteThe study never connects its behavioral typology to the existing literature on posture toward AI tools (offloading vs. surrender) — a delegator's 'minimal intervention, high acceptance' pattern and an apprentice's 'critique that doesn't improve output' pattern look like specific instances of a more general posture distinction (thinking with vs. instead of thinking) documented elsewhere, but the article treats its typology as sui generis rather than as an instance of that broader mechanism.
    Read next: Corpus material on cognitive offloading vs. cognitive surrender, and on why AI's declarative tone and surface-correctness make passive acceptance the low-effort default.
  • incompleteThe 24.1/25.8/50.1 split and the amplifier/delegator/apprentice behaviors are derived from a single company (KPMG) on client-engagement-style professional-services tasks; the article never addresses whether these proportions or behaviors would replicate in other industries, task types (e.g., software engineering, where an adjacent 'AI as amplifier' literature already exists), or more senior career stages.
    Read next: Cross-industry or cross-domain replications of the typology, especially in software engineering where similar amplifier/dysfunction-multiplier framings and junior-pipeline concerns already circulate.

Closes gaps elsewhere

  • Vibe coding and agentic engineering are getting closer than I'd like · g4 — Willison's essay raised the apprenticeship problem (if AI absorbs the tasks juniors learn on, how do they develop the expertise that makes AI an amplifier?) without addressing it.
