A technically well-performing API is close to worthless if it isn't coherent with the organization's goals, ownership, and structure — and because organizations and their APIs change constantly, that coherence has to be continuously mapped and re-established rather than achieved once and assumed to hold.

practical · mixed — An interview/preview piece (Nordic APIs staff writer with Gareth Faull of London Stock Exchange Group, ahead of his Summit talk) that introduces a theoretical framework (the spacetime-crystal analogy for organizational coherence) in service of a practical prescription: map your organization and APIs together, continually, rather than optimizing API performance in isolation. The exposition (physics lesson, analogy mapping) sets up an argumentative through-line toward actionable measurement advice.

Key terms

  • Coherence Organizational Coherence
    The property of an API estate and the organization above it holding consistent decisions about ownership, capabilities, standards, and business objectives, such that the structure does not fracture as the organization changes — distinct from, and prior to, individual API performance.
  • Spacetime crystal Organizational Coherence
    A physics analogy (a phase of matter with a repeating pattern at regular time intervals, like a self-sustaining clock) used to describe an organizational/API structure whose underlying logic repeats/holds consistently across time (i.e., survives organizational change) rather than only across space (i.e., being internally tidy at a single moment).
  • Business gardening Organizational Coherence
    The ongoing practice of tending an organization: continually mapping how goals connect to work, which long-lived products exist, and how they relate to each other — explicitly not a one-time exercise.
  • Mapping
    Working out why things are the way they are: how goals connect to work, what long-lived products an organization has to steward, and how they relate to each other — the diagnostic precondition for improving coherence.
  • Coherence ceiling Organizational Coherence
    The limit an incoherent higher-level organizational structure imposes on a lower-level, internally-coherent product or team — however coherent a single product is made, it cannot 'fix' the incoherence of the organizational goals above it.
  • Product tree
    A structure Faull proposes as a starting measurement instrument: it maps an API's goals, jobs, and work items, and is then extended upward to map the organizational goals above it, yielding 'a first pass at a coherence measurement.'

Arguments

Organizations should treat coherence between the API estate and organizational intent — not just individual API quality — as the primary target of API governance work.
Explicit premises:
  • A technically 'good' API (proper observability, governance, uptime) can still fail to serve the organization if it's misaligned with organizational goals.
  • Symptoms typically blamed on poor API management — sprawl, duplicated APIs, overlapping capabilities, competing standards, unclear ownership — are actually coherence failures, not performance failures.
Implicit premises:
  • Organizational value, not technical excellence, is the correct measure of success for an API estate.
  • Technical health metrics (uptime, latency, adoption numbers) are necessary but not sufficient proxies for whether an API estate is serving the organization.
  • There is a meaningful, in-principle-measurable distinction between local (product-level) coherence and global (organization-level) coherence.
Inferences:
  • A governance program that only tracks observability/performance KPIs will systematically miss coherence failures and can overstate the health of an API program.
Coherence must be continuously re-mapped and re-measured rather than assessed once ('business gardening'). strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Organizations and APIs change constantly — new products, acquisitions, new technologies, changing standards, changing consumer needs (e.g. agentic traffic emerging as a distinct consumer type within a few years).
  • A map or measurement taken once becomes stale as the organization evolves.
Implicit premises:
  • The rate of organizational and technological change — accelerated by the rise of AI-based decision-making — now outpaces what periodic, one-off audits can track.
  • Treating coherence as a static, one-time-achieved property is a category error given the spacetime-crystal framing itself: a crystal's order exists precisely because of a repeating temporal pattern, not despite the passage of time.
Inferences:
  • Coherence-measurement tooling (such as the open-source framework Faull hopes to release) needs to be built for continuous, recurring use rather than as a one-time assessment checklist.
AI-based decision-making raises the stakes of organizational/API incoherence rather than reducing the need for coherence work.
Explicit premises:
  • Faull's SSRN research finds AI decision systems exhibit growing incoherence over time (conflicting decisions, lost rationales, drifting intent), and characterizes these failures as structural rather than algorithmic.
  • The more an AI system ingests incoherence, the more it risks reinforcing it.
Implicit premises:
  • AI systems inherit and act on the same underlying organizational structures and data as humans do, so they cannot route around organizational incoherence.
  • Because AI can act at higher speed, scale, and autonomy than humans, an incoherence-reinforcing feedback loop compounds faster with AI in the loop than with humans alone.
Inferences:
  • Any organization deploying AI decision-making on top of an incoherent API/data estate is likely to accelerate, not smooth over, its existing coherence problems — making coherence work a precondition for AI adoption rather than a parallel, independent track.

Narratives

  • Two APIs provide identical functionality; from an infrastructure view the obvious move is to sunset one, but zooming out reveals one is tuned for growing agentic traffic (different rate limits) and the other for 'normal' system traffic.
    → Illustrates that apparent incoherence/redundancy can actually be a coherent, deliberate response to organizational context once you look holistically — and cautions against naive infrastructure-only consolidation.
  • Different authentication (AuthN) methods persisting across an API estate as a diagnostic tell that businesses were acquired but never integrated into a coherent whole.
    → Grounds the abstract concept of 'incoherence' in a concrete, spottable technical signal, and supports the proposition that acquisitions are a common cause of incoherence.
  • The spacetime-crystal physics lesson itself (crystalline structures as highly ordered, repeating arrangements; spacetime crystals as matter exhibiting a repeating pattern at regular time intervals, like a self-sustaining clock).
    → Functions as an extended analogy that does the conceptual work of the entire piece before any prescriptive advice is given — it supplies the vocabulary (space/time/structure/disorder) that every later claim in the article is expressed through.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What makes an API estate valuable beyond individual API performance?
    Coherence with organizational goals, ownership, standards, and structure.
  • ✅ What causes API/organizational incoherence?
    Acquisitions, developer/executive silos, and uncoordinated architectural change (e.g. microservices adoption), among others.
  • ⭕ How would you start measuring the coherence of an organization and its APIs?
    Faull offers a provisional method — a 'product tree' mapping an API's goals/jobs/work-items, extended upward to organizational goals — but explicitly calls it only 'a first pass,' and his own open-source measurement framework is not yet released.
  • ✅ Is 100% organizational coherence achievable or even a desirable goal?
    No — Faull states there is no such thing as a 100% coherent organization; what remains genuinely open is what an acceptable or target level of coherence would be.
  • ⭕ How do you decide which incoherencies are worth fixing versus tolerating, given finite organizational bandwidth to 'tend the garden'?
  • ⭕ Who is organizationally responsible for doing 'business gardening' on an ongoing basis?
