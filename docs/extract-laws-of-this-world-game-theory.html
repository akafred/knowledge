The Laws of this World (Game Theory)

Incentivising · article · 2026-06-19 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

The widening gap between effort and reward is not personal deficiency but structural ignorance of five hidden market mechanisms — positional leverage, visibility-as-signal, market indifference to effort, problem selection, and outcome- rather than output-accounting — so working harder cannot close it, but understanding and redirecting toward these mechanisms can.

practical · mixed — A hortatory, second-person self-help essay (opening DM anecdote, direct address, imperative 'you must') that borrows the vocabulary and a handful of citations from labor economics, signaling theory and expertise research to argue for five concrete behavior changes. Structurally it is argumentative-expository (five numbered 'principles', each with a claim plus supporting citation or restatement) wrapped in a narrative frame (the recurring DM, the 'worn-out idealist' refrain).

Key terms

  • Position
    One's location within a leverage-multiplied system (role, platform, network access) that multiplies the return on effort exponentially rather than linearly; distinct from raw effort or competence.
  • Leverage
    A multiplier applied to effort, taxonomized into four types: People (network/contacts), Capital (working money, not savings), Media (content reach), and Code (automation/AI agents).
  • Costly signal Costly Signaling (Signaling Theory)
    A visible, hard-to-fake demonstration of competence (documented results, public claims with sources) that the person bears personal risk/cost for broadcasting, making it credible to a market that 'cannot price what it cannot see.'
  • Output vs. outcome
    Output is what is produced (an email, a meeting, a book read); outcome is what changed in the world because of it (a decision, a changed relationship, a changed behavior). Institutions reward the former because it's measurable; markets reward the latter.
  • Asymmetric investment
    The author's own coinage for the condition of putting in more effort for proportionally less result — presented as the symptom of the broken 'work hard, get rewarded' model, not a personal failing.

Arguments

Readers who feel they work hard without results should stop trying to work harder and instead diagnose and redirect their position, visibility/signaling, problem selection, and output-vs-outcome accounting. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • Position/leverage multiplies effort's return exponentially (Rosen).
  • Visibility functions as a costly signal that markets require to price competence (Spence-derived).
  • Markets reward perceived value delivered, not hours or sacrifice.
  • Effort only builds expertise when directed at the right, feedback-rich problem (Ericsson et al.).
  • Output and outcome are different things, and only outcome is what individuals (unlike institutions) are actually rewarded for.
Implicit premises:
  • These five mechanisms are jointly sufficient to explain the effort-reward gap for essentially any reader, independent of the reader's starting resources, structural constraints, or luck.
  • A reader can unilaterally acquire leverage, visibility, and problem-fit through better individual strategy, without needing pre-existing access to capital, network, or platform.
  • The 'successful individuals' cited as evidence of the model (superstars, institutions) are reliable, non-self-serving narrators of what actually caused their own success — no correction is made for survivorship bias.
Inferences:
  • If position/leverage is itself what multiplies the return on future effort, then acquiring the very first unit of leverage (a first contact, a first bit of capital, a first platform, a first automatable workflow) is the actual bottleneck for someone starting with none — an implication the essay's own model produces but never surfaces or addresses.

Narratives

  • The recurring DM: 'even though I put in a ton of effort, it seems like I never get anywhere,' framed as one instance of a message the author receives constantly.
    → Establishes reader identification and licenses the essay's core reassurance ('you are not deficient') before the five principles are introduced; also functions as the author's own display of a track record (frequent, similar DMs) — itself an instance of the 'costly signal' the essay later names, performed rather than argued for.
  • The 'worn-out idealist' who eventually 'returns to embrace what he previously rebelled against,' repeated as the essay's closing line.
    → Implicitly supports the essay's framing that resisting the five 'laws' is a phase people eventually abandon — used as a rhetorical closer rather than an argued claim.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ Why does working hard no longer reliably produce proportional results?
    Answered via the five principles: position/leverage, visibility/signaling, market indifference to effort, problem selection, and output-vs-outcome accounting.
  • ⭕ How does someone with no existing position, capital, network, or platform acquire the initial leverage the essay's own model treats as the multiplier of everything else?

Gaps

  • illogicalThe title and framing promise 'five game-theoretical mechanisms,' but only Principle 2 (the 'costly signal') is actually game/information theory, and even there the essay cites Spence's job-market-signaling paper without reconstructing the underlying argument — why a signal that is costly enough separates credible (high-quality) senders from bluffers in equilibrium. Principle 1 (Rosen's superstar economics) is labor/increasing-returns economics, Principle 3 is basic price theory (value to the buyer), Principle 4 is educational psychology (deliberate practice), and Principle 5 is elementary cost/benefit accounting. None of the other four involve a strategic interaction between two or more players, an equilibrium concept, or a payoff matrix. The 'game theory' framing functions as intellectual costly-signaling in its own right — borrowed academic citations lending the piece more rigor than its actual analytical content delivers.
    Read next: Read Spence (1973) directly, plus a signaling-games treatment (separating vs. pooling equilibria) or Zahavi's handicap principle, to see the machinery the essay borrows the vocabulary of but doesn't deploy.
  • incompleteThe five principles are reverse-engineered from observing successful 'superstars' and institutions, with no comparison group of people who pursued the same leverage-building, signaling, and problem-selection strategies and still failed. Without that counterfactual, the essay cannot distinguish 'these mechanisms cause success' from 'these mechanisms are what winners retrospectively describe' — a textbook survivorship-bias gap.
    Read next: Michael Mauboussin, The Success Equation, or Nassim Taleb on survivorship bias and hidden failure populations, for how to correct causal claims drawn only from winners.
  • incompleteThe essay converts what its own model implies is partly a structural/distributional problem (unequal starting access to leverage — people, capital, media reach, automatable workflows) into a purely informational/strategic one, solvable by 'knowing the mechanisms.' It never asks how a reader with none of the four leverage types acquires the first unit of any of them, nor engages evidence that visibility and sponsorship access is itself unevenly distributed by factors outside individual signaling choice.
    Read next: extract-60-years-men-women-leadership#p6 — the corpus's own evidence that 'informal sponsorship flows along lines of familiarity and visibility that formal policy does not reach' documents visibility access as structurally uneven, not simply a matter of an individual choosing to signal harder.
