Beyond Zero: Google Publishes Successor to BeyondCorp

Renato Losio · rss · 2026-09-05 source ↗ Reader 🖥️

Agent Governance (Internal)Agent SecurityNon-Deterministic SoftwarePolicy-as-CodePrivilege Drift / Scope SprawlSandboxing
Google's newly published Beyond Zero model proposes extending Zero Trust from static, application-level, human-speed access control to continuous, resource- and action-level authorization combining static policy with dynamic AI-driven decisions for both humans and AI agents — an aspirational architecture that early practitioner and community reaction meets with skepticism about its maturity, complexity, and the wisdom of non-deterministic access controls.

practical · mixed — InfoQ news reporting on a Google research paper: expository summary of the paper's five principles, plus argumentative content carried by quoted sources (Valente/Zalewski's justification, Narraway's and Hacker News's skepticism). Ultimately practical — the underlying claim is about how enterprises should architect access control, not merely what is true about it.

Key terms

  • Beyond Zero
    Google's successor to BeyondCorp: a 'security model for the AI era' that authorizes individual actions on individual resources continuously, using contextual and risk-based controls, for both human and AI-agent accessors.
  • BeyondCorp
    Google's 2014 zero-trust whitepaper/model that replaced network-perimeter security with identity- and context-based verification of human users accessing applications — now framed as the predecessor whose assumptions have expired.
  • resource-level / action-level authorization (vs. application-level authorization)
    Authorization evaluated per individual action on a specific resource or API call, rather than per session-level grant of access to an entire application.
  • continuous authorization
    Access decisions re-evaluated for every action in real time rather than granted once at login or session start and then trusted for the session's duration.
  • machine-speed enforcement
    Authorization decisions and enforcement mechanisms that operate fast enough to keep pace with AI agents acting far faster, and in far greater volume, than human users.
  • non-deterministic access control
    As used by Hacker News commenter 'stogot': authorization decisions made by an AI model, whose outputs are not guaranteed to be identical given identical inputs, as opposed to a fixed, deterministic policy engine.

Arguments

Zero Trust needs a successor model (Beyond Zero) built around AI agents rather than only humans. strength: medium
Explicit premises:
  • BeyondCorp assumed accessors are human, actions occur at human speed, and applications are the correct trust boundary.
  • AI agents violate all three assumptions: they are non-human, act at machine speed, and act across resources/APIs rather than staying within a single application's boundary.
Implicit premises:
  • A security model's validity depends on its founding assumptions matching the actual population and behavior of the accessors it governs.
  • When the accessor population shifts from purely human to a mix of humans and autonomous agents operating at orders-of-magnitude greater scale and speed, the level at which trust is granted must also shift downward, from application to individual action.
Inferences:
  • If authorization stays bounded at the application level while accessors include agents acting at machine speed, an agent's every action within its granted application access goes unchecked — meaning application-level trust silently authorizes far more than any human reviewer intended, once agents are involved.
AI-driven (non-deterministic) authorization decisions are a poor security design choice. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Zero trust access control is expected to be deterministic.
  • AI-driven decision-making is non-deterministic.
Implicit premises:
  • Security-critical decisions must produce the same output from the same input so they can be tested, audited, and reasoned about in advance.
  • A control that can behave unpredictably under identical conditions cannot be fully verified, and unverifiable controls are unacceptable at a security boundary.
Inferences:
  • Any dynamic AI-driven layer inherits the underlying model's error/hallucination rate and applies it directly to access-control outcomes — a qualitatively different risk from a deterministic policy engine's bugs, which are at least reproducible.
Ordinary enterprises should not expect to implement Beyond Zero the way Google can, at least not soon. strength: high
Explicit premises:
  • Google's own Beyond Zero deployments are internal-only.
  • Not all of the architecture's components are built yet.
  • More explanatory papers are still to come, with no stated deadlines.
  • This mirrors the 2014 BeyondCorp playbook of publishing vision before tooling.
Implicit premises:
  • A paper describing an internally-validated architecture at Google's scale does not, by itself, constitute a deployable reference implementation for organizations lacking Google's engineering resources, internal tooling, and multi-year runway.
Inferences:
  • In the near term, Beyond Zero functions more as a research agenda and shared vocabulary for the industry than as an adoptable control — a conclusion the article doesn't state outright but that follows from stacking Narraway's observations against the closing line about undefined timelines.

Narratives

  • Valente's LinkedIn account of Google's internal system scaling from thousands of human workers, to hundreds of thousands, to being 'joined by millions of agents' — with continuous per-action authorization going from 'seemed like overkill' to necessary.
    → Implicitly supports the proposition that Beyond Zero emerged from organic operational necessity driven by scale, not from speculative up-front design — lending the architecture empirical credibility that a pure thought-experiment would lack.
  • The lineage from the 2014 BeyondCorp whitepaper to the 2026 Beyond Zero paper, explicitly cast by the author and by Narraway as 'the same playbook.'
    → Functions as a narrative of institutional continuity that implicitly argues for taking the new model seriously (Google shipped the previous vision and the industry did eventually adopt it) while simultaneously supporting the skeptical reading (it also means years will pass before this is broadly usable).

Questions addressed

  • ✅ How should enterprise access control adapt now that AI agents, not just humans, are requesting access to resources?
    Resolved at the level of a proposed architecture (five principles centered on resource/action-level, continuously authorized, context-aware control) — not yet resolved as a demonstrated, adoptable practice outside Google.
  • ⭕ Are AI-driven, non-deterministic authorization decisions compatible with security's traditional need for auditability and predictability?
    Raised explicitly by Hacker News commenters and implicitly by the auditability/reversibility point, but the article reports the objection without adjudicating it or examining Beyond Zero's actual static/dynamic split in response.
  • ⭕ What has to change — standards, vendor APIs — for organizations beyond Google to adopt fine-grained, resource-level authorization?
    Partially named (SaaS vendors must expose action-level authorization; standards must mature) but not detailed — left as an acknowledged open problem rather than a solved one.

Gaps

  • incompleteThe article never explains the internal mechanics of the 'dynamic AI-driven decisions' component — what kind of model performs the risk evaluation, what its false-positive/false-negative rate is, or how it would interact with existing IAM/policy-as-code engines (OPA, Cedar) already used for agent governance elsewhere in the corpus.
    Read next: Google's forthcoming implementation papers, and a direct comparison against policy-as-code frameworks (OPA/Cedar) already covered in the corpus's agent-governance material (e.g. the AWS Loom and privilege-drift extractions).
  • incompleteNo adoption data or case study exists yet: Google states deployments are internal-only, and the 'community reaction' section is limited to a single Hacker News thread days after publication — there's no account of what a non-Google organization with materially fewer resources would actually need to build to get even a partial version working.
    Read next: Track follow-up InfoQ or industry coverage as Google publishes the promised additional papers, and read Kane Narraway's own 'Beyond Zero: For The Rest Of Us' post directly for a practitioner-scale adaptation.
  • uninformedThe article doesn't engage at all with how Beyond Zero's continuous, per-action authorization model would interact with, replace, or duplicate the just-in-time token issuance and platform-wide permission-inventory approaches already covered elsewhere in the corpus's privilege-drift and agent-governance material, even though both are solving the overlapping problem of bounding what an agent can do at any given moment.
    Read next: Read the privilege-drift-in-multi-agent-systems and AWS Loom governance extractions side by side with Beyond Zero's five principles to map where the mechanisms converge or diverge.
  • illogicalThe Hacker News syllogism ('Zero trust is deterministic. AI is non-deterministic. Non-deterministic access controls are a terrible idea.') is reported but never examined against Beyond Zero's actual hybrid design — static policies handle baseline enforcement, and AI-driven decisions are reserved for higher-risk scenarios per the paper's second principle — so the article lets an objection to a stronger, purer 'AI decides everything' claim stand unchallenged even though it may not be the claim Google is making.
    Read next: A close technical reading of the original ACM paper to establish exactly which authorization decisions are delegated to the AI-driven layer versus kept as static, deterministic policy.
