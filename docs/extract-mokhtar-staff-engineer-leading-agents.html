The Staff Engineer skillset of defining outcomes, reducing ambiguity, creating guardrails, and reviewing work — originally built for leading human teams — transfers directly to leading AI coding agents, and is becoming more valuable, not less, as agents take over more implementation.

practical · argumentative — A first-person career-advice essay: the author argues engineers should adopt a specific stance (lead agents like a team) and defends it with a personal case study, not a theoretical account of AI capability.

Key terms

  • Leading agents
    Treating AI coding agents as team members to be assigned roles, given context, reviewed, and corrected — the same posture as leading human reports — rather than as an autocomplete tool.
  • Enabler / multiplier
    A leadership identity in which value comes from creating opportunities and clarity for others (human or agent) to do the work, rather than doing the work oneself.
  • Direction
    The combination of defining outcomes, reducing ambiguity, setting boundaries, and deciding when output is good enough — explicitly distinguished from 'prompting,' which the author treats as too narrow a description of the skill.

Arguments

The Staff Engineer skillset is becoming more valuable, not less, in the AI era.
Explicit premises:
  • Leading AI agents well requires the same skills as leading human teams: defining outcomes, reducing ambiguity, creating guardrails, reviewing work
  • Agents, like people given a bare ticket, produce worse output when given vague direction and fill the gaps with assumptions
  • Implementation is becoming automatable while direction, review, and business-context work are not
Implicit premises:
  • Organizations will continue to need someone accountable for distinguishing 'impressive-looking code' from useful, maintainable software even as implementation volume grows
  • An individual engineer's personally-acquired human-leadership skill transfers cleanly to agent-leadership without additional training or organizational support
Inferences:
  • If direction is the scarce skill, engineering organizations should weight hiring and promotion criteria toward judgment and context-gathering over raw implementation speed — a conclusion the author gestures at for individuals but never states as an organizational policy

Narratives

  • The author built six role-specific custom GitHub Copilot agents (Lead Engineer, Implementer, Reviewer, QA, Security, Technical Writer) plus an orchestrator agent, and dispatched a one-line instruction ('Build endpoint for user management') to the whole team — producing a result that left him both 'shocked' and unsure whether to feel proud or threatened.
    → Makes concrete the abstract claim that agents should be led 'like a team' rather than used as autocomplete, and dramatizes the ambivalence (opportunity vs. job-loss fear) that recurs through the piece.

Questions addressed

  • ✅ What should a newly-titled Staff Engineer actually do, given vague and inconsistent role expectations?
    He defined the role himself through direct conversations with teammates ('How can I help you?') and an external course, landing on an enabler/multiplier self-definition.
  • ✅ Does AI's growing coding capability threaten the Staff Engineer's (or any engineer's) job?
    No — because the enabler/multiplier skillset built for leading humans is exactly what's needed to lead agents, so the role's value increases rather than disappears.
  • ⭕ How does an engineering organization, rather than one self-motivated individual, scale this leadership shift across its whole workforce and keep quality consistent when many engineers are each directing their own agent teams?

Gaps

  • incompleteThe piece is written entirely from the perspective of one self-motivated individual's personal transition; it never addresses the team or organizational dimension — how review capacity, coordination, and quality standards hold up when many engineers in the same org are each independently 'leading' their own agent swarms, or how an organization trains and rewards this shift systematically rather than relying on individuals to discover it the way the author did.

  • incompleteNo discussion of durable, large-scale, or legacy codebases; every example (CLI tool, personal dashboard, browser extension, a user-management endpoint) is small, disposable, or greenfield. The claim that the Staff Engineer skillset transfers to leading agents is untested against the harder case of agents operating inside a decade-old, high-stakes production system.
    Read next: extract-orosz-when-ai-writes-almost-all-code (production-quality regressions already observed) and extract-whats-an-orchestrator-why-software-needs-one (accountability scaling with agent-driven productivity).
  • illogicalThe author frames 'reviewing becomes more important' as sufficient reassurance without reconciling it with his own admission that agents 'make mistakes... sometimes create bugs that look very convincing.' If agent output is specifically liable to look convincing while being wrong, review itself becomes harder exactly as it becomes more necessary — a tension the piece raises but doesn't resolve.
